Net Metering vs. Net Billing: How Export Credits Change Payback

Payback at full, half, quarter and zero export credit in 13 states, the export rate each needs for a 15-year payback, and the rules new customers get.

Quick verdict

Net metering credits each kWh your panels send to the grid at the retail price. Net billing credits it at a lower rate set by the utility or regulator. The difference matters most where power is cheap: in our model, a home in Hawaii pays back within 15 years even with no export credit at all, while one in Texas needs exports credited at about 91% of retail to get there.

The table below runs the same house through full, half, quarter and zero credit in 13 states, then shows the export rate each state needs for a 15-year payback. For five of those states we also list what a new customer actually gets today.

The two systems side by side

Net metering (1:1) Net billing
Value of an exported kWh The retail price A lower rate, often tied to the utility's avoided cost
Power you use as it's produced Saves the retail price Saves the retail price
Power you buy from the grid Retail price Retail price
What raises your savings Producing close to a year of your use Using more of your output as it's made

Avoided cost is roughly what the utility would have spent to produce or buy that power itself. A true-up is the yearly settlement of leftover credits.

How the comparison works

Only the value of an exported kWh changes. Everything else is our fixed example home: 7 kW at $3,500 per kW, 10,500 kWh a year with 40% used while the sun is up, PVWatts output for one reference city per state, EIA average residential prices for September 2026, and credits capped at a year of grid purchases. "Credit needed" is the export credit, as a share of the retail price and in cents, at which payback reaches 15 years.

The "none" column is the payback from self-use alone. It is a useful floor: it tells you what the system is worth if exports earned nothing.

What the table shows

The export credit matters in proportion to how much you export. In this house, 64% of a Los Angeles system's output and 66% of a Phoenix system's goes to the grid, against 49% in Seattle. Sunny reference cities make more midday surplus, so they lean harder on the credit.

Expensive power tolerates a weak credit. California reaches a 15-year payback with exports worth just 2.2¢, and Massachusetts with 3.3¢. A low export rate lengthens payback there, but it rarely decides whether solar works.

Cheap power does not. Florida and Texas need exports credited at about 91% of retail to reach 15 years. Arizona needs 72%, slightly less than Illinois despite cheaper power, because a Phoenix system makes and exports more, so each point of credit is worth more. In Nevada, Louisiana and Washington, the example misses 15 years even at full credit.

A cent of export credit is worth different amounts in different places. In Colorado and Arizona, 10.7 to 10.8¢ per exported kWh gets the house to 15 years; in Texas it takes 14.5¢. Compare an offered export rate with your own retail price before comparing it with another state.

Washington shows that full credit can still leave a long payback when power is cheap and the sky is cloudy: 19.2 years. California shows the opposite: a low credit on top of a high price still pays back in about 12 years.

Getting more out of net billing

Under net billing, every kWh you use as it is produced is worth the full retail price, and every kWh you export is worth less. Three ways to shift the balance:

  • Size for use, not for the roof. A smaller system exports less of its output. Can a solar system be too big? shows where extra panels stop paying.
  • Move flexible loads into daylight. Laundry, dishwashers, water heating and EV charging. Solar and daytime electricity use puts numbers on the effect.
  • Price a battery separately. Storage can turn midday exports into evening self-use, at extra cost; the battery payback calculator tests it against your export rate.

Ask how long your utility keeps a new customer on the terms in force when you connect. California and Hawaii both fix them for a set period, nine and seven years respectively.

Run your own case

Find your retail price and the export rate your utility or plan pays, divide one by the other, and enter that share in the solar calculator. If your export rate is below the "credit needed" figure for your price level, look first at a smaller system and more daytime use.
