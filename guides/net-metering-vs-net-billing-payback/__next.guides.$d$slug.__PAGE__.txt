21:T109d,<div class="short-answer"><p class="short-answer-label">Quick verdict</p>
<p>Net metering credits each kWh your panels send to the grid at the retail price. Net billing credits it at a lower rate set by the utility or regulator. The difference matters most where power is cheap: in our model, a home in Hawaii pays back within 15 years even with no export credit at all, while one in Texas needs exports credited at about 91% of retail to get there.</p>
<p>The table below runs the same house through full, half, quarter and zero credit in 13 states, then shows the export rate each state needs for a 15-year payback. For five of those states we also list what a new customer actually gets today.</p>

33:T1649,<h2 id="what-the-table-shows">What the table shows</h2>
<p><strong>The export credit matters in proportion to how much you export.</strong> In this house, 64% of a Los Angeles system&#39;s output and 66% of a Phoenix system&#39;s goes to the grid, against 49% in Seattle. Sunny reference cities make more midday surplus, so they lean harder on the credit.</p>
<p><strong>Expensive power tolerates a weak credit.</strong> California reaches a 15-year payback with exports worth just 2.2¢, and Massachusetts with 3.3¢. A low export rate lengthens payback there, but it rarely decides whether solar works.</p>
<p><strong>Cheap power does not.</strong> Florida and Texas need exports credited at about 91% of retail to reach 15 years. Arizona needs 72%, slightly less than Illinois despite cheaper power, because a Phoenix system makes and exports more, so each point of credit is worth more. In Nevada, Louisiana and Washington, the example misses 15 years even at full credit.</p>
<p><strong>A cent of export credit is worth different amounts in different places.</strong> In Colorado and Arizona, 10.7 to 10.8¢ per exported kWh gets the house to 15 years; in Texas it takes 14.5¢. Compare an offered export rate with your own retail price before comparing it with another state.</p>

<p>Washington shows that full credit can still leave a long payback when power is cheap and the sky is cloudy: 19.2 years. California shows the opposite: a low credit on top of a high price still pays back in about 12 years.</p>
<h2 id="getting-more-out-of-net-billing">Getting more out of net billing</h2>
<p>Under net billing, every kWh you use as it is produced is worth the full retail price, and every kWh you export is worth less. Three ways to shift the balance:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Size for use, not for the roof.</strong> A smaller system exports less of its output. <a href="/guides/oversized-solar-system-annual-true-up/">Can a solar system be too big?</a> shows where extra panels stop paying.</li>
<li><strong>Move flexible loads into daylight.</strong> Laundry, dishwashers, water heating and EV charging. <a href="/guides/solar-daytime-electricity-use/">Solar and daytime electricity use</a> puts numbers on the effect.</li>
<li><strong>Price a battery separately.</strong> Storage can turn midday exports into evening self-use, at extra cost; the <a href="/battery-payback-calculator/">battery payback calculator</a> tests it against your export rate.</li>
</ul>
<p>Ask how long your utility keeps a new customer on the terms in force when you connect. California and Hawaii both fix them for a set period, nine and seven years respectively.</p>
<h2 id="run-your-own-case">Run your own case</h2>
<p>Find your retail price and the export rate your utility or plan pays, divide one by the other, and enter that share in the <a href="/solar-calculator/">solar calculator</a>. If your export rate is below the &quot;credit needed&quot; figure for your price level, look first at a smaller system and more daytime use.</p>
