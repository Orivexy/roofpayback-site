38:Ta8f,<div class="short-answer"><h2 id="quick-verdict">Quick verdict</h2>
<p>Net metering gives the shorter payback in every state in the table below. Net billing does the least damage where power is expensive.</p>
<p>The credit for power you send to the grid can move payback as much as sunshine does. In our example home, Texas pays back in 14.3 years with full net metering, 19.2 years when exports earn half the retail rate, and 23.1 years at a quarter of retail. California moves from 7.4 to 9.8 to 12.5 years.</p>
<p>Your electricity price sets the baseline for payback. The export rule comes right after it, and where exports earn only a fraction of retail it can cancel much of what a good price would deliver. Pin down both before you compare a single quote.</p>
</div><h2 id="the-two-systems-side-by-side">The two systems side by side</h2>
<p>Panels make the most power around midday, when many homes use the least. The surplus flows to the grid. Your utility then credits it under one of two broad approaches.</p>

<h2 id="when-net-metering-wins">When net metering wins</h2>
<p>Full 1:1 credit beats net billing in every row of the table. The margin is what changes.</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Low-price states feel it most.</strong> In Louisiana, dropping from full to quarter credit pushes payback from 16.7 years to beyond our 25-year horizon. At 12.87¢ per kWh, losing three quarters of the export value strips out most of what those kWh were worth, and the remaining savings arrive slowly. The guide to <a href="/guides/is-solar-worth-it-where-electricity-is-cheap/">solar where electricity is cheap</a> looks at that group in detail.</li>
<li><strong>Homes that export a lot gain the most.</strong> If much of your output goes to the grid during the day, a full credit treats that power as if you had used it yourself.</li>
<li><strong>Sizing close to a full year of use fits best here.</strong> That approach relies on exported credits holding their full value. The model caps credits at a year of grid purchases, so production beyond that earns nothing extra.</li>
</ul>
<h2 id="when-net-billing-hurts-less">When net billing hurts less</h2>
<p>Net billing never comes out ahead in this comparison, but there are cases where it does far less damage.</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>High-price states keep self-used power valuable.</strong> In Hawaii, every kWh you use directly still saves 47.18¢. That is why moving to quarter credit only takes payback from 4.8 years to 8.3 years there.</li>
<li><strong>Homes with heavy daytime use export less.</strong> The more of your own power you consume as it&#39;s made, the less the export rate matters.</li>
<li><strong>Right-sized systems limit exposure.</strong> A system sized for daytime use sends less to the grid at the low rate.</li>
</ul>
<p>In our data, only Hawaii stays under ten years with a quarter-retail credit. In most states the payback reaches 20 years or more at that rate, or does not arrive within 25 years.</p>
<h2 id="where-they-tie">Where they tie</h2>
<p>Some things don&#39;t change between the two systems:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>The price of power you buy.</strong> Both charge full retail for kWh drawn from the grid.</li>
<li><strong>The value of power you use as it&#39;s produced.</strong> A kWh your house uses at midday saves the full retail price either way. Export rules only touch the surplus.</li>
<li><strong>The need to read the tariff.</strong> Neither label tells you the exact terms. Your utility&#39;s tariff page and DSIRE do.</li>
</ul>
<h2 id="getting-the-most-out-of-net-billing">Getting the most out of net billing</h2>
<p>If your utility pays less than retail for exports, the goal shifts. Instead of making as much power as possible, aim to use as much of it as possible yourself.</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Size for daytime use</strong>, not for 100% of your annual bill. <a href="/guides/how-many-solar-panels-do-i-need/">How many solar panels you need</a> walks through sizing.</li>
<li><strong>Shift flexible loads to midday:</strong> laundry, dishwasher, water heating, EV charging.</li>
<li><strong>Ask installers to model your actual tariff.</strong> A quote that assumes full net metering in a net-billing area overstates savings.</li>
<li><strong>Check how long current rules are locked in.</strong> Some programs keep existing customers on their original terms for a set period. Your utility can confirm.</li>
</ul>
<p>Batteries can also raise self-use. They add cost, though, and whether they pay off is a separate calculation based on your rates.</p>
<h2 id="which-one-fits-you">Which one fits you</h2>
<p>You rarely get to choose. Your utility decides. What you can choose is how to plan around it.</p>
<p><strong>If your utility offers full net metering:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>Expect the shortest payback for your state&#39;s price level.</li>
<li>Sizing close to your annual use is reasonable.</li>
<li>Confirm how long the terms last for new customers.</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>If your utility uses net billing:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>Find the export rate and run the half or quarter scenario that comes closest.</li>
<li>Size for daytime use and shift loads to midday.</li>
<li>In a high-price state, solar can still pay back in a reasonable time.</li>
<li>In a low-price state, the export rule may decide whether the system pays back at all.</li>
</ul>
<p>Full net metering is the best case, and not every home gets it. Ask what your utility pays for exported power, then run the matching scenario in the <a href="/solar-calculator/">calculator</a>. For the wider picture since the federal credit ended, read <a href="/guides/is-solar-worth-it-without-tax-credit/">is solar still worth it without the tax credit</a>.</p>
