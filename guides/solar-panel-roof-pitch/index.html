Does Roof Pitch Matter for Solar? 20° vs. 30° Compared

Roof pitch changes solar output by only a few percent. See how 20° and 30° compare on south, east, and west roofs, and what it means for payback.

Published October 18, 2026·Updated November 15, 2026·5 min read·RoofPayback editorial·Figures checked against EIA and PVWatts data

The verdict

Roof pitch matters far less than the direction your roof faces. Tilting a south-facing array from a 20° pitch to a 30° pitch changes yearly output by 2.4% on average across states. Turning that same array to face east or west instead of south costs roughly 14.5% to 14.8% at a 20° pitch.

So a roof that is a bit steeper or flatter than ideal is rarely a reason to skip solar. A roof that faces the wrong way is the bigger problem.

Pitch simply means how steep the roof is. Solar companies usually give it as an angle in degrees. A flat roof is near 0°, and a steep roof is far higher.

How we tested it

Every figure here comes from PVWatts v8. The base case is a south-facing array at a 20° pitch with 14% losses, modeled in one reference city per state. Each other case is measured against that base. We report the average across states plus the extremes we found. Details are on our methodology page.

Myth: Solar only works if your roof has the perfect angle

Fact: A small miss on pitch costs very little.

Panels produce the most when sunlight hits them straight on. In the U.S., the sun sits in the southern half of the sky, so a panel tilted toward the south catches more of it over a year. The ideal tilt shifts with latitude, but the effect is gentle.

The test shows how gentle. On a south-facing roof, going from 20° to 30° moved output by 2.4% on average. Across states, the change ran from -1.7% in Hawaii to 4.7% in Alaska. Neither end is large enough to make or break a project on its own.

Myth: Steeper panels always produce more

Fact: Steeper helps in some places, hurts in others, and makes east- and west-facing roofs worse.

Latitude explains the first part. At high latitudes like Alaska, the sun stays low in the sky, so a steeper panel faces it better. Near the equator, as in Hawaii, the sun rides higher, and a flatter panel does better. That is why the 30° south roof gained the most in Alaska and slightly lost output in Hawaii.

Direction explains the second part. On east and west faces, more pitch cuts output further:

Direction At 20° (avg.) At 30° (avg.) Range at 30°
East -14.5% -17.2% -11.1% (Hawaii) to -23.3% (Alaska)
West -14.8% -17.7% -11.6% (Hawaii) to -21.4% (Montana)

A steep east or west panel points more toward the horizon and less toward the high midday sun. Steep roofs facing east or west lost the most of anything tested.

Myth: As long as the roof doesn't face north, direction barely matters

Fact: Direction is the biggest roof factor in the test, by a wide margin.

All of these are at a 20° pitch, compared with south at 20°:

Direction (20°) Average Mildest loss Steepest loss
Southeast -4.1% -1.8% (Florida) -7.2% (Alaska)
Southwest -4.3% -2.2% (Hawaii) -5.9% (Montana)
East -14.5% -7.2% (Hawaii) -20.7% (Alaska)
West -14.8% -7.6% (Hawaii) -18.7% (Montana)
East and west split -14.7% -7.4% (Hawaii) -18% (Alaska)

Southeast and southwest roofs give up only a few percent. Full east, full west or a split east-west layout give up much more. Set beside the 2.4% pitch effect, direction clearly moves output more than the pitch change tested.

Notice too that Hawaii shows the mildest losses for most directions. Alaska and Montana tend to show the steepest.

Myth: An east-west roof means solar is off the table

Fact: It produces less, but that alone does not settle the question.

East and west roofs lose real output, roughly 14.5% to 14.8% at 20°. Whether the system still pays off turns on your power price, your utility's credit rules and the system cost. Our guide to east-west facing solar panels covers when that layout can still make sense.

The payback logic is simple. Less output means less electricity from the same hardware. The cost stays about the same, so payback takes longer. Our example home, a 7 kW system with no incentives described on the methodology page, shows the pattern: a roof that loses 14.5% (east at 20°) pays back slower than one that loses 4.1% (southeast at 20°). For your own state, enter your roof in the solar calculator, and see solar payback period explained for background.

Your price per kilowatt-hour changes the picture as well. When it costs more, every kWh your panels make is worth more, so a few percent of lost output costs you more dollars. The electricity prices by state page uses EIA's August 2026 average residential data.

Myth: Pitch is the main thing to ask an installer about

Fact: Several other factors can matter as much or more.

  • Shade. Trees and chimneys can cost more than any pitch change.
  • Roof space. A steeper roof may fit fewer or more panels, depending on layout. See how many solar panels you need.
  • Export rules. Net metering and solar incentive programs vary by state and utility. DSIRE keeps a database of current programs at programs.dsireusa.org. Also read net metering vs. net billing.
  • Mounting. On a flat roof, installers can sometimes tilt the panels. On a pitched roof, panels usually follow the roof line.

Better questions for a quote:

  1. What output do you expect for my exact roof direction and pitch?
  2. Which tool did you use, and what loss assumption?
  3. Are there roof sections that face a better direction?
  4. How does my utility credit exported power?

Estimates differ by company, so compare more than one quote. You can also model your own roof in PVWatts.

What actually decides it

Direction first. Southeast and southwest roofs lose 4.1% and 4.3% on average against south, while east and west lose 14.5% and 14.8%. Pitch comes well behind: a 30° south roof beats 20° by 2.4% on average.

After that, shade, usable roof space, your utility's export rules and your price per kWh can matter as much as pitch. A pitch that is a little off should not rule solar out. Check those factors, then run your roof through the solar calculator.
