Does Roof Pitch Matter for Solar? 20° vs. 30° Compared

A 30° south roof makes 0.5% to 4.7% more than a 20° one in 50 states, and Hawaii loses. State-by-state table, x/12 pitch conversion and payback effect.

Published October 18, 2026·Updated November 28, 2026·5 min read·RoofPayback editorial·Figures checked against EIA and PVWatts data

Official data: EIA · DOE · IRS · how we check

The verdict

Pitch matters, but only by a few percent within the range most homes have. In PVWatts, tilting a south-facing array from 20° to 30° raised yearly output in 50 of our 51 reference cities, by 0.5% in Houston up to 4.7% in Anchorage, and cut it by 1.7% in Honolulu. The average gain was 2.4%.

On east- and west-facing roofs the same extra pitch works against you, costing 1% to 5% more output depending on the state. Direction is the larger effect: turning a roof from south to east or west costs about 14.5% to 14.8% at 20°, as east- or west-facing solar panels shows.

The U.S. Department of Energy says panels "perform best on south-facing roofs with a slope between 15 and 40 degrees," and that other roofs may be suitable too.

Convert your roof pitch to degrees

Roofers in the U.S. describe pitch as rise over a 12-inch run, while solar tools use degrees. The conversion is the angle whose tangent is rise ÷ 12:

Roof pitch Angle
3/12 14°
4/12 18.4°
5/12 22.6°
6/12 26.6°
7/12 30.3°
8/12 33.7°
9/12 36.9°
10/12 39.8°
12/12 45°

So our 20° baseline sits between a 4/12 and a 5/12 roof, and 30° is almost exactly a 7/12 roof.

The order follows latitude closely. The farther north the city, the lower the sun sits for much of the year and the more a steeper south-facing panel gains. Near the Gulf Coast the gain is under 1%, and in Honolulu, at about 21° north, the flatter panel wins.

What it does to payback

Very little, because a few percent of output is a few percent of savings. Running our example home (7 kW, 10,500 kWh a year, $3,500 per kW, no incentives, September 2026 EIA prices, full export credit) at both pitches:

  • Alaska, south roof: 12.7 years at 20°, 12.2 years at 30°. This is the largest improvement in the table.
  • Minnesota, south roof: 14 years at 20°, 13.5 years at 30°.
  • Florida, south roof: 14.3 years at both pitches.
  • Alaska, east roof: 15.7 years at 20°, 16.2 years at 30°. A steep east or west face pushes the other way.

In practical terms, a steep south roof in the north is a small bonus and a steep east or west roof a small extra cost. Neither turns a good project into a bad one. Your electricity price and how exports are credited move payback far more; see net metering vs. net billing.

What we did not test

Our PVWatts data covers only 20° and 30°. We did not model low pitches under 20°, roofs steeper than 30°, or flat roofs where racks set the tilt, so the table says nothing about them. DOE's design guidance gives the general rule: "To obtain the highest annual energy output, modules in the northern hemisphere are pointed due south and inclined at an angle equal to the local latitude." Real roofs rarely match that exactly, which is why the differences above stay small. PVWatts lets you enter your own tilt and direction for your address, and an installer's estimate should use your measured pitch.

What to ask an installer

  1. What tilt and direction does your estimate use, and was it measured on my roof?
  2. Which roof faces did you consider, and why did you pick these?
  3. On a flat or low-slope roof, will the panels be tilted on racks, and does that change how many fit?
  4. What does shade from trees, chimneys or dormers cost on each face?

Then compare quotes on expected yearly kWh and price per watt, and run the numbers in the solar calculator. For month-by-month output at the 20° baseline, see how much electricity a solar panel produces.
