22:T6ed,<div class="short-answer"><h2 id="the-verdict">The verdict</h2>
<p>Roof pitch matters far less than the direction your roof faces. Tilting a south-facing array from a 20° pitch to a 30° pitch changes yearly output by 2.4% on average across states. Turning that same array to face east or west instead of south costs roughly 14.5% to 14.8% at a 20° pitch.</p>
<p>So a roof that is a bit steeper or flatter than ideal is rarely a reason to skip solar. A roof that faces the wrong way is the bigger problem.</p>
<p>Pitch simply means how steep the roof is. Solar companies usually give it as an angle in degrees. A flat roof is near 0°, and a steep roof is far higher.</p>
</div><h2 id="how-we-tested-it">How we tested it</h2>
<p>Every figure here comes from PVWatts v8. The base case is a south-facing array at a 20° pitch with 14% losses, modeled in one reference city per state. Each other case is measured against that base. We report the average across states plus the extremes we found. Details are on our <a href="/methodology/">methodology page</a>.</p>
<h2 id="myth-solar-only-works-if-your-roof-has-the-perfect-angle">Myth: Solar only works if your roof has the perfect angle</h2>
<p><strong>Fact:</strong> A small miss on pitch costs very little.</p>
<p>Panels produce the most when sunlight hits them straight on. In the U.S., the sun sits in the southern half of the sky, so a panel tilted toward the south catches more of it over a year. The ideal tilt shifts with latitude, but the effect is gentle.</p>
<p>The test shows how gentle. On a south-facing roof, going from 20° to 30° moved output by 2.4% on average. Across states, the change ran from -1.7% in Hawaii to 4.7% in Alaska. Neither end is large enough to make or break a project on its own.</p>
24:T1709,<h2 id="myth-steeper-panels-always-produce-more">Myth: Steeper panels always produce more</h2>
<p><strong>Fact:</strong> Steeper helps in some places, hurts in others, and makes east- and west-facing roofs worse.</p>
<p>Latitude explains the first part. At high latitudes like Alaska, the sun stays low in the sky, so a steeper panel faces it better. Near the equator, as in Hawaii, the sun rides higher, and a flatter panel does better. That is why the 30° south roof gained the most in Alaska and slightly lost output in Hawaii.</p>

<p>Notice too that Hawaii shows the mildest losses for most directions. Alaska and Montana tend to show the steepest.</p>
<h2 id="myth-an-east-west-roof-means-solar-is-off-the-table">Myth: An east-west roof means solar is off the table</h2>
<p><strong>Fact:</strong> It produces less, but that alone does not settle the question.</p>
<p>East and west roofs lose real output, roughly 14.5% to 14.8% at 20°. Whether the system still pays off turns on your power price, your utility&#39;s credit rules and the system cost. Our guide to <a href="/guides/east-west-facing-solar-panels/">east-west facing solar panels</a> covers when that layout can still make sense.</p>
<p>The payback logic is simple. Less output means less electricity from the same hardware. The cost stays about the same, so payback takes longer. Our example home, a 7 kW system with no incentives described on the <a href="/methodology/">methodology</a> page, shows the pattern: a roof that loses 14.5% (east at 20°) pays back slower than one that loses 4.1% (southeast at 20°). For your own state, enter your roof in the <a href="/solar-calculator/">solar calculator</a>, and see <a href="/guides/solar-payback-period-explained/">solar payback period explained</a> for background.</p>
<p>Your price per <a href="/guides/what-is-a-kilowatt-hour/">kilowatt-hour</a> changes the picture as well. When it costs more, every kWh your panels make is worth more, so a few percent of lost output costs you more dollars. The <a href="/electricity-prices/">electricity prices by state</a> page uses EIA&#39;s August 2026 average residential data.</p>
<h2 id="myth-pitch-is-the-main-thing-to-ask-an-installer-about">Myth: Pitch is the main thing to ask an installer about</h2>
<p><strong>Fact:</strong> Several other factors can matter as much or more.</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Shade.</strong> Trees and chimneys can cost more than any pitch change.</li>
<li><strong>Roof space.</strong> A steeper roof may fit fewer or more panels, depending on layout. See <a href="/guides/how-many-solar-panels-do-i-need/">how many solar panels you need</a>.</li>
<li><strong>Export rules.</strong> Net metering and solar incentive programs vary by state and utility. DSIRE keeps a database of current programs at <a href="https://programs.dsireusa.org/">programs.dsireusa.org</a>. Also read <a href="/guides/net-metering-vs-net-billing-payback/">net metering vs. net billing</a>.</li>
<li><strong>Mounting.</strong> On a flat roof, installers can sometimes tilt the panels. On a pitched roof, panels usually follow the roof line.</li>
</ul>
<p>Better questions for a quote:</p>
<ol>
<li>What output do you expect for my exact roof direction and pitch?</li>
<li>Which tool did you use, and what loss assumption?</li>
<li>Are there roof sections that face a better direction?</li>
<li>How does my utility credit exported power?</li>
</ol>
<p>Estimates differ by company, so compare more than one quote. You can also model your own roof in PVWatts.</p>
<h2 id="what-actually-decides-it">What actually decides it</h2>
<p>Direction first. Southeast and southwest roofs lose 4.1% and 4.3% on average against south, while east and west lose 14.5% and 14.8%. Pitch comes well behind: a 30° south roof beats 20° by 2.4% on average.</p>
<p>After that, shade, usable roof space, your utility&#39;s export rules and your price per kWh can matter as much as pitch. A pitch that is a little off should not rule solar out. Check those factors, then run your roof through the <a href="/solar-calculator/">solar calculator</a>.</p>
