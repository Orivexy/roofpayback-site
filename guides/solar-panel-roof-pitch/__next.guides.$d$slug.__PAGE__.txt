23:T624,<div class="short-answer"><h2 id="short-answer">Short answer</h2>
<p>Roof pitch matters much less than which way the roof faces. In our numbers, moving a south-facing array from a 20° pitch to a 30° pitch changes yearly output by 2.4% on average across states. Facing east or west instead of south costs roughly 14.5% to 14.8% at a 20° pitch. So if your roof is a bit steeper or flatter than ideal, that is rarely a reason to skip solar. A roof that faces the wrong way is a bigger issue.</p>
</div><h2 id="what-pitch-means-and-why-it-matters">What &quot;pitch&quot; means and why it matters</h2>
<p>Pitch is how steep your roof is. In solar, people usually give it as an angle in degrees. A flat roof is near 0°. A steep roof is far higher.</p>
<p>Panels make the most power when sunlight hits them straight on. In the U.S., the sun sits in the southern half of the sky. A panel tilted toward the south catches more of it over a full year. The best tilt changes with your latitude, but the effect is gentle. A small miss costs little.</p>
<h2 id="how-we-measured-it">How we measured it</h2>
<p>We used PVWatts v8, a free solar production estimator from the U.S. Department of Energy&#39;s National Laboratory of the Rockies (formerly NREL). The base case is a south-facing array at a 20° pitch with 14% losses, using one reference city per state. Every other case is compared with that base. You can read more on our <a href="/methodology/">methodology page</a>.</p>
<p>Numbers below are the average across states, plus the smallest and largest change we found.</p>

<p>Why would steeper help in Alaska? The sun stays lower in the sky at high latitudes, so a steeper panel faces it better. Near the equator, the sun is higher, so a flatter panel does better.</p>

<p>If your home has an east-west roof, our guide to <a href="/guides/east-west-facing-solar-panels/">east-west facing solar panels</a> covers when it can still make sense.</p>
<h2 id="what-a-drop-in-output-means-for-your-payback">What a drop in output means for your payback</h2>
<p>Less output means less electricity from the same hardware. The cost stays about the same, so payback takes longer. Our example home is a good way to see this. It assumes:</p>
<ul>
<li>A 7 kW system.</li>
<li>10500 kWh a year of household use. A kWh, or kilowatt-hour, is the unit your utility bills you for. See <a href="/guides/what-is-a-kilowatt-hour/">what is a kilowatt hour</a>.</li>
<li>40% of use happens while the sun is up.</li>
<li>Installed cost of $3000 per kW, or $21000 in total, with no incentives.</li>
<li>$100 a year in maintenance.</li>
<li>Electricity prices rising 2.5% a year, and panels losing 0.5% of output a year.</li>
<li>A 25-year horizon.</li>
<li>Export credits capped at a year of grid purchases, with an annual true-up.</li>
</ul>
<p>Under those assumptions, a roof that loses 14.5% of output (east at 20°) will pay back slower than one that loses 4.1% (southeast at 20°). To see exact figures for your state, plug your roof into the <a href="/solar-calculator/">solar calculator</a>. For background, read <a href="/guides/solar-payback-period-explained/">solar payback period explained</a>.</p>
<p>Your local electricity price matters too. A high price makes each kWh your panels produce worth more. Check the <a href="/electricity-prices/">electricity prices by state</a> page. Prices come from the EIA&#39;s July 2026 average residential data.</p>
<h2 id="other-things-that-matter-as-much-as-pitch">Other things that matter as much as pitch</h2>
<ul>
<li><strong>Shade.</strong> Trees and chimneys can cost more than any pitch change.</li>
<li><strong>Roof space.</strong> A steeper roof may fit fewer or more panels, depending on layout. See <a href="/guides/how-many-solar-panels-do-i-need/">how many solar panels you need</a>.</li>
<li><strong>Your utility&#39;s export rules.</strong> Net metering and solar incentive programs vary by state and utility. DSIRE keeps a database of current programs at <a href="https://programs.dsireusa.org/">programs.dsireusa.org</a>. Also see <a href="/guides/net-metering-vs-net-billing-payback/">net metering vs. net billing</a>.</li>
<li><strong>Incentives.</strong> The federal Residential Clean Energy Credit (Section 25D) is not available for property placed in service after December 31, 2025, per the <a href="https://www.irs.gov/credits-deductions/residential-clean-energy-credit">IRS</a>. Our example home assumes no incentives. See <a href="/guides/is-solar-worth-it-without-tax-credit/">is solar worth it without the tax credit</a>.</li>
<li><strong>Mounting.</strong> Installers can sometimes tilt panels on a flat roof. On a pitched roof, panels usually follow the roof. Ask what your installer can do.</li>
</ul>
<h2 id="what-to-ask-an-installer">What to ask an installer</h2>
<ol>
<li>What output do you expect for my exact roof direction and pitch?</li>
<li>Which tool did you use, and what loss assumption?</li>
<li>Are there roof sections that face a better direction?</li>
<li>How does my utility credit exported power?</li>
</ol>
<p>Estimates differ by company, so compare more than one quote. You can also run your own roof in PVWatts.</p>
<h2 id="bottom-line">Bottom line</h2>
<p>For a south-facing roof, a 30° pitch beats 20° by 2.4% on average. That is a small gain. The direction of your roof matters far more: southeast and southwest lose 4.1% and 4.3% on average, while east and west lose 14.5% and 14.8%. Do not rule out solar because of a pitch that is a little off. Instead, check direction, shade, your utility&#39;s rules, and your own price per kWh. Then run the numbers in our <a href="/solar-calculator/">solar calculator</a>.</p>
