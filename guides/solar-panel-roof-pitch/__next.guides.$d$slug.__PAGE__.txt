22:T1aaa,<div class="short-answer"><p class="short-answer-label">The verdict</p>
<p>Pitch matters, but only by a few percent within the range most homes have. In PVWatts, tilting a south-facing array from 20° to 30° raised yearly output in 50 of our 51 reference cities, by 0.5% in Houston up to 4.7% in Anchorage, and cut it by 1.7% in Honolulu. The average gain was 2.4%.</p>
<p>On east- and west-facing roofs the same extra pitch works against you, costing 1% to 5% more output depending on the state. Direction is the larger effect: turning a roof from south to east or west costs about 14.5% to 14.8% at 20°, as <a href="/guides/east-west-facing-solar-panels/">east- or west-facing solar panels</a> shows.</p>
<p>The U.S. Department of Energy says panels &quot;perform best on south-facing roofs with a slope between 15 and 40 degrees,&quot; and that other roofs may be suitable too.</p>
</div><h2 id="convert-your-roof-pitch-to-degrees">Convert your roof pitch to degrees</h2>

33:T9b8,<h2 id="what-it-does-to-payback">What it does to payback</h2>
<p>Very little, because a few percent of output is a few percent of savings. Running our example home (7 kW, 10,500 kWh a year, $3,500 per kW, no incentives, September 2026 EIA prices, full export credit) at both pitches:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Alaska, south roof:</strong> 12.7 years at 20°, 12.2 years at 30°. This is the largest improvement in the table.</li>
<li><strong>Minnesota, south roof:</strong> 14 years at 20°, 13.5 years at 30°.</li>
<li><strong>Florida, south roof:</strong> 14.3 years at both pitches.</li>
<li><strong>Alaska, east roof:</strong> 15.7 years at 20°, 16.2 years at 30°. A steep east or west face pushes the other way.</li>
</ul>
<p>In practical terms, a steep south roof in the north is a small bonus and a steep east or west roof a small extra cost. Neither turns a good project into a bad one. Your electricity price and how exports are credited move payback far more; see <a href="/guides/net-metering-vs-net-billing-payback/">net metering vs. net billing</a>.</p>
<h2 id="what-we-did-not-test">What we did not test</h2>
<p>Our PVWatts data covers only 20° and 30°. We did not model low pitches under 20°, roofs steeper than 30°, or flat roofs where racks set the tilt, so the table says nothing about them. DOE&#39;s design guidance gives the general rule: &quot;To obtain the highest annual energy output, modules in the northern hemisphere are pointed due south and inclined at an angle equal to the local latitude.&quot; Real roofs rarely match that exactly, which is why the differences above stay small. PVWatts lets you enter your own tilt and direction for your address, and an installer&#39;s estimate should use your measured pitch.</p>
<h2 id="what-to-ask-an-installer">What to ask an installer</h2>
<ol>
<li>What tilt and direction does your estimate use, and was it measured on my roof?</li>
<li>Which roof faces did you consider, and why did you pick these?</li>
<li>On a flat or low-slope roof, will the panels be tilted on racks, and does that change how many fit?</li>
<li>What does shade from trees, chimneys or dormers cost on each face?</li>
</ol>
<p>Then compare quotes on expected yearly kWh and price per watt, and run the numbers in the <a href="/solar-calculator/">solar calculator</a>. For month-by-month output at the 20° baseline, see <a href="/guides/how-much-electricity-does-a-solar-panel-produce/">how much electricity a solar panel produces</a>.</p>
