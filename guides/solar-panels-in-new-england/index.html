Solar Panels in New England: Prices, Sun and Payback

The six New England states compared: payback, typical home use, price trends and how Connecticut, Maine and Vermont credit solar exports (September 2026 data).

Published November 3, 2026·Updated November 28, 2026·5 min read·RoofPayback editorial·Figures checked against EIA and PVWatts data

Official data: EIA · DOE · IRS · how we check

The verdict

New England has some of the shortest solar paybacks outside Hawaii and California because it has some of the highest electricity prices. The six states of the Census Bureau's New England division make similar amounts of power per kW, so the gaps between them come almost entirely from price. In our example, full-credit payback runs from 8.2 years in Maine to 10.9 in Vermont.

Two things are specific to the region. Typical homes use much less electricity than our example home, so right-sized systems are small. And the rules for exported power differ more from state to state than the sunshine does: Connecticut replaced net metering in 2022, Maine uses kWh credits that expire after a year, and Vermont adjusts its net-metering credits every two years.

The six states side by side

The example home is a 7 kW system at $24,500 using 10,500 kWh a year, with no incentives and PVWatts output for one reference city per state (methodology). Prices are EIA's September 2026 residential averages; the division as a whole averaged 29.56¢, against 18.17¢ nationally.

State (city) Price (change from a year earlier) Output per kW Full credit Half credit Quarter credit
Maine (Portland) 31.29¢ (+12.2%) 1,319 kWh 8.2 years 10.9 years 13.1 years
Massachusetts (Boston) 30.97¢ (+2.2%) 1,302 kWh 8.3 years 11.1 years 13.3 years
Rhode Island (Providence) 29.93¢ (+5.6%) 1,294 kWh 8.7 years 11.5 years 13.8 years
New Hampshire (Manchester) 29.17¢ (+5.2%) 1,259 kWh 9.1 years 12 years 14.2 years
Connecticut (Hartford) 27.09¢ (-10.9%) 1,252 kWh 9.8 years 12.9 years 15.2 years
Vermont (Burlington) 24.83¢ (+4.4%) 1,222 kWh 10.9 years 14.2 years 16.6 years

Output varies by less than 8% across the region; price varies by more than 25%. Even at quarter credit, every state pays back within 17 years.

Connecticut is the one state whose price fell over the year. A single year's move is not a trend, and over a longer span all six rose: comparing the 12 months to September 2017 with the 12 months to September 2026 in EIA's utility data, prices grew about 7.2% a year in Maine, 5.6% in Rhode Island, 4.9% in Massachusetts, 4% in New Hampshire and 3.5% in Connecticut and Vermont, before inflation.

Smaller homes, smaller systems

New England homes use less power than the national average, so the 10,500 kWh example home overstates what most need. EIA-861M sales data for October 2025 to September 2026 put the average residential account at 527 kWh a month in Maine and 705 in Connecticut, against 885 nationally. Sizing a system to each state's average account:

State Average use per month Average bill per month Right-sized system Payback, full credit
Maine 527 kWh $157 5 kW 8.4 years
Rhode Island 554 kWh $164 5 kW 8.8 years
Vermont 592 kWh $142 6 kW 11.1 years
Massachusetts 609 kWh $185 6 kW 8.8 years
New Hampshire 647 kWh $175 6 kW 9.2 years
Connecticut 705 kWh $192 7 kW 10 years

An account can be an apartment or a second home, so these are averages, not targets. The point for a buyer: a 5 or 6 kW system costs $17,500 to $21,000 at $3.50 per watt and still pays back in about the same time as the larger example, because cost and savings shrink together. How each state credits exports

This is where New England differs most, and where the half- and quarter-credit columns become relevant.

  • Connecticut. According to the Public Utilities Regulatory Authority, the Residential Renewable Energy Solutions program, "launched in January 2022," replaces the legacy net metering program. It has a Buy-All tariff and a Netting tariff, with rates updated for projects that enroll in 2026. The full-credit column is not a given for a new system; ask which tariff and rate the installer used.
  • Maine. The Public Utilities Commission's Net Energy Billing kWh Credit Program is open to all utility customers and gives kWh credits on the bill for power sent to the grid. Unused credits expire after 12 months, which works much like the yearly settlement in our model.
  • Vermont. The Public Utility Commission reviews and approves each utility's net-metering tariff and updates net-metering rate adjustors every two years; a 2026 biennial update is listed on its site. Check the credit value that applies on the date you register.
  • Massachusetts, New Hampshire and Rhode Island. We could not confirm the current residential rules from the regulators' own pages for this update. Ask your utility how an exported kWh is credited, then check its tariff with the Massachusetts Department of Public Utilities, the New Hampshire Public Utilities Commission or the Rhode Island Public Utilities Commission.

Net metering vs. net billing explains how these arrangements change payback.

Winter in New England

Winter output is low but not extreme by national standards: the summer-to-winter output ratio is 1.6 to 1.7 in five of the six states and 2 in Vermont. In our model, settling credits monthly instead of yearly added only 0.1 years in Vermont and 0.2 in Maine, because the example system's summer surplus is modest there. Snow on the panels is not included. Solar panels in winter by state has the full comparison.

Before you get quotes

  1. Total 12 months of kWh from your bills; expect a smaller system than the example.
  2. Find out which credit arrangement your utility offers new systems, and its current rate.
  3. Compare quotes on price per watt against the $3.50 used here; solar panel cost by state shows how much that moves payback.
  4. Run your numbers in the solar calculator, and see the Maine, Connecticut or Massachusetts pages for local detail.
