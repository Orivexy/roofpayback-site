Solar Panels in New England: Prices, Sun and Payback

Maine, New Hampshire, Massachusetts and more: how high New England electricity prices and lower winter sun shape solar payback for an example home.

The verdict

Solar in New England is a trade-off, and in our example home the trade-off comes out in solar's favor. The region has weak winter output and less yearly sun than the Southwest. It also has some of the highest electricity prices in the country, and those prices carry the math. Maine, Massachusetts and New Hampshire show some of the shorter paybacks outside Hawaii and California.

Every figure below uses the same example home, a 7 kW system at $24,500 with no incentives (methodology), with PVWatts output for one reference city per state and EIA residential prices for August 2026. Whether cloudy places can make solar work at all is covered in solar in cloudy states; this guide sticks to what is particular to the six New England states.

Myth: Winter makes the panels close to useless

Fact: Winter really is the weak spot, but where credits are settled over a full year, summer can cover for it.

PVWatts totals per kW for December through February, set against June through August:

State Winter Summer Ratio
Maine 236 kWh 412 kWh 1.7
Massachusetts 230 kWh 402 kWh 1.7
New Hampshire 234 kWh 383 kWh 1.6
Rhode Island 237 kWh 395 kWh 1.7
Connecticut 225 kWh 388 kWh 1.7
Vermont 199 kWh 396 kWh 2

Vermont has the widest swing. Its summer is about twice its winter. January there yields just 65 kWh per kW, against 137 kWh in July. Short days and snow on the panels both play a part. The monthly figures come from a model, so real snow cover could cut winter output further.

The saving grace is how credits are settled. Where a utility settles on an annual basis, a strong summer can offset a weak winter on paper. How that works for you is set by your utility's rules. Read net metering vs. net billing and solar panels in winter by state before counting on it.

Myth: Sunshine explains the payback gaps inside New England

Fact: Inside New England, payback follows the price of power far more closely than the sun.

The six states sit within a narrow band of output, but their prices are spread much wider:

State (reference city) Yearly output per kW Price (Aug 2026) Year earlier Full-credit payback
Maine (Portland) 1,319 kWh 32.11¢/kWh 28.14¢/kWh 8 years
Massachusetts (Boston) 1,302 kWh 30.48¢/kWh 30.55¢/kWh 8.5 years
Rhode Island (Providence) 1,294 kWh 28.18¢/kWh 26.53¢/kWh 9.2 years
New Hampshire (Manchester) 1,259 kWh 27.64¢/kWh 24.44¢/kWh 9.6 years
Connecticut (Hartford) 1,252 kWh 24.51¢/kWh 30.17¢/kWh 10.8 years
Vermont (Burlington) 1,222 kWh 23.84¢/kWh 22.1¢/kWh 11.3 years

The payback column follows the price column exactly. Output happens to fall in the same order, but it varies far less. Maine has the highest price of the six and pays back fastest. Vermont pairs the lowest output with the lowest price and comes last. Maine's 8 years at full credit is close to California's 7.4 years.

Prices did not all move the same way over the year. EIA puts New England as a whole at 28.31¢/kWh in August 2026, down from 29.01¢ a year earlier (-2.4%), against a U.S. average of 18.16¢. Within that, Maine rose 14.1% and New Hampshire 13.1%, while Connecticut fell 18.8% and Massachusetts barely moved. A single month is a snapshot, so a one-year swing is no forecast. State detail is on the Maine and Massachusetts price pages, and why electricity prices differ by state explains the gaps.

Myth: A lower export credit wipes out the savings

Fact: It stretches payback by years, but every New England state in the model still pays back inside 25 years.

The full-credit figures assume each exported kWh earns the retail rate. Some utilities credit exports at less than that, so the table also shows half and quarter credit:

State Full credit Half credit Quarter credit Year-1 savings (full)
Maine 8 years 10.7 years 12.8 years $2,964
Massachusetts 8.5 years 11.3 years 13.5 years $2,778
Rhode Island 9.2 years 12.2 years 14.5 years $2,552
New Hampshire 9.6 years 12.6 years 14.9 years $2,435
Connecticut 10.8 years 14.1 years 16.6 years $2,149
Vermont 11.3 years 14.7 years 17.2 years $2,039

The drop from full to quarter credit adds roughly five or six years in each state. High prices cushion it: the slowest New England case, Vermont at quarter credit, still finishes in 17.2 years. Lower-price states feel the same cut much harder; see the Texas guide for a contrast.

Myth: Without the federal tax credit, New England solar no longer works

Fact: None of the paybacks above use any incentive.

The IRS ended the 30% federal credit for systems placed in service after 2025, and the example already leaves it out. State and utility programs vary and change often; DSIRE keeps a database of current ones, and any program you qualify for would shorten payback further. Is solar worth it without the tax credit explains how to check what remains.

What actually decides it

Price decides most of it, and the export rule comes second. Beyond the price of power, these factors push your result either way:

  • Your export credit. The gap between the full-credit and quarter-credit columns is large. Find out how your utility credits exported power before you compare quotes.
  • Installed price. The example uses $3,500 per kW. A higher quote lengthens payback and a lower one shortens it; solar panel cost by state shows by how much.
  • Your usage. The example home uses 10,500 kWh per year. A smaller home saves less.
  • Roof shape and shade. Tilt, direction and trees all change output. See roof pitch and east-west panels.
  • Future prices. The model assumes 2.5% yearly increases. If prices fall, as Connecticut's did over the past year, savings shrink. See payback if electricity prices rise.

To test your own case:

  1. Pull 12 months of bills and add up your yearly kWh use.
  2. Look up your utility's export credit rules.
  3. Run your numbers in the solar calculator.
  4. Get several written quotes and compare the price per watt.
  5. Open your state page, such as New Hampshire solar panels or Maine solar panels.

In the example, full-credit paybacks run from 8 years in Maine to 11.3 years in Vermont, and the spread comes mostly from price, not sun. If you live in New England, your utility's rate and its export rule tell you more about your payback than your roof's view of the sky.
