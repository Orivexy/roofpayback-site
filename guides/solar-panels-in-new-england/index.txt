37:T12c1,<div class="short-answer"><h2 id="the-verdict">The verdict</h2>
<p>Solar in New England is a trade-off, and in our example home the trade-off comes out in solar&#39;s favor. The region has weak winter output and less yearly sun than the Southwest. It also has some of the highest electricity prices in the country, and those prices carry the math. Maine, Massachusetts and New Hampshire show some of the shorter paybacks outside Hawaii and California.</p>
<p>Every figure below uses the same example home, a 7 kW system at $24,500 with no incentives (<a href="/methodology/">methodology</a>), with PVWatts output for one reference city per state and EIA residential prices for August 2026. Whether cloudy places can make solar work at all is covered in <a href="/guides/cloudy-states-solar-worth-it/">solar in cloudy states</a>; this guide sticks to what is particular to the six New England states.</p>

<p>The drop from full to quarter credit adds roughly five or six years in each state. High prices cushion it: the slowest New England case, Vermont at quarter credit, still finishes in 17.2 years. Lower-price states feel the same cut much harder; see the <a href="/guides/is-solar-worth-it-in-texas/">Texas guide</a> for a contrast.</p>
<h2 id="myth-without-the-federal-tax-credit-new-england-solar-no-longer-works">Myth: Without the federal tax credit, New England solar no longer works</h2>
<p><strong>Fact:</strong> None of the paybacks above use any incentive.</p>
<p>The IRS ended the 30% federal credit for systems placed in service after 2025, and the example already leaves it out. State and utility programs vary and change often; <a href="https://programs.dsireusa.org/">DSIRE</a> keeps a database of current ones, and any program you qualify for would shorten payback further. <a href="/guides/is-solar-worth-it-without-tax-credit/">Is solar worth it without the tax credit</a> explains how to check what remains.</p>
<h2 id="what-actually-decides-it">What actually decides it</h2>
<p>Price decides most of it, and the export rule comes second. Beyond the price of power, these factors push your result either way:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Your export credit.</strong> The gap between the full-credit and quarter-credit columns is large. Find out how your utility credits exported power before you compare quotes.</li>
<li><strong>Installed price.</strong> The example uses $3,500 per kW. A higher quote lengthens payback and a lower one shortens it; <a href="/guides/solar-panel-cost-by-state/">solar panel cost by state</a> shows by how much.</li>
<li><strong>Your usage.</strong> The example home uses 10,500 kWh per year. A smaller home saves less.</li>
<li><strong>Roof shape and shade.</strong> Tilt, direction and trees all change output. See <a href="/guides/solar-panel-roof-pitch/">roof pitch</a> and <a href="/guides/east-west-facing-solar-panels/">east-west panels</a>.</li>
<li><strong>Future prices.</strong> The model assumes 2.5% yearly increases. If prices fall, as Connecticut&#39;s did over the past year, savings shrink. See <a href="/guides/solar-payback-if-electricity-prices-rise/">payback if electricity prices rise</a>.</li>
</ul>
<p>To test your own case:</p>
<ol>
<li>Pull 12 months of bills and add up your yearly kWh use.</li>
<li>Look up your utility&#39;s export credit rules.</li>
<li>Run your numbers in the <a href="/solar-calculator/">solar calculator</a>.</li>
<li>Get several written quotes and compare the price per watt.</li>
<li>Open your state page, such as <a href="/solar-panels/new-hampshire/">New Hampshire solar panels</a> or <a href="/solar-panels/maine/">Maine solar panels</a>.</li>
</ol>
<p>In the example, full-credit paybacks run from 8 years in Maine to 11.3 years in Vermont, and the spread comes mostly from price, not sun. If you live in New England, your utility&#39;s rate and its export rule tell you more about your payback than your roof&#39;s view of the sky.</p>
