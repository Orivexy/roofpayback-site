Why Homes Pay More for Electricity Than Businesses

Homes paid 18.16¢/kWh in August 2026 vs 9.64¢/kWh for industry, per EIA. See why delivery costs drive the gap and how it varies by state.

Quick verdict

Homes pay the most per unit of electricity in the U.S. In August 2026, the average residential price was 18.16¢ per kilowatt-hour (kWh), per the U.S. Energy Information Administration (EIA). Commercial customers, such as stores and offices, paid 14.47¢/kWh. Industrial customers, such as factories, paid 9.64¢/kWh.

Delivery explains most of the gap. The EIA says retail prices are usually highest for residential and commercial customers "because it costs more to distribute electricity to them." Big industrial users take power at higher voltages, which is cheaper to supply.

For a homeowner, the practical upshot is simple: the residential rate is the number on your bill, and it is usually higher than the averages you may see quoted for business or for all customers combined.

Side by side: the three customer types

The EIA reports average prices by customer type every month in its Electric Power Monthly. Recent months are preliminary and can be revised later.

Residential (homes) Commercial (stores, offices) Industrial (factories, plants) All sectors
August 2026 18.16¢/kWh 14.47¢/kWh 9.64¢/kWh 14.87¢/kWh
August 2025 17.57¢/kWh 13.87¢/kWh 8.98¢/kWh 14.15¢/kWh
Change 3.4% 4.3% 7.3%
How power arrives Lower voltage, local lines Lower voltage, local lines Higher voltage

Two quick reads. The order is the familiar one: homes highest, commercial in the middle, industry lowest. And all three rose over the year, with the industrial price rising fastest.

These are national averages. Your rate depends on your utility, plan and state. The full state list is on electricity prices by state, and the August 2026 electricity price guide looks at a single month in more detail.

Why delivery decides who pays more

Power travels a long way before it reaches an outlet. The EIA describes the grid as substations, transformers and power lines linking power plants to customers. Each customer type steps off that chain at a different point.

  • Generation. Power plants make the electricity. The EIA says generating cost is the largest part of the price.
  • Transmission. High-voltage lines carry power long distances. "Higher-voltage electricity makes long-distance electricity transmission more efficient and less expensive," the EIA notes.
  • Substations and transformers. These lower the voltage for the last part of the trip.
  • Distribution. Local lines carry lower-voltage electricity, which the EIA says is safer to use in homes and businesses.

Factories can leave the chain early. Industrial consumers "use more electricity and can receive it at higher voltages, so supplying electricity to these customers is more efficient and less expensive," per the EIA. Their retail price is generally close to the wholesale price, meaning close to what the power itself costs on the wholesale market.

Homes ride the chain all the way to the end, and each one uses a fairly small amount. So a home's rate covers the power plus a larger share of the local network that brings it to the door.

Behind all of it, the EIA says prices reflect the cost to build, finance, maintain and run power plants and the grid. Some for-profit utilities also build in a financial return for owners and shareholders.

When businesses win: states where homes pay far more

In most places, business and industry pay clearly less than households. For a homeowner, these are the states where the "all sectors" figure most understates your own rate. The EIA's August 2026 state figures show how wide that gap can get.

State Residential Commercial Industrial All sectors
Texas 15.91¢/kWh 8.96¢/kWh 7.21¢/kWh 11.23¢/kWh
New York 28.65¢/kWh 23.06¢/kWh 10.54¢/kWh 24.6¢/kWh
Pennsylvania 21.77¢/kWh 13.93¢/kWh 10.47¢/kWh 15.99¢/kWh
Michigan 22.99¢/kWh 16.21¢/kWh 8.99¢/kWh 16.71¢/kWh

Texas and New York stand out. In both, homes pay far more than businesses: a Texas household paid 15.91¢/kWh while the state's all-sector average was 11.23¢. Pennsylvania and Michigan follow the national order with a sizable step at each level.

When homes hold their own

In some states a household's rate sits much closer to what businesses pay, or the usual order gets scrambled.

Tennessee 13.42¢/kWh 13.13¢/kWh 6.92¢/kWh 11.93¢/kWh
North Dakota 13.31¢/kWh 8.07¢/kWh 8.12¢/kWh 8.91¢/kWh

California 29.75¢/kWh 30.02¢/kWh 24.12¢/kWh 29.02¢/kWh
  • Tennessee: the residential and commercial averages sit close together.
  • North Dakota and Nevada: industrial customers paid slightly more than commercial customers in August 2026.
  • California: the commercial average was slightly above the home rate, and every sector there pays a high price.

Monthly state averages can bounce around. One month is a snapshot, not a trend.

Where they tie

A few forces hit every customer type alike.

  • Rising prices. Homes, businesses and industry all paid more in August 2026 than in August 2025, nationally.
  • Summer peaks. The EIA says prices are usually highest in summer, when total demand is high. August is a summer month, so all of these figures carry that season.
  • Expensive states stay expensive. In Hawaii, residential was 47.18¢/kWh, commercial 43.55¢/kWh, industrial 39.33¢/kWh and all sectors 43.04¢/kWh. Even Hawaii's industrial customers paid more than homes in every other state listed here. The EIA says Hawaii's prices are high mainly because most of its electricity comes from petroleum fuels that must be imported.

What households can do about it

Switching a home to an industrial rate is not an option. Understanding the gap still helps you read your bill.

  • Find your all-in rate. Divide your bill total by the kWh you used. Then compare that number with your state's average.
  • Ask about rate plans. Some utilities offer time-of-day pricing to cut demand at peak times, the EIA notes. Whether it helps you depends on when you use power and on your utility's terms; see time-of-use rates explained.
  • Check whether you can shop for supply. In some states households can buy power from a supplier other than their utility. Electricity choice states explained covers where and how.

Which one fits you

For a homeowner, the right number depends on what you are deciding:

  • Budgeting or checking a bill: use the residential rate, ideally your own all-in rate from your bill. The all-sector average blends in cheaper business power and will look lower than what you pay.
  • Comparing states before a move: compare residential columns only. In Texas the gap between the residential and all-sector figures is far wider than in Tennessee, so mixing them distorts the comparison.
  • Weighing rooftop solar: use the residential rate. Power from your own panels replaces power you would have bought at that rate, and the price level is the first thing that decides payback. The second is how your utility credits exports: net metering and similar programs vary by state and utility, and where export credits are low they can override a high rate. DSIRE keeps a database of current programs, and net metering vs. net billing explains the math.
  • Running a business from a separate commercial account: a commercial account is priced on a business rate, so compare it with your state's commercial average instead.

To test your home, use the solar calculator, which combines EIA residential prices with PVWatts estimates; the methodology page lists every assumption.

The bottom line for households: you pay the delivery-heavy end of the price ladder, so every kWh you avoid buying is worth more to you than the same kWh is to a factory down the road.
