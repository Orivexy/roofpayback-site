21:Tfcf,<div class="short-answer"><h2 id="quick-verdict">Quick verdict</h2>
<p>Homes pay the most per unit of electricity in the U.S. In August 2026, the average residential price was 18.16¢ per <a href="/guides/what-is-a-kilowatt-hour/">kilowatt-hour</a> (kWh), per the U.S. Energy Information Administration (EIA). Commercial customers, such as stores and offices, paid 14.47¢/kWh. Industrial customers, such as factories, paid 9.64¢/kWh.</p>
<p>Delivery explains most of the gap. The EIA says retail prices are usually highest for residential and commercial customers &quot;because it costs more to distribute electricity to them.&quot; Big industrial users take power at higher voltages, which is cheaper to supply.</p>
<p>For a homeowner, the practical upshot is simple: the residential rate is the number on your bill, and it is usually higher than the averages you may see quoted for business or for all customers combined.</p>

<p>Two quick reads. The order is the familiar one: homes highest, commercial in the middle, industry lowest. And all three rose over the year, with the industrial price rising fastest.</p>
<p>These are national averages. Your rate depends on your utility, plan and state. <h2 id="why-delivery-decides-who-pays-more">Why delivery decides who pays more</h2>
<p>Power travels a long way before it reaches an outlet. The EIA describes the grid as substations, transformers and power lines linking power plants to customers. Each customer type steps off that chain at a different point.</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Generation.</strong> Power plants make the electricity. The EIA says generating cost is the largest part of the price.</li>
<li><strong>Transmission.</strong> High-voltage lines carry power long distances. &quot;Higher-voltage electricity makes long-distance electricity transmission more efficient and less expensive,&quot; the EIA notes.</li>
<li><strong>Substations and transformers.</strong> These lower the voltage for the last part of the trip.</li>
<li><strong>Distribution.</strong> Local lines carry lower-voltage electricity, which the EIA says is safer to use in homes and businesses.</li>
</ul>
<p>Factories can leave the chain early. Industrial consumers &quot;use more electricity and can receive it at higher voltages, so supplying electricity to these customers is more efficient and less expensive,&quot; per the EIA. Their retail price is generally close to the wholesale price, meaning close to what the power itself costs on the wholesale market.</p>
<p>Homes ride the chain all the way to the end, and each one uses a fairly small amount. So a home&#39;s rate covers the power plus a larger share of the local network that brings it to the door.</p>
<p>Behind all of it, the EIA says prices reflect the cost to build, finance, maintain and run power plants and the grid. Some for-profit utilities also build in a financial return for owners and shareholders.</p>

<p>Monthly state averages can bounce around. One month is a snapshot, not a trend.</p>
<h2 id="where-they-tie">Where they tie</h2>
<p>A few forces hit every customer type alike.</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Rising prices.</strong> Homes, businesses and industry all paid more in August 2026 than in August 2025, nationally.</li>
<li><strong>Summer peaks.</strong> The EIA says prices are usually highest in summer, when total demand is high. August is a summer month, so all of these figures carry that season.</li>
<li><strong>Expensive states stay expensive.</strong> In <a href="/electricity-prices/hawaii/">Hawaii</a>, residential was 47.18¢/kWh, commercial 43.55¢/kWh, industrial 39.33¢/kWh and all sectors 43.04¢/kWh. Even Hawaii&#39;s industrial customers paid more than homes in every other state listed here. The EIA says Hawaii&#39;s prices are high mainly because most of its electricity comes from petroleum fuels that must be imported.</li>
</ul>
<h2 id="what-households-can-do-about-it">What households can do about it</h2>
<p>Switching a home to an industrial rate is not an option. Understanding the gap still helps you read your bill.</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Find your all-in rate.</strong> Divide your bill total by the kWh you used. Then compare that number with your <a href="/electricity-prices/">state&#39;s average</a>.</li>
<li><strong>Ask about rate plans.</strong> Some utilities offer time-of-day pricing to cut demand at peak times, the EIA notes. Whether it helps you depends on when you use power and on your utility&#39;s terms; see <a href="/guides/time-of-use-electricity-rates-explained/">time-of-use rates explained</a>.</li>
<li><strong>Check whether you can shop for supply.</strong> In some states households can buy power from a supplier other than their utility. <a href="/guides/electricity-choice-states-explained/">Electricity choice states explained</a> covers where and how.</li>
</ul>
<h2 id="which-one-fits-you">Which one fits you</h2>
<p>For a homeowner, the right number depends on what you are deciding:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Budgeting or checking a bill:</strong> use the residential rate, ideally your own all-in rate from your bill. The all-sector average blends in cheaper business power and will look lower than what you pay.</li>
<li><strong>Comparing states before a move:</strong> compare residential columns only. In Texas the gap between the residential and all-sector figures is far wider than in Tennessee, so mixing them distorts the comparison.</li>
<li><strong>Weighing rooftop solar:</strong> use the residential rate. Power from your own panels replaces power you would have bought at that rate, and the price level is the first thing that decides payback. The second is how your utility credits exports: net metering and similar programs vary by state and utility, and where export credits are low they can override a high rate. DSIRE keeps a database of current programs, and <a href="/guides/net-metering-vs-net-billing-payback/">net metering vs. net billing</a> explains the math.</li>
<li><strong>Running a business from a separate commercial account:</strong> a commercial account is priced on a business rate, so compare it with your state&#39;s commercial average instead.</li>
</ul>
<p>To test your home, use the <a href="/solar-calculator/">solar calculator</a>, which combines EIA residential prices with PVWatts estimates; the <a href="/methodology/">methodology</a> page lists every assumption.</p>
<p>The bottom line for households: you pay the delivery-heavy end of the price ladder, so every kWh you avoid buying is worth more to you than the same kWh is to a factory down the road.</p>
