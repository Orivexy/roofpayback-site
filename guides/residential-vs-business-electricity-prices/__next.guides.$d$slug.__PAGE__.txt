21:T1fc3,<div class="short-answer"><p class="short-answer-label">Quick verdict</p>
<p>Homes pay the most per unit of electricity. In September 2026, the average U.S. residential price was 18.17¢ per <a href="/guides/what-is-a-kilowatt-hour/">kilowatt-hour</a> (kWh), according to the U.S. Energy Information Administration (EIA). Commercial customers such as stores and offices paid 14.27¢, and industrial customers such as factories paid 9.52¢. Nationally, a household paid 1.27 times the commercial price and 1.91 times the industrial price.</p>
<p>That ratio is far from uniform. In North Dakota homes paid 1.83 times what businesses paid, and in Texas 1.78 times. In Tennessee and California the two prices were almost equal. The ranking below covers every state.</p>
</div><h2 id="why-delivery-decides-who-pays-more">Why delivery decides who pays more</h2>
<p>The EIA says retail prices are usually highest for residential and commercial customers &quot;because it costs more to distribute electricity to them.&quot; Industrial consumers &quot;use more electricity and can receive it at higher voltages, so supplying electricity to these customers is more efficient and less expensive.&quot;</p>
<p>Power passes through a chain: generation, high-voltage transmission (&quot;higher-voltage electricity makes long-distance electricity transmission more efficient and less expensive,&quot; per the EIA), substations and transformers, and finally local distribution lines. A factory can take power at a higher voltage, before it is stepped down for local lines. A house sits at the very end of it and buys a small amount, so its rate carries a larger share of poles, wires, meters and billing per kWh.</p>
<p>Generation itself is still the biggest piece: &quot;The cost of generating electricity is the largest component of the price of electricity,&quot; the EIA says. Delivery explains the gap between customer types; generation explains much of the gap between states.</p>

32:T10c0,<h2 id="four-things-the-ranking-shows">Four things the ranking shows</h2>
<p><strong>A high ratio does not mean an expensive state.</strong> The top two, North Dakota and Texas, have below-average home prices (14.29¢ and 15.91¢). What sets them apart is unusually cheap business power: North Dakota&#39;s commercial price, 7.79¢, is the lowest in the country, with Nebraska (8.94¢) and Texas (8.96¢) next.</p>
<p><strong>A low ratio does not mean cheap homes.</strong> Hawaii and California are near the bottom of the ranking because everyone there pays a lot: businesses in California paid 30.38¢, more than homes paid anywhere except Hawaii, Maine, Massachusetts and California itself. In Tennessee, homes and businesses pay nearly the same modest price.</p>
<p><strong>New York is a middle case.</strong> Its homes paid 28.42¢ against 23.09¢ for businesses, a ratio of 1.23, below the national 1.27. Where New York stands out is industry: at 10.2¢, its industrial price is barely over a third of its home price.</p>
<p><strong>The &quot;all sectors&quot; average understates a household&#39;s rate most in Maine.</strong> Maine homes paid 8.45¢ more than its all-sector average (31.29¢ against 22.84¢). Michigan (6.98¢) and Pennsylvania (6.55¢) follow. If you see a single &quot;electricity price&quot; quoted for your state, check whether it is the residential figure.</p>
<h2 id="the-industrial-end-of-the-market">The industrial end of the market</h2>
<p>Industrial prices ranged from <strong>6.18¢ in New Mexico</strong> to <strong>40.89¢ in Hawaii</strong>. The other states under 7¢ were Louisiana (6.55¢), Tennessee (6.81¢) and Texas (6.99¢). After Hawaii, the highest were California (24.17¢), Rhode Island (20.55¢), Alaska (19.43¢) and Massachusetts (19.05¢). Hawaii&#39;s industrial price is above the residential price of every mainland state.</p>
<p>Industry usually pays the least, but not everywhere. In September 2026, industrial customers paid more than commercial customers in two states: North Dakota (8.35¢ against 7.79¢) and Nevada (10.07¢ against 9.87¢).</p>
<p>Single-month sector figures can swing, especially while they are preliminary. Georgia&#39;s August 2026 figures showed industry at 13.05¢ against 13.81¢ for commercial customers; in September, Georgia industry paid 8.81¢ and commercial customers 12.19¢. Before drawing a conclusion from one state&#39;s industrial price, check a second month.</p>
<h2 id="which-rate-to-use-for-your-decision">Which rate to use for your decision</h2>
<ul>
<li><strong>Budgeting or checking a bill:</strong> use the residential rate, ideally your own all-in rate (bill total divided by kWh). The all-sector average blends in cheaper business power.</li>
<li><strong>Comparing states before a move:</strong> compare residential columns only. In Maine, mixing in the all-sector figure would understate a household&#39;s price by more than a quarter.</li>
<li><strong>Weighing rooftop solar:</strong> use the residential rate, because panel output replaces power you would buy at that rate. How your utility credits exports is the second factor; see <a href="/guides/net-metering-vs-net-billing-payback/">net metering vs. net billing</a>. The <a href="/solar-calculator/">solar calculator</a> uses EIA residential prices, and the <a href="/methodology/">methodology</a> page lists its assumptions.</li>
<li><strong>Running a business from a separate commercial account:</strong> compare it with your state&#39;s commercial column instead.</li>
</ul>
<p>Rate plans can change what a household pays per kWh, too. Some utilities price by time of day (<a href="/guides/time-of-use-electricity-rates-explained/">time-of-use rates explained</a>), and in some states households can buy supply from a competing supplier (<a href="/guides/electricity-choice-states-explained/">electricity choice states explained</a>).</p>
<h2 id="how-we-measured-it">How we measured it</h2>
<p>All prices are EIA&#39;s average price to ultimate customers for September 2026, from the Electric Power Monthly, Table 5.6.A. Each figure is a sector&#39;s revenue divided by its kWh sold, so it averages many utilities and rate plans. Recent months are preliminary. The ratio is our calculation from those two columns.</p>
