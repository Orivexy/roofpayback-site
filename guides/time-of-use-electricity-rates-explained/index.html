Time-of-Use Electricity Rates Explained for Homeowners

How time-of-use rates work, with real 2026 peak hours and prices from PG&E and Georgia Power, and what moving EV charging out of the peak can save.

Plain-English answer

On a time-of-use (TOU) rate, the price of a kilowatt-hour (kWh) depends on the clock. Power costs more in the hours when the grid is busiest and less when demand is low. Your utility sets both the hours and the prices.

Whether TOU saves you money comes down to one question: can you move use out of the expensive hours? Two real 2026 tariffs show how different the answer can be. On PG&E's E-TOU-C plan in California, peak and off-peak prices differ by 3¢ to 12¢ per kWh. On Georgia Power's Overnight Advantage plan, the summer afternoon price is more than thirteen times the overnight price.

How it works

On a flat rate, every kWh costs the same at any hour. A TOU rate splits the day into periods, usually called peak, off-peak and sometimes shoulder (or partial peak, or super off-peak). Your meter records when you use power, and the bill prices each period separately.

Plans differ on five points, and the rate sheet answers all of them: which hours are peak, whether that changes by season, whether weekends count, how wide the price gap is, and what fixed charge or minimum bill sits on top.

Two real tariffs, side by side

PG&E, E-TOU-C (California). Peak is 4 p.m. to 9 p.m. every day, weekends included. Summer runs June 1 to September 30; the other eight months are winter. PG&E's pricing sheet, with prices effective March 1, 2026 and rounded to the nearest cent, shows:

E-TOU-C Peak (4–9 p.m.) Off-peak
Summer, use within your baseline allowance 44¢ 32¢
Summer, use above baseline 52¢ 40¢
Winter, use within baseline 32¢ 29¢
Winter, use above baseline 40¢ 37¢

The baseline allowance is a block of lower-priced energy set by where you live, your heating source and the season. PG&E notes that the sheet does not include all fees, such as local taxes or surcharges. PG&E's other main TOU plan, E-TOU-D, puts the peak at 5 p.m. to 8 p.m. on weekdays only, with all weekend and holiday hours off-peak.

Georgia Power, TOU-OA-15, "Overnight Advantage." For bills from June 2026:

TOU-OA Hours Energy charge
On-peak 2 p.m. to 7 p.m., Monday to Friday, June to September 30.3495¢
Off-peak 7 a.m. to 11 p.m. outside the on-peak window 10.3598¢
Super off-peak 11 p.m. to 7 a.m., every day, all year 2.2272¢

Independence Day and Labor Day count as off-peak. On top of these energy charges come a Basic Service Charge of $0.4603 a day and the fuel cost recovery and other riders the tariff lists, so these are not all-in prices. The contract term is one year: you must stay on the plan for twelve months, and it renews automatically unless you give 30 days' notice.

For comparison, Georgia Power's standard residential schedule (R-31) charges 8.2116¢ per kWh in winter and 8.7738¢ to 15.0828¢ in summer, depending on monthly use, before the same kinds of riders.

What shifting a load is worth

Take 3,500 kWh a year of home EV charging, the example used in our home EV charging cost guide, spread evenly across the year.

  • On PG&E E-TOU-C, the peak premium averages about 6¢ per kWh over the year (12¢ for four summer months, 3¢ for eight winter months, in either baseline tier). Charging entirely off-peak instead of entirely in the 4–9 p.m. window saves about $210 a year.
  • On Georgia Power TOU-OA, 3,500 kWh at the super off-peak energy charge costs about $78, against about $287 at the standard R-31 winter energy charge. Fuel and other riders are added to both, so the real gap differs; use these numbers to compare the plans' structure, not as a bill estimate.

The same arithmetic runs the other way. On TOU-OA, an air conditioner running from 2 p.m. to 7 p.m. on summer weekdays pays 30.3495¢ per kWh in energy charges, two to three and a half times the standard schedule's summer rates. A household that is home and cooling in those hours can pay more on TOU, not less.

Who tends to come out ahead

  • EV owners who can schedule overnight charging. Most cars and chargers can delay the start time.
  • Homes that can run laundry, dishwashers and pool pumps late at night or early in the morning.
  • Homes with a battery or a smart thermostat that pre-cools before the peak. Whether the peak gap covers a battery's cost is what the battery payback calculator works out.
  • People who are out during the peak window.

Who may pay more

Households that cook, cool and do chores in the peak window and cannot move them. Remote workers in hot climates on a plan with an afternoon peak are the clearest case. Before switching, ask your utility for a comparison of your last twelve months of usage on each plan; many utilities offer one, and the electricity cost calculator helps with the totals. Ask, too, whether there is a minimum term, as there is on Georgia Power's plan.

Time-of-use and rooftop solar

Panels produce most around midday, but PG&E's peak runs from 4 p.m. to 9 p.m., when output is falling or zero. So on E-TOU-C most solar output offsets off-peak hours, and the expensive evening hours are met by the grid or by a battery charged during the day. In California, new solar customers since April 15, 2023 are on the Net Billing Tariff, which credits exports at Avoided Cost Calculator values that the CPUC says are "usually lower than import rates." That is why batteries and evening load-shifting matter more there than they used to; net metering vs. net billing payback shows the effect on payback.

Our solar calculator uses a single flat price per kWh and does not model TOU periods, so treat its result as a first look and check it against your utility's TOU schedule.

Questions for your utility

  • What are the peak hours, and do they change by season or on weekends?
  • What is the price per kWh in each period, including riders?
  • What fixed charge or minimum bill applies? See fixed charges on your electric bill.
  • Is there a minimum term, and how do I switch back?
  • If I have solar, how are exports credited on this plan?

Glossary

  • Time-of-use (TOU) rate: a plan where the price per kWh changes with the time of day, and often with the season.
  • Peak / off-peak / super off-peak: the highest-, middle- and lowest-priced periods.
  • Baseline allowance: in California, a block of energy each month at the lowest price, based on climate zone and heating source.
  • Rider: an extra charge or credit a utility adds on top of the base rate, such as fuel cost recovery.
  • Export credit: what the utility credits you for solar power sent to the grid.
