Time-of-Use Electricity Rates Explained for Homeowners

How time-of-use electricity rates work, who they help, who they may cost more, and what to check with your utility, including how solar fits in.

Plain-English answer

On a time-of-use (TOU) rate, the price of electricity changes with the clock. Power costs more during the hours when lots of people use it and less when demand is low. Your utility picks those hours and sets those prices, so two homes in the same state can face very different rates.

Whether TOU saves you money comes down to one question: can you move some of your use into the cheap hours? Laundry, dishwasher cycles and EV charging are the usual candidates. If most of your use lands in the expensive hours and you can't shift it, TOU can raise your bill. Comparing your own usage with your utility's rate schedule is the only reliable way to tell.

How it works

Start with what most homes have today: a flat rate. Every kilowatt-hour (kWh) costs the same, whether you use it at noon or late in the evening.

A TOU rate splits the day into blocks. Utilities usually call them:

  • Peak — the expensive hours.
  • Off-peak — the cheap hours.
  • Shoulder or mid-peak — an in-between price some plans add.

Each block has its own price per kWh. Your meter records when you use power, and your bill adds up each block at its own price.

Behind the pricing is a simple fact about the grid. Electricity has to be produced at the moment it's used. When many homes run air conditioning or cook dinner at the same time, the system is under the most strain. TOU prices give customers a reason to ease off at those times and use more when the grid is quiet. How much that reason is worth to you rests on your habits and on the specific plan your utility offers.

What varies from one utility to the next

No two TOU plans are quite alike. Things to look for on a rate sheet:

  • Which hours count as peak.
  • How wide the gap is between peak and off-peak prices.
  • Whether the schedule changes by season.
  • Whether weekends and holidays get different pricing.
  • Whether a fixed monthly charge sits on top.

We don't list these details for specific utilities, because they change by utility and by plan. Your utility's website or customer service line is the place to get them.

An everyday analogy

Think of a movie theater with matinee pricing. The same seat and the same film cost less on a weekday afternoon than on a Friday night. If your schedule lets you go in the afternoon, you save. If you can only go on Friday night, you pay the full price, and maybe more than you would at a theater with one price all day.

TOU electricity works the same way. The utility is the theater. Peak hours are Friday night. A household that can run the dishwasher at "matinee" times wins. A household that has to cook, cool and do chores during the evening rush pays the premium.

The analogy has one limit. You choose when to see a movie. Some electricity use, like cooling a house on a hot afternoon, is hard to move.

Who tends to come out ahead

Flexible households usually do best. Examples:

  • EV owners who charge overnight. Overnight charging can often be scheduled for cheaper hours. Our guide on home EV charging cost by state compares charging costs.
  • Homes that can run laundry and dishwashers late at night or early in the morning.
  • Homes with a battery or a smart thermostat that shifts use on its own.
  • People who are out of the house during the usual evening peak.

Who may pay more

TOU can cost more if you're home most evenings and use a lot of power then. Families with young children, remote workers who cool the house all afternoon, and anyone who can't change when they cook or run the air conditioner may see higher bills.

Many utilities offer both a flat plan and a TOU plan. Where both exist, you can often compare them against a year of past bills before switching. Ask how to switch back if the new plan doesn't suit you.

The base price still matters

A TOU schedule sits on top of a price level that already varies hugely across the country. These are average residential prices from the U.S. Energy Information Administration (EIA) for August 2026:

State Average residential price (August 2026) A year earlier
Hawaii 47.18¢ per kWh 38.53¢
California 29.75¢ per kWh 30.92¢
New York 28.65¢ per kWh 26.64¢
Texas 15.91¢ per kWh 15.4¢
Louisiana 12.87¢ per kWh 12.55¢
Nevada 12.72¢ per kWh 12.54¢

These are statewide averages across all hours, not peak or off-peak prices. A TOU plan in any of these states would charge above the average at some hours and below it at others.

Where people get confused

"TOU and solar always go together well." Not necessarily. Panels produce during the day. If your most expensive hours fall in the evening, after sunset, solar covers less of the costly period. Daytime output still avoids buying power in those hours, but the big savings depend on when your peak is.

"What I get for exported power is the same everywhere." It isn't. When panels make more than the house uses, the extra goes to the grid. How the utility credits it varies by state and utility. DSIRE, a database of programs, is at programs.dsireusa.org. Our guide on net metering vs. net billing payback shows how those rules shift payback.

"A solar calculator already includes my TOU plan." Ours doesn't. It uses a simple example home and does not model TOU schedules, so your real result could be better or worse. The solar calculator is a starting point, not a substitute for your rate sheet.

Questions to put to your utility

  • What hours are peak, and do they change by season?
  • What is the price per kWh in each period?
  • Are there fixed monthly charges or demand charges?
  • Can I try the plan and switch back?
  • If I have or plan to get solar, how are exported kWh credited under this plan?

Write the answers down and set them next to your last twelve months of bills. The electricity cost calculator helps you see what you use.

Glossary

  • Time-of-use (TOU) rate: A plan where the price per kWh changes by time of day, and sometimes by season.
  • Flat rate: A plan where every kWh costs the same at any hour.
  • Peak: The highest-priced hours on a TOU plan, when demand on the grid is greatest.
  • Off-peak: The lowest-priced hours, when demand is low.
  • Shoulder (mid-peak): An in-between price period some plans use.
  • Fixed charge: A monthly fee you pay no matter how much power you use.
  • Demand charge: A charge based on your highest rate of use, rather than total energy. Ask your utility whether your plan has one.
  • Export credit: What the utility pays or credits you for solar power you send to the grid.

The short version

A TOU rate rewards using power when it's cheap and charges more when it isn't. Flexible households tend to benefit. Homes that use most of their power at peak can pay more. Prices also differ widely by state, so your utility's rate sheet tells you more than any average.

Thinking about solar? The timing of your peak hours decides how much of the expensive period your panels can cover, so get the TOU schedule and the export-credit terms from your utility before trusting any payback estimate. The solar calculator gives a flat-rate first look to compare against.
