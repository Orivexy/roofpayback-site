Which States Use the Most Electricity per Home?

Louisiana homes average 1,231 kWh a month versus 507 in California. See usage, bills and prices for the highest and lowest states, and why use isn't cost.

Published November 23, 2026·Updated November 26, 2026·5 min read·RoofPayback editorial·Figures checked against EIA and PVWatts data

At a glance

Louisiana tops the list: the average home there used 1,231 kWh per month between October 2025 and September 2026, according to EIA data. Tennessee (1,216) and Mississippi (1,192) follow. At the other end, California homes averaged just 507 kWh, with Hawaii (515) and Maine (527) close behind. Heat, air conditioning and electric heating explain most of the gap, and a low-use state can still have a high bill.

One term first: a "customer" in these numbers is one utility account, usually one household. A kilowatt-hour (kWh) is the unit your utility bills you for; this explainer covers it in detail.

Louisiana, Tennessee, Mississippi and Alabama: long summers, cheap power

Start in the Gulf South, where four states sit at the top of the usage ranking. Louisiana averages 1,231 kWh a month (14,768 kWh a year). Tennessee is at 1,216, Mississippi at 1,192 and Alabama at 1,157.

Air conditioning runs for months here, and many homes also heat with electricity. Cheap power makes that easy to live with: Louisiana's September 2026 residential price was 13.33¢ per kWh, Tennessee's 13.29¢ and Mississippi's 14.47¢.

That is why Louisiana's average bill is $161 a month, about the same as the $160 national average, even though its homes use far more electricity than the U.S. figure of 885 kWh. Alabama is the exception in this group: its $191 monthly bill ranks 4th highest in the country.

Texas and Florida: the August and July peaks

Texas homes averaged 1,133 kWh a month and a $181 bill. The highest month was August, at $266, which is what a Gulf Coast summer does to a cooling load. Florida used 1,104 kWh with a $169 average bill, peaking in July at $222.

Neither state has a winter spike worth worrying about. For a household here, the July and August bills show how much of the year's use is air conditioning, and that use falls in the afternoon, when panels produce. Whether a system sized to the annual total also covers the summer months depends on how your utility settles credits: with an annual true-up, surplus from mild months can offset summer purchases; with monthly settlement, each month stands more on its own. Solar and daytime electricity use and oversized solar system annual true-up explain both.

Maryland, Virginia and Delaware: heat plus electric heat

Maryland is the surprise of the mid-Atlantic. It averages 960 kWh a month (11,514 kWh a year) at a price of 21.54¢ per kWh in September 2026, and its average bill of $204 a month is the second highest in the nation. Its worst month is January at $271, a sign that electric heating matters as much as summer cooling.

Virginia sits at 1,079 kWh and $181, also peaking in January ($240). Delaware uses 942 kWh and pays $170, with its highest bill in August ($210). Two seasons of heavy use are the pattern in this region, which is why a mid-Atlantic solar estimate has to account for winter output too.

New York and New England: low use, high prices

Massachusetts averages only 609 kWh a month, yet its bill is $185 because its price is among the highest (30.97¢ per kWh in September 2026). New York is lower still at 591 kWh and still pays $169. Connecticut uses 705 kWh and pays $192, third highest in the country.

Compare Connecticut with Louisiana. Louisiana uses far more electricity and pays less per month. In New England, price rather than volume drives the bill, and Maine shows it clearly: 527 kWh a month, but a 31.29¢ price in September 2026.

Natural gas and oil heat many homes here, so electricity is not carrying the winter load. Check New England solar for what that means for panel economics.

California and Hawaii: the lowest use in the country

California is the lowest of all 51 entries at 507 kWh a month (6,084 kWh a year). Mild coastal weather and widespread efficiency programs keep demand down, while a price of 30.86¢ per kWh (September 2026) still produces a $163 average bill.

Hawaii is nearly as low at 515 kWh (6,181 kWh a year), but its 48.31¢ price pushes the bill to $232, the highest of any state. It peaks in August at $269. For the islands in more detail, see why electricity is expensive in Hawaii.

New Mexico, Utah and Colorado: small bills in the interior West

The cheapest bills in the country are in the interior West. New Mexico averages $102 a month on 662 kWh, Utah $105 on 793 kWh, and Colorado $115 on 686 kWh. Dry air, cool nights and moderate prices all help.

These states show the opposite of Hawaii: modest use, modest prices, small bills. They also show why solar where electricity is cheap needs a harder look at installed cost.

What your state's usage means for a solar decision

Your own usage matters more than any state average. The example home on this site uses 10,500 kWh a year, which falls between Iowa's 10,265 kWh and Ohio's 10,509 kWh; the rest of the assumptions are on the methodology page. If your home uses far more, as in Louisiana or Tennessee, a larger system may pay for itself in a way a state-average estimate hides. If it uses far less, as in California, a smaller system fits better.

Pull your last twelve bills and add up the kWh. Then put that number in the solar calculator instead of the state default. Homes that run on electricity for both cooling and heating, like many in Maryland and Virginia, should also read solar for high electricity use.
