21:Ta9b,<div class="short-answer"><p class="short-answer-label">At a glance</p>
<p>Louisiana tops the list: the average home there used <strong>1,231 kWh per month</strong> between October 2025 and September 2026, according to EIA data. Tennessee (1,216) and Mississippi (1,192) follow. At the other end, California homes averaged just <strong>507 kWh</strong>, with Hawaii (515) and Maine (527) close behind. Heat, air conditioning and electric heating explain most of the gap, and a low-use state can still have a high bill.</p>
<p>One term first: a &quot;customer&quot; in these numbers is one utility account, usually one household. A kilowatt-hour (kWh) is the unit your utility bills you for; <a href="/guides/what-is-a-kilowatt-hour/">this explainer</a> covers it in detail.</p>
</div><h2 id="louisiana-tennessee-mississippi-and-alabama-long-summers-cheap-power">Louisiana, Tennessee, Mississippi and Alabama: long summers, cheap power</h2>
<p>Start in the Gulf South, where four states sit at the top of the usage ranking. Louisiana averages 1,231 kWh a month (14,768 kWh a year). Tennessee is at 1,216, Mississippi at 1,192 and Alabama at 1,157.</p>
<p>Air conditioning runs for months here, and many homes also heat with electricity. Cheap power makes that easy to live with: Louisiana&#39;s September 2026 residential price was 13.33¢ per kWh, Tennessee&#39;s 13.29¢ and Mississippi&#39;s 14.47¢.</p>
<p>That is why Louisiana&#39;s average bill is $161 a month, about the same as the $160 national average, even though its homes use far more electricity than the U.S. figure of 885 kWh. Alabama is the exception in this group: its $191 monthly bill ranks 4th highest in the country.</p>
<h2 id="texas-and-florida-the-august-and-july-peaks">Texas and Florida: the August and July peaks</h2>
<p>Texas homes averaged 1,133 kWh a month and a $181 bill. The highest month was August, at $266, which is what a Gulf Coast summer does to a cooling load. Florida used 1,104 kWh with a $169 average bill, peaking in July at $222.</p>
<p>Neither state has a winter spike worth worrying about. For a household here, the July and August bills show how much of the year&#39;s use is air conditioning, and that use falls in the afternoon, when panels produce. Whether a system sized to the annual total also covers the summer months depends on how your utility settles credits: with an annual true-up, surplus from mild months can offset summer purchases; with monthly settlement, each month stands more on its own. <a href="/guides/solar-daytime-electricity-use/">Solar and daytime electricity use</a> and <a href="/guides/oversized-solar-system-annual-true-up/">oversized solar system annual true-up</a> explain both.</p>

32:T141b,<h2 id="maryland-virginia-and-delaware-heat-plus-electric-heat">Maryland, Virginia and Delaware: heat plus electric heat</h2>
<p>Maryland is the surprise of the mid-Atlantic. It averages 960 kWh a month (11,514 kWh a year) at a price of 21.54¢ per kWh in September 2026, and its average bill of <strong>$204 a month</strong> is the second highest in the nation. Its worst month is January at $271, a sign that electric heating matters as much as summer cooling.</p>
<p>Virginia sits at 1,079 kWh and $181, also peaking in January ($240). Delaware uses 942 kWh and pays $170, with its highest bill in August ($210). Two seasons of heavy use are the pattern in this region, which is why a <a href="/guides/solar-panels-in-the-mid-atlantic/">mid-Atlantic solar</a> estimate has to account for winter output too.</p>
<h2 id="new-york-and-new-england-low-use-high-prices">New York and New England: low use, high prices</h2>
<p>Massachusetts averages only 609 kWh a month, yet its bill is $185 because its price is among the highest (30.97¢ per kWh in September 2026). New York is lower still at 591 kWh and still pays $169. Connecticut uses 705 kWh and pays $192, third highest in the country.</p>
<p>Compare Connecticut with Louisiana. Louisiana uses far more electricity and pays less per month. In New England, price rather than volume drives the bill, and Maine shows it clearly: 527 kWh a month, but a 31.29¢ price in September 2026.</p>
<p>Natural gas and oil heat many homes here, so electricity is not carrying the winter load. Check <a href="/guides/solar-panels-in-new-england/">New England solar</a> for what that means for panel economics.</p>
<h2 id="california-and-hawaii-the-lowest-use-in-the-country">California and Hawaii: the lowest use in the country</h2>
<p>California is the lowest of all 51 entries at 507 kWh a month (6,084 kWh a year). Mild coastal weather and widespread efficiency programs keep demand down, while a price of 30.86¢ per kWh (September 2026) still produces a $163 average bill.</p>
<p>Hawaii is nearly as low at 515 kWh (6,181 kWh a year), but its 48.31¢ price pushes the bill to $232, the highest of any state. It peaks in August at $269. For the islands in more detail, see <a href="/guides/why-is-electricity-expensive-in-hawaii/">why electricity is expensive in Hawaii</a>.</p>
<h2 id="new-mexico-utah-and-colorado-small-bills-in-the-interior-west">New Mexico, Utah and Colorado: small bills in the interior West</h2>
<p>The cheapest bills in the country are in the interior West. New Mexico averages $102 a month on 662 kWh, Utah $105 on 793 kWh, and Colorado $115 on 686 kWh. Dry air, cool nights and moderate prices all help.</p>
<p>These states show the opposite of Hawaii: modest use, modest prices, small bills. They also show why <a href="/guides/is-solar-worth-it-where-electricity-is-cheap/">solar where electricity is cheap</a> needs a harder look at installed cost.</p>

<h2 id="what-your-state-39-s-usage-means-for-a-solar-decision">What your state&#39;s usage means for a solar decision</h2>
<p>Your own usage matters more than any state average. The example home on this site uses 10,500 kWh a year, which falls between Iowa&#39;s 10,265 kWh and Ohio&#39;s 10,509 kWh; the rest of the assumptions are on the <a href="/methodology/">methodology</a> page. If your home uses far more, as in Louisiana or Tennessee, a larger system may pay for itself in a way a state-average estimate hides. If it uses far less, as in California, a smaller system fits better.</p>
<p>Pull your last twelve bills and add up the kWh. Then put that number in the <a href="/solar-calculator/">solar calculator</a> instead of the state default. Homes that run on electricity for both cooling and heating, like many in Maryland and Virginia, should also read <a href="/guides/how-many-solar-panels-do-i-need/">solar for high electricity use</a>. For how bills compare across states without the usage angle, see <a href="/guides/average-electric-bill-by-state/">average electric bill by state</a>.</p>
