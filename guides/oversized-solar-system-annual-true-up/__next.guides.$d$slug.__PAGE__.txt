22:Tae3,<div class="short-answer"><h2 id="the-answer">The answer</h2>
<p>Yes, a solar system can be too big. If your panels make more power over a year than your home buys from the grid, the extra <a href="/guides/what-is-a-kilowatt-hour/">kilowatt-hours</a> (kWh) may earn no credit at all. In the example runs, once production passes that line, each added panel costs money but saves nothing in year 1.</p>
<p>Utility and state rules decide how much this hurts you. Before you sign a quote, find out how your utility handles extra production.</p>
</div><h2 id="what-is-an-annual-true-up-exactly">What is an annual true-up, exactly?</h2>
<p>Plenty of net metering programs keep a running tally of your credits all year. Sunny months build credits. Dark months use them up. At year&#39;s end, the utility does a &quot;true-up&quot;: a yearly settlement of what you sent to the grid against what you bought.</p>
<p>The model here caps export credits at a year of grid purchases. You can wipe out your annual purchases, but you cannot earn past that. Anything produced beyond the cap is uncredited.</p>
<p>Real utilities differ. Some pay a low rate for leftover kWh, and some pay nothing. The model assumes no extra value so you can see the downside clearly. For how different crediting styles compare, read <a href="/guides/net-metering-vs-net-billing-payback/">net metering vs. net billing payback</a>.</p>
<h2 id="how-can-i-tell-when-a-system-has-hit-the-cap">How can I tell when a system has hit the cap?</h2>
<p>Watch year-1 savings as the system grows. When they stop rising, you have hit the wall.</p>
<p>Texas shows it step by step. Savings go from $678 at 3 kW to $1,131 at 5 kW to $1,583 at 7 kW. They reach $1,671 at 9 kW, then stay at $1,671 at 12 kW. The hardware keeps getting pricier, but the savings flatten. Everything above that flat line is uncredited.</p>

<p>It stretches it. Payback is the time it takes for savings to repay the system cost. When cost keeps climbing and savings stand still, that time grows. The <a href="/guides/solar-payback-period-explained/">payback period guide</a> explains the calculation.</p>

<h2 id="does-a-bigger-system-at-least-earn-more-over-25-years">Does a bigger system at least earn more over 25 years?</h2>
<p>Usually not, once you pass the cap. The 25-year gain falls as size climbs:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Florida:</strong> $24,658 at 7 kW, $19,763 at 9 kW, $9,263 at 12 kW.</li>
<li><strong>Arizona:</strong> $26,978, $19,978 and $9,478.</li>
<li><strong>California:</strong> $79,631, $72,700 and $62,200.</li>
<li><strong>Texas:</strong> $23,624 at 7 kW and $23,062 at 9 kW, nearly level, then 12 kW drops to $12,562.</li>
</ul>
<p>So across these runs, the biggest gain usually sits at or near the size that just covers annual use. Going bigger gives back less, not more.</p>
<h2 id="what-size-is-just-right-where-i-live">What size is &quot;just right&quot; where I live?</h2>

<h2 id="should-i-size-up-for-the-future-or-for-panel-aging">Should I size up for the future, or for panel aging?</h2>
<p>Consider three forces.</p>
<p>First, panels age. The model has them losing 0.5%/yr, so production shrinks over time. A system sized right to the edge of the cap wastes little in the early years.</p>
<p>Second, the cap is a hard wall in this model. Production above your annual purchases earns nothing, so the last panels added have no payoff.</p>
<p>Third, your usage may change. Adding an EV or a heat pump can justify a slightly larger system. The <a href="/guides/solar-and-electric-car-charging/">solar and EV charging guide</a> shows how the right size grows with a car, and the <a href="/guides/solar-for-high-electricity-use/">high-usage homes guide</a> covers bigger households. Size for it only when the plan is firm.</p>
<p>Treat any &quot;best size&quot; as an estimate. It moves with your rate plan, your roof and your utility&#39;s rules.</p>
<h2 id="what-should-i-ask-my-installer-and-utility">What should I ask my installer and utility?</h2>
<ul>
<li>Does my utility credit extra kWh at the end of the year, and at what rate?</li>
<li>Is my plan net metering or net billing? Does the credit change by time of day?</li>
<li>Is there a size limit tied to my past usage?</li>
<li>Will my usage change in the next few years?</li>
</ul>
<p>Net metering rules, including what happens to leftover credits, are tracked by state in the <a href="https://programs.dsireusa.org/">DSIRE database</a>. Your utility&#39;s tariff has the final word.</p>
<h2 id="if-you-remember-one-thing">If you remember one thing</h2>
<p>Bigger is not automatically better. Once production passes your annual grid purchases, extra panels add cost without adding savings, and payback gets longer.</p>
<p>Aim for a system near your yearly use, and adjust only for firm changes such as an EV. Test different sizes with your own numbers in the <a href="/solar-calculator/">solar calculator</a>, check your state&#39;s rate on <a href="/electricity-prices/">electricity prices</a>, and ask your utility how it treats extra production before you buy.</p>
