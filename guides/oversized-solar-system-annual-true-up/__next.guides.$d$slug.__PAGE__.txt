23:T91d,<div class="short-answer"><h2 id="short-answer">Short answer</h2>
<p>Yes, a solar system can be too big. When your panels make more power over a year than your home buys from the grid, the extra kilowatt-hours (kWh) may earn no credit at all. In our example, the best size is below 100% offset. Past that point, each added panel costs money but saves nothing.</p>
<p>This depends on your utility and your state&#39;s rules. Always check how your utility handles extra production before you sign a quote.</p>
</div><h2 id="what-an-annual-true-up-means">What an annual true-up means</h2>
<p>Many net metering programs track your credits all year. Sunny months build up credits. Dark months use them. At the end of the year, the utility does a &quot;true-up,&quot; which is a yearly settlement of what you sent to the grid and what you bought.</p>
<p>In our model, export credits are capped at a year of grid purchases. That means you can wipe out your annual purchases, but you cannot earn more than that. Any production beyond the cap is uncredited. Some utilities pay a low rate for leftover kWh. Others pay nothing. The model assumes no extra value, so you can see the downside clearly.</p>
<p>For a full comparison of crediting styles, see our guide on <a href="/guides/net-metering-vs-net-billing-payback/">net metering vs. net billing payback</a>. If kWh is a new term, start with <a href="/guides/what-is-a-kilowatt-hour/">what is a kilowatt-hour</a>.</p>
<h2 id="the-example-home">The example home</h2>
<p>Every number below uses the same example home. Here are its assumptions:</p>
<ul>
<li>10500 kWh/yr of electricity use</li>
<li>40% of use happens while the sun is up</li>
<li>Installed cost of $3000 per kW, with no incentives</li>
<li>$100/yr maintenance</li>
<li>Electricity price rising 2.5%/yr</li>
<li>Panel degradation of 0.5%/yr</li>
<li>A 25-year horizon</li>
<li>Export credits capped at a year of grid purchases</li>
</ul>
<p>Production comes from PVWatts v8 for a south-facing roof at 20° pitch with 14% losses, using one reference city per state. Prices are EIA July 2026 average residential. PVWatts is a free solar production estimator from the U.S. Department of Energy&#39;s National Laboratory of the Rockies (formerly NREL). Our <a href="/methodology/">methodology page</a> explains the full model.</p>

<p>The 25-year gain tells the same story. In Florida, the 25-year gain is $28296 at 7 kW, $24406 at 9 kW and $15406 at 12 kW. In Arizona it is $31661, $25661 and $16661. <p>So in these runs, the biggest gain usually sits at or near the size that just covers annual use. A bigger system gives back less, not more.</p>
<h2 id="why-the-best-size-is-below-100-offset">Why the best size is below 100% offset</h2>
<p>Offset means the share of your yearly use that solar covers. You might expect 100% to be the sweet spot. The tables suggest otherwise, for three reasons.</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Model degradation.</strong> Panels lose 0.5%/yr, so production shrinks over time. But a system sized to the edge of the cap wastes little early on.</li>
<li><strong>The cap is a hard wall.</strong> Production above annual purchases earns nothing in this model, so the last panels you add have no payoff.</li>
<li><strong>Your usage may change.</strong> If you add an EV or a heat pump, a slightly larger system can make sense. Our <a href="/guides/home-ev-charging-cost-by-state/">EV charging cost guide</a> shows how much that load can matter. If you plan that change, size for it only when it is firm.</li>
</ul>
<p>Treat the exact best size as an estimate. It moves with your rate plan, your roof and your utility&#39;s rules.</p>

<h2 id="what-to-ask-your-installer-and-utility">What to ask your installer and utility</h2>
<ul>
<li>Does my utility credit extra kWh at the end of the year? At what rate?</li>
<li>Is my plan net metering or net billing? Does the credit change by time of day?</li>
<li>Is there a size limit tied to my past usage?</li>
<li>Will my usage change in the next few years?</li>
</ul>
<p>You can check state programs in the DSIRE database at <a href="https://programs.dsireusa.org/">https://programs.dsireusa.org/</a>, which tracks current net metering and incentive programs. Rules vary by state and utility. The federal 30% Residential Clean Energy Credit is not available for property placed in service after December 31, 2025, so do not assume it lowers your cost. Our guide on <a href="/guides/is-solar-worth-it-without-tax-credit/">solar without the tax credit</a> explains the effect.</p>
<h2 id="bottom-line">Bottom line</h2>
<p>A bigger system is not always a better one. In our example, once production passes your annual grid purchases, extra panels add cost but no savings, and payback gets longer. Aim for a system near your yearly use, then adjust for known changes like an EV. Use the <a href="/solar-calculator/">solar calculator</a> to test sizes with your own numbers, and check <a href="/electricity-prices/">electricity prices</a> for your state. Then ask your utility how it treats extra production before you buy.</p>
