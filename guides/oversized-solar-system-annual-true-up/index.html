Can a Solar System Be Too Big? Annual True-Up Explained

An oversized solar system can produce kWh your utility never credits. See where the waste shows up in sizing tables and how to pick a smarter size.

The answer

Yes, a solar system can be too big. If your panels make more power over a year than your home buys from the grid, the extra kilowatt-hours (kWh) may earn no credit at all. In the example runs, once production passes that line, each added panel costs money but saves nothing in year 1.

Utility and state rules decide how much this hurts you. Before you sign a quote, find out how your utility handles extra production.

What is an annual true-up, exactly?

Plenty of net metering programs keep a running tally of your credits all year. Sunny months build credits. Dark months use them up. At year's end, the utility does a "true-up": a yearly settlement of what you sent to the grid against what you bought.

The model here caps export credits at a year of grid purchases. You can wipe out your annual purchases, but you cannot earn past that. Anything produced beyond the cap is uncredited.

Real utilities differ. Some pay a low rate for leftover kWh, and some pay nothing. The model assumes no extra value so you can see the downside clearly. For how different crediting styles compare, read net metering vs. net billing payback.

How can I tell when a system has hit the cap?

Watch year-1 savings as the system grows. When they stop rising, you have hit the wall.

Texas shows it step by step. Savings go from $678 at 3 kW to $1,131 at 5 kW to $1,583 at 7 kW. They reach $1,671 at 9 kW, then stay at $1,671 at 12 kW. The hardware keeps getting pricier, but the savings flatten. Everything above that flat line is uncredited.

Other states hit the cap sooner:

State 7 kW year-1 savings 9 kW year-1 savings 12 kW year-1 savings
Texas $1,583 $1,671 $1,671
California $3,124 $3,124 $3,124
Florida $1,574 $1,574 $1,574
Arizona $1,580 $1,580 $1,580

California, Florida and Arizona are already capped at 7 kW. Moving up to 9 kW or 12 kW adds cost and no year-1 savings. Texas at 7 kW falls slightly short of the cap, and 9 kW just reaches it.

These runs start from our example home using 10,500 kWh a year, priced at $3,500 per kW with no incentives (methodology), with output from PVWatts v8 and EIA August 2026 prices.

What does an oversized system do to payback?

It stretches it. Payback is the time it takes for savings to repay the system cost. When cost keeps climbing and savings stand still, that time grows. The payback period guide explains the calculation.

State 7 kW payback 9 kW payback 12 kW payback
Texas 14.3 years 16.3 years 20.5 years
California 7.4 years 9.3 years 12 years
Florida 14.1 years 17.2 years 21.5 years
Arizona 13.9 years 17.1 years 21.4 years

Notice that even Texas, where 9 kW still adds some savings, takes longer to pay back at 9 kW than at 7 kW.

Does a bigger system at least earn more over 25 years?

Usually not, once you pass the cap. The 25-year gain falls as size climbs:

  • Florida: $24,658 at 7 kW, $19,763 at 9 kW, $9,263 at 12 kW.
  • Arizona: $26,978, $19,978 and $9,478.
  • California: $79,631, $72,700 and $62,200.
  • Texas: $23,624 at 7 kW and $23,062 at 9 kW, nearly level, then 12 kW drops to $12,562.

So across these runs, the biggest gain usually sits at or near the size that just covers annual use. Going bigger gives back less, not more.

What size is "just right" where I live?

The size that produces 10,500 kWh a year differs a lot by location. Offset means the share of your yearly use that solar covers, and these sizes are roughly where production equals use, or about 100% offset.

State kW needed
Arizona 6 kW
California 6.3 kW
Florida 6.8 kW
Texas 7.4 kW
Illinois 8 kW
New York 8.1 kW
Massachusetts 8.1 kW
Washington 9.7 kW

Sunnier states need fewer panels for the same output. Washington needs the most on this list. Sizing at or a little under these numbers keeps you from paying for uncredited kWh. To turn kW into a panel count, see how many solar panels do I need.

Should I size up for the future, or for panel aging?

Consider three forces.

First, panels age. The model has them losing 0.5%/yr, so production shrinks over time. A system sized right to the edge of the cap wastes little in the early years.

Second, the cap is a hard wall in this model. Production above your annual purchases earns nothing, so the last panels added have no payoff.

Third, your usage may change. Adding an EV or a heat pump can justify a slightly larger system. The solar and EV charging guide shows how the right size grows with a car, and the high-usage homes guide covers bigger households. Size for it only when the plan is firm.

Treat any "best size" as an estimate. It moves with your rate plan, your roof and your utility's rules.

What should I ask my installer and utility?

  • Does my utility credit extra kWh at the end of the year, and at what rate?
  • Is my plan net metering or net billing? Does the credit change by time of day?
  • Is there a size limit tied to my past usage?
  • Will my usage change in the next few years?

Net metering rules, including what happens to leftover credits, are tracked by state in the DSIRE database. Your utility's tariff has the final word.

If you remember one thing

Bigger is not automatically better. Once production passes your annual grid purchases, extra panels add cost without adding savings, and payback gets longer.

Aim for a system near your yearly use, and adjust only for firm changes such as an EV. Test different sizes with your own numbers in the solar calculator, check your state's rate on electricity prices, and ask your utility how it treats extra production before you buy.
