Can a Solar System Be Too Big? Annual True-Up Explained

Past a year of your use, extra panels make kWh nobody pays for. See uncredited kWh and payback by size in four states, and what real true-up rules do.

Published October 20, 2026·Updated November 28, 2026·5 min read·RoofPayback editorial·Figures checked against EIA and PVWatts data

Official data: EIA · DOE · IRS · how we check

The answer

Yes. Under net metering with an annual true-up, credits for exported power can offset up to a year of what you buy from the grid, and usually no more. Once a system makes more than that, the extra kilowatt-hours earn little or nothing, but the panels that make them still cost money. In our model, payback gets longer with every kW past that point, and the 25-year gain shrinks.

Under net billing, where exports earn less than retail, the limit rarely bites, but oversizing still slows payback for a different reason.

What is an annual true-up?

Many crediting programs keep a running balance through the year: sunny months build credits, darker months use them. Once a year the utility settles the account. That settlement is the true-up, and what happens to leftover credits at that moment is what makes a system "too big." Three rules we checked in official sources:

  • California (CPUC): under the Net Billing Tariff, billing is monthly with an annual true-up, and credits roll over for 12 months. For customers still on net metering, surplus kWh at the true-up earn net surplus compensation, which the CPUC puts at "approximately $0.02 to $0.03 per kWh."
  • Washington (RCW 80.60.030): excess generation is credited to the next bill, and unused credits from the previous year go to the utility on March 31, with no payment to the customer.
  • Hawaii (Hawaiian Electric's Smart Renewable Energy Export): credits are reconciled annually, and credits left at the end of the year expire.

Our model takes the strict version: credits can offset up to a year of grid purchases, and anything beyond that is worth nothing. For other utilities, the tariff and DSIRE have the details.

When does a system hit the limit?

When first-year savings stop rising as the system grows. Our example home uses 10,500 kWh a year, 40% of it while the sun is up, with full retail credit for exports, $3,500 per kW and September 2026 EIA prices. In Houston:

System Cost Year-1 savings kWh made but never credited, year 1 Payback 25-year gain
3 kW $10,500 $678 0 15.3 years $8,696
5 kW $17,500 $1,131 0 14.6 years $16,160
7 kW $24,500 $1,583 0 14.3 years $23,624
9 kW $31,500 $1,671 2,293 16.3 years $23,062
12 kW $42,000 $1,671 6,557 20.5 years $12,562

Savings stop at $1,671 somewhere between 7 and 9 kW. Going from 9 to 12 kW adds $10,500 of panels and 4,264 more uncredited kWh, and cuts the 25-year gain by $10,500. Each panel past the limit is pure cost.

In all three, 7 kW already makes more than the house can get credit for, so savings are flat from 7 kW up. A 12 kW system in Phoenix makes 21,060 kWh in its first year, half of which is never credited. These California rows assume full retail credit, which only homes already on net metering get; see below for new systems.

The size that just covers yearly use is roughly your annual kWh divided by local output per kW. How many solar panels do I need does that calculation for different states and homes.

What changes under net billing?

When exports earn a fraction of retail, the yearly limit on credits is much harder to reach, because each exported kWh is worth less against the same grid purchases. So the waste in the tables above mostly disappears, but the economics of extra panels do not improve. California at a quarter of retail, our working assumption for its Net Billing Tariff:

System Year-1 savings Payback 25-year gain
5 kW $1,619 10.3 years $33,893
7 kW $1,878 12.1 years $35,169
9 kW $2,137 13.4 years $36,444
12 kW $2,525 14.8 years $38,357

Every kW is credited, but each added one earns only the low export rate on most of its output. Payback lengthens by 1.3 to 1.8 years at each step up, while the 25-year gain barely moves. Under net billing the better lever is using more of the output at home, covered in solar and daytime electricity use.

Should I size up for the future?

Only for changes you are sure of. An electric car or a heat pump raises yearly use and can justify a bigger system; the solar and electric car charging guide runs that case. Panel aging is not a reason to oversize: in our model output falls 0.5% a year, so a system sized to today's use still covers about 89% of it in year 25.

What to ask before you sign

  • When is my true-up, and what happens to credits left over at that point?
  • Are leftover kWh paid for, and at what rate?
  • Is there a limit on system size tied to my past use?
  • Does the quote size the system to my last 12 months of bills, or to the roof?
