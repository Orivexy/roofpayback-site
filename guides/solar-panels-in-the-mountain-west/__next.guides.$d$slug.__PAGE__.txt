22:T11eb,<div class="short-answer"><p class="short-answer-label">At a glance</p>
<p>The eight states of the Census Bureau&#39;s Mountain division have strong sun and power prices below the national average; the division averaged 15.17¢ per kWh in September 2026, against 18.17¢ for the U.S. With full export credit, our example system pays back in 11.9 years in Colorado and 16.1 in Utah.</p>
<p>Two things set the region apart. In the sunny south, a typical home needs a smaller system than our example, so the standard figures understate how well solar can do there. In the north, summer produces so much more than winter that the way credits are settled matters more than anywhere outside the Pacific Northwest.</p>

31:Tc8e,<h2 id="the-north-winter-and-how-credits-are-settled">The north: winter and how credits are settled</h2>
<p>Boise and Billings have some of the widest seasonal swings in the country, with summer-to-winter output ratios of 2.8 and 2.3; Salt Lake City is at 2.1. Our model normally lets credits from summer offset bills for up to a year. If a utility instead settles each month and forfeits the excess, the summer surplus loses much of its value. Run that way, the example&#39;s payback grows from 15.4 to 17.4 years in Idaho and from 15.3 to 16.2 years in Montana, the two largest effects among the 11 states in our winter comparison. Utah, which that comparison left out, goes from 16.1 to 17.6 years when run the same way. <a href="/guides/solar-panels-in-winter-by-state/">Solar panels in winter by state</a> has the full comparison.</p>
<h2 id="how-exports-are-credited">How exports are credited</h2>
<p>Colorado is the state where we could confirm a current rule from an official source. Senate Bill 21-261, signed on June 21, 2021, &quot;allows a customer to carry forward monthly bill credits from distributed generation indefinitely,&quot; within a qualifying retail utility&#39;s territory, unless the customer chooses annual reimbursement or donates the excess to a low-income energy assistance program. That settles how long credits last; it does not tell you what each exported kWh is worth, so ask your utility for that too.</p>
<p>For Arizona, Idaho, Montana, Nevada, New Mexico, Utah and Wyoming, we could not verify the current residential rules from the regulators&#39; own pages for this update, so do not assume the full-credit column applies. Ask your utility what an exported kWh earns and when credits are settled, and check its tariff with your state utility commission. <a href="/guides/net-metering-vs-net-billing-payback/">Net metering vs. net billing</a> explains the arrangements.</p>
<h2 id="price-trends">Price trends</h2>
<p>Comparing 12-month averages ending September 2017 and September 2026 in EIA&#39;s utility data, residential prices rose about 3.6% a year in Colorado, 2.8% in Idaho and Montana, 2.7% in Arizona, 2.4% in Wyoming, 2.1% in Utah, 2% in New Mexico and 1.6% in Nevada, before inflation. Half the region sits below the 2.5% yearly increase our model assumes. <a href="/guides/solar-payback-if-electricity-prices-rise/">Solar payback if electricity prices rise</a> shows what a slower path does to payback.</p>
<h2 id="before-you-take-the-numbers-home">Before you take the numbers home</h2>
<ol>
<li>Size from 12 months of bills, especially in the sunny south.</li>
<li>Ask how exports are credited and whether credits are settled monthly or yearly.</li>
<li>Compare quotes on price per watt; at today&#39;s prices each 50 cents per watt moves payback by roughly two years in Nevada and Utah. See <a href="/guides/is-solar-worth-it-where-electricity-is-cheap/">solar where electricity is cheap</a>.</li>
