22:Tb4a,<div class="short-answer"><h2 id="at-a-glance">At a glance</h2>
<p>The eight Mountain West states have no shortage of sun. What they lack is expensive electricity: every one of them sits below the U.S. average price. Each <a href="/guides/what-is-a-kilowatt-hour/">kilowatt-hour</a> (kWh) that panels make there saves less money than it would where power costs more.</p>
<p>With full credit for exported power, our example payback runs from 12.4 years in Colorado to 16.4 years in Idaho. Colorado, New Mexico and Arizona do best; Idaho, Utah and Nevada are slowest, and Nevada shows how little sunshine alone can do.</p>
<p>The figures come from our <a href="/methodology/">example home</a>, with PVWatts production for one reference city per state and EIA August 2026 residential prices. They count no incentives; the federal 30% residential credit no longer covers systems placed in service after 2025, according to the IRS, and state or local programs vary.</p>
</div><h2 id="wyoming-the-northern-front-runner">Wyoming: the northern front-runner</h2>
<p>Cheyenne is a strong solar spot for the region. It produces 1,533 kWh per kW of panels per year. Power costs 14.43¢/kWh, barely up from 14.33¢ a year earlier.</p>
<p>That mix gives Wyoming the shortest payback of the three northern states. In the example, year-1 savings with full credit reach $1,515, and payback lands at 14.7 years.</p>
<p>Export rules change the story quickly, though:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Full credit:</strong> 14.7 years.</li>
<li><strong>Half credit:</strong> 20 years.</li>
<li><strong>Quarter credit:</strong> 24.3 years.</li>
</ul>
<p>Wyoming also has the steadiest output through the year of the three. Its summer-to-winter output ratio is 1.6, the smallest of the three, so winter months give up less ground to summer. For local detail, see the <a href="/solar-panels/wyoming/">Wyoming solar page</a> and <a href="/electricity-prices/wyoming/">Wyoming electricity prices</a>.</p>
<h2 id="montana-rising-prices-long-winters">Montana: rising prices, long winters</h2>
<p>Billings has the weakest output in this group, at 1,344 kWh per kW per year. What works in Montana&#39;s favor is price. Rates climbed 9% in a year, to 15.57¢ from 14.29¢, the highest price of the three northern states.</p>
<p>Higher prices help, but example payback is still 15.4 years with full credit.</p>
<p>The bigger hurdle is the season. Montana&#39;s summer-to-winter ratio is 2.3. A kW of panels in July produces 166 kWh. In January the same panels make only 65 kWh. Households that heat with electricity, or simply use more power in the dark months, may lean on the grid in winter even with a system sized for the whole year.</p>
<p>Living in Missoula or another part of the state? Reference-city output may differ from yours. Check <a href="/solar-panels/montana/">Montana solar</a> and your own utility&#39;s rules.</p>

30:T17a7,<h2 id="idaho-low-prices-sharpest-seasons">Idaho: low prices, sharpest seasons</h2>
<p>Boise gets 1,456 kWh per kW, better than Billings. The price is the problem. At 13.37¢/kWh, Idaho has one of the lowest rates in the region, even after a 10.7% rise from 12.08¢ a year earlier.</p>
<p>That low price makes Idaho the slowest of the three:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Full credit:</strong> 16.4 years.</li>
<li><strong>Half credit:</strong> 22 years.</li>
<li><strong>Quarter credit:</strong> the example does not pay back within 25 years.</li>
</ul>
<p>Seasons hit Idaho hardest too. Its summer-to-winter output ratio is 2.8, the biggest of the three. Summer does most of the work. Homeowners around Meridian or Boise can find more on <a href="/solar-panels/idaho/">Idaho solar</a>.</p>
<h2 id="colorado-and-new-mexico-where-the-region-does-best">Colorado and New Mexico: where the region does best</h2>
<p>Head south and the numbers improve. Denver produces 1,595 kWh per kW, and Colorado&#39;s price of 17.17¢/kWh is the closest in the region to the U.S. average of 18.16¢. Payback with full credit drops to 12.4 years.</p>
<p>Albuquerque pairs some of the strongest output in the region, 1,779 kWh per kW, with a price of 16.51¢/kWh. New Mexico&#39;s full-credit payback is 12.8 years.</p>
<p>Neither state has expensive power by national standards. They simply combine good sun with prices that aren&#39;t as low as their northern neighbors&#39;.</p>
<h2 id="utah-nevada-and-arizona-sun-rich-price-poor">Utah, Nevada and Arizona: sun-rich, price-poor</h2>
<p>This southern stretch shows that sunshine alone doesn&#39;t set payback.</p>
<p>Las Vegas produces 1,753 kWh per kW, far more than Cheyenne. Nevada&#39;s price, though, is only 12.72¢/kWh, the lowest in the table. The result is a 16.1-year payback, longer than Wyoming&#39;s 14.7 years despite much more sun.</p>
<p>Utah follows the same pattern. Salt Lake City makes 1,484 kWh per kW at 13.31¢/kWh, for 16.2 years.</p>
<p>Arizona lands between the two groups. Phoenix produces 1,755 kWh per kW at 15.05¢/kWh, and payback comes in at 13.9 years. For more on how far low prices can drag down a sunny state, see <a href="/guides/is-solar-worth-it-where-electricity-is-cheap/">is solar worth it where electricity is cheap</a>.</p>

<h2 id="before-you-take-the-numbers-home">Before you take the numbers home</h2>
<p><strong>Your quote may differ from ours.</strong> The example uses $3,500 per kW. Savings equal the kWh you offset times your rate. A low rate shrinks the savings while system cost stays about the same, so the quote carries extra weight here. <a href="/guides/solar-panel-cost-by-state/">Solar panel cost by state</a> shows how payback moves with the price per watt.</p>
<p><strong>Your utility&#39;s export rules matter.</strong> Some utilities credit exported power at less than the retail rate. The Wyoming and Idaho figures above show how fast payback stretches at half and quarter credit. <a href="/guides/net-metering-vs-net-billing-payback/">Net metering vs. net billing</a> explains the difference, and DSIRE keeps a database of current programs by state.</p>
<p><strong>Winter use matters in the north.</strong> A large summer-to-winter ratio means summer makes most of your power. Our <a href="/guides/solar-panels-in-winter-by-state/">winter solar guide</a> goes deeper.</p>
<p>A practical sequence:</p>
<ol>
<li>Find your actual rate on your bill and compare it with the averages above.</li>
<li>Ask your utility how it credits exported power.</li>
<li>Get several quotes and compare the price per watt.</li>
<li>Run your numbers in the <a href="/solar-calculator/">solar calculator</a>.</li>
<li>Check <a href="/electricity-prices/">electricity prices</a> and the <a href="/guides/solar-payback-period-explained/">payback guide</a>.</li>
</ol>
<h2 id="the-pattern-across-the-region">The pattern across the region</h2>
<p>Good sun is common across the Mountain West. Cheap power is too, and the two pull in opposite directions.</p>
<p>In the north, Wyoming leads at 14.7 years in our example, followed by Montana at 15.4 and Idaho at 16.4. Long, dark winters widen the gap between summer and winter output, especially in Idaho and Montana.</p>
<p>In the south, more sun does not buy a faster payback on its own. Nevada and Utah, with some of the lowest prices, trail Wyoming. Colorado and New Mexico come out ahead because their prices are the highest in the region, and their output is strong too.</p>
<p>Sort the table by price and the two fastest states are also the two most expensive; sunshine only reshuffles the rest. After price, the export rule is the next filter: in Wyoming it is the difference between 14.7 years and 24.3, and in Idaho, between 16.4 years and no payback within 25 years. Anywhere in the Mountain West, those two numbers from your utility tell you more than the sky does.</p>
