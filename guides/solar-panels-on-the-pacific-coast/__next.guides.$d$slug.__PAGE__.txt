23:Tb7e,<div class="short-answer"><h2 id="short-answer">Short answer</h2>
<p>The Pacific states are not one solar market. Hawaii and California pair high electricity prices with strong sun, so the example system pays back fastest there. Oregon and Washington pair modest prices with cloudier skies, so payback stretches out. Alaska sits in between: low sun, but a high price per kilowatt-hour (kWh, the unit your utility bills you for).</p>
<p>Everything below uses the same example home and the same method, so the five states can be compared fairly.</p>
</div><h2 id="why-the-west-coast-splits-in-two">Why the West Coast splits in two</h2>
<p>Look at the August 2026 averages from the U.S. Energy Information Administration (EIA). The Pacific Contiguous division, which covers California, Oregon and Washington, averaged 25.59¢/kWh for residential customers, up 0.7% from 25.42¢ a year earlier. The Pacific Noncontiguous division, Alaska and Hawaii, averaged 40.79¢/kWh, up 17.5% from 34.72¢.</p>
<p>Those regional averages hide a wide gap inside the contiguous group. California sits at 29.75¢. Oregon is at 15.92¢ and Washington at 14.63¢. Two of the three &quot;coastal&quot; states are actually cheaper than the national average of 18.16¢.</p>
<p>That one fact explains most of what follows. Solar saves you the price of the power you stop buying, so the price matters about as much as the sunshine.</p>

37:T13dc,<h2 id="hawaii-and-california-price-does-the-heavy-lifting">Hawaii and California: price does the heavy lifting</h2>
<p>Hawaii&#39;s 47.18¢/kWh is the highest in the country in this data, and it is rising fast: up 22.5% from 38.53¢ a year earlier. Our separate guide on <a href="/guides/why-is-electricity-expensive-in-hawaii/">why electricity is so expensive in Hawaii</a> covers the causes. For solar, the effect is simple. Each kWh the panels make replaces a very expensive kWh from the grid.</p>
<p>California makes more power per kW than Oregon or Washington, but its real advantage is price. At 29.75¢/kWh, the example payback is 7.4 years with full credit. Even with only a quarter credit for exports, it is 12.5 years. California&#39;s rate rules have changed how exports are credited, which is why the half-credit and quarter-credit columns matter. Our guide on <a href="/guides/net-metering-vs-net-billing-payback/">net metering vs net billing</a> explains the difference, and the <a href="/guides/is-solar-worth-it-in-california/">California solar guide</a> goes deeper.</p>
<p>California&#39;s price did slip 3.8% from 30.92¢ a year earlier. A falling price lengthens payback slightly, so it is worth watching rather than assuming.</p>
<h2 id="alaska-weak-sun-strong-price">Alaska: weak sun, strong price</h2>
<p>Anchorage&#39;s reference output is only 922 kWh per kW per year, the lowest of the five. A surprising result is that the payback is still 12.1 years with full credit. The reason is the 29.41¢/kWh price, up 5.8% from 27.79¢ a year earlier.</p>
<p>Alaska is also where local details matter most. Snow cover, very long summer days and very short winter days make a single yearly number a rough guide. Treat the figure as a starting point for quotes, not a forecast.</p>
<h2 id="oregon-and-washington-cheaper-power-longer-wait">Oregon and Washington: cheaper power, longer wait</h2>
<p>Oregon&#39;s price is 15.92¢/kWh, up 0.4% from a year earlier. Washington&#39;s is 14.63¢/kWh, up 7.2% from 13.65¢. Combined with the lowest sun in the table, the example paybacks are 17.6 years for Oregon and 19.6 years for Washington with full credit.</p>
<p>With half credit, Washington reaches 24.6 years, right at the edge of the 25-year horizon. With a quarter credit, neither state pays back inside 25 years in this model.</p>
<p>That does not mean solar is a bad idea there. It means the decision rests on things the example leaves out: your own quote, financing, any incentives you qualify for, and how your utility credits exports. The <a href="https://www.irs.gov/credits-deductions/residential-clean-energy-credit">federal Residential Clean Energy Credit</a> is no longer available for property placed in service after December 31, 2025, so check the DSIRE database of state and utility programs at <a href="https://programs.dsireusa.org/">programs.dsireusa.org</a>. Our guide to <a href="/guides/is-solar-worth-it-without-tax-credit/">solar without the tax credit</a> shows how the math looks with no incentives at all.</p>
<h2 id="what-a-typical-bill-looks-like">What a typical bill looks like</h2>
<p>At 900 kWh a month and each state&#39;s average price, EIA-based figures come to roughly $268 in California, $265 in Alaska, $425 in Hawaii, $143 in Oregon and $132 in Washington. These are averages. EIA prices include taxes and fees, and your real bill depends on your utility, rate plan and usage.</p>
<p>Notice that Alaska&#39;s bill is close to California&#39;s despite far less sun. That is the same price effect again. For more on how rate plans change your savings, see <a href="/guides/time-of-use-electricity-rates-explained/">time-of-use electricity rates explained</a>.</p>
<h2 id="how-to-check-your-own-numbers">How to check your own numbers</h2>
<ol>
<li>Find your actual rate on your bill and compare it with the state averages on our <a href="/electricity-prices/">electricity prices</a> page.</li>
<li>Run your own roof through the <a href="/solar-calculator/">solar calculator</a>.</li>
<li>Ask each installer how exported power is credited, and get that in writing.</li>
<li>Compare cash and loan pricing using our <a href="/guides/solar-loan-vs-cash/">solar loan vs cash</a> guide.</li>
<li>Remember that quotes vary. Berkeley Lab reports a median installed price of $4.0 per watt for host-owned residential systems installed in 2024, with a 20th to 80th percentile range of $3.0 to $5.2 per watt, before incentives. The example above uses $3.5 per watt.</li>
</ol>
<h2 id="bottom-line">Bottom line</h2>
<p>Along the Pacific, electricity price beats sunshine. Hawaii and California have the shortest example paybacks because grid power there is expensive. Alaska surprises on the upside for the same reason. Oregon and Washington have the longest paybacks in this group because their power is cheaper and their skies are cloudier.</p>
<p>Use these numbers to decide whether getting quotes is worth your time, then let real quotes and your utility&#39;s export rules make the final call. These are illustrations, not guarantees.</p>
