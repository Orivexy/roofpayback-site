22:Tf04,<div class="short-answer"><h2 id="short-version">Short version</h2>
<p>California, Oregon and Washington share a coastline but not a solar market. California pairs high electricity prices with strong sun, so the example system pays back in 7.4 years there. Oregon and Washington pair prices below the national average with cloudier skies and dark winters, so the same system takes 17.6 and 19.6 years.</p>
<p>Price is the first thing behind that gap: solar saves you the price of each <a href="/guides/what-is-a-kilowatt-hour/">kilowatt-hour</a> you stop buying. How your utility credits exported power comes second, and in the Northwest it can decide whether a system pays back inside 25 years at all. Alaska and Hawaii, the other two Pacific states, are covered in <a href="/guides/solar-in-alaska-and-hawaii-compared/">solar in Alaska vs. Hawaii</a>.</p>
</div><h2 id="before-you-ask-for-quotes">Before you ask for quotes</h2>
<p>Get three things straight first. They make every quote easier to judge.</p>
<p><strong>Your real rate.</strong> Find the price per kWh on your bill and compare it with the state averages on our <a href="/electricity-prices/">electricity prices</a> page. In August 2026, the U.S. Energy Information Administration (EIA) put the Pacific Contiguous division, which is these three states, at 25.59¢/kWh for homes, up 0.7% from 25.42¢.</p>
<p><strong>Which half of the coast you live in.</strong> That regional average hides a split. California sits at 29.75¢. Oregon is at 15.92¢ and Washington at 14.63¢, both below the national average of 18.16¢. A Portland or Seattle homeowner should judge quotes against the Northwest figures below, not against California results.</p>
<p><strong>Which way prices are heading.</strong> Washington&#39;s price rose 7.2% in a year, from 13.65¢. Oregon&#39;s barely moved, up 0.4% from 15.85¢. California&#39;s slipped 3.8% from 30.92¢. A falling price lengthens payback slightly, so don&#39;t assume California rates only climb.</p>
<p>Then run your roof through the <a href="/solar-calculator/">solar calculator</a>, so you walk into the conversation with your own baseline.</p>
<h2 id="questions-to-ask-the-installer">Questions to ask the installer</h2>
<ul>
<li><strong>How does my utility credit exported power?</strong> Get the answer in writing. In this example, moving from full to half credit adds more than two years to payback in California and about five in Oregon and Washington.</li>
<li><strong>What production estimate did you use, and from which tool?</strong> Ask how it accounts for your roof, shade and local weather. PVWatts is the public benchmark our <a href="/methodology/">methodology</a> uses.</li>
<li><strong>What does the estimate assume for December and January?</strong> In Portland and Seattle, winter output is a small fraction of summer output (see the numbers below). A single yearly figure hides that.</li>
<li><strong>What is the installed price per watt?</strong> Compare it with the national ranges in our guide to <a href="/guides/solar-panel-cost-by-state/">solar panel cost by state</a>.</li>
<li><strong>What do cash and loan prices look like?</strong> Our <a href="/guides/solar-loan-vs-cash/">solar loan vs. cash</a> guide shows how to compare them.</li>
<li><strong>Which incentives do you assume?</strong> The <a href="https://www.irs.gov/credits-deductions/residential-clean-energy-credit">federal Residential Clean Energy Credit</a> is no longer available for property placed in service after December 31, 2025. State and utility programs are listed on <a href="https://programs.dsireusa.org/">DSIRE</a>.</li>
<li><strong>In California: which export tariff does the savings estimate use?</strong> Ask the utility to confirm it. The half-credit and quarter-credit cases below show how much rides on that answer.</li>
</ul>

30:T1287,<h2 id="red-flags">Red flags</h2>
<ul>
<li><strong>A quote that subtracts the expired federal credit.</strong> It no longer applies to new systems.</li>
<li><strong>Vague answers on export credit.</strong> In Oregon and Washington, the credit rule can decide whether the system pays back within the example horizon at all.</li>

<li><strong>A payback figure with no assumptions behind it.</strong> Ask for the production tool, the rate and the export rule it used.</li>

<h3>California: price does most of the work</h3>
<p>Los Angeles makes more power per kW than either Northwest city, but the bigger edge is price. Even if exports earn only a quarter of the retail rate, the example payback is 12.5 years, shorter than Oregon or Washington manage at full credit. Seasons are mild by comparison: summer output is about 1.6 times winter output, so a dark month rarely sinks the year. The <a href="/guides/is-solar-worth-it-in-california/">California solar guide</a> goes deeper on the state&#39;s rates and rules.</p>
<h3>Oregon: the weakest January of the three</h3>
<p>Portland&#39;s panels make less in January than Los Angeles or Seattle, and its summer output runs 3.2 times its winter output. That pattern makes the export rule decisive. With an annual true-up, summer surplus covers part of the winter bills. With half credit, payback stretches to 22.4 years, and with a quarter credit it falls outside 25 years in this model.</p>
<h3>Washington: cheap power and short days</h3>
<p>Seattle has the lowest output of the three at 1,088 kWh per kW, a summer-to-winter ratio of 3.3, and the lowest price. At half credit the example reaches 24.6 years, right at the edge of the 25-year horizon. A home using 10,500 kWh a year would need about 9.7 kW to cover it in Washington, against 6.3 kW in California, so roof space runs out sooner. That does not rule solar out, but it puts your quote, financing and export terms in charge of the answer. See <a href="/guides/is-solar-worth-it-where-electricity-is-cheap/">solar where electricity is cheap</a> and <a href="/guides/cloudy-states-solar-worth-it/">solar in cloudy states</a> for how those cases play out.</p>
<p><strong>Typical bills.</strong> At 900 kWh a month and each state&#39;s average price, EIA-based figures come to roughly $268 in California, $143 in Oregon and $132 in Washington. EIA averages include taxes and fees; your bill depends on utility, plan and usage. Rate plans matter too: see <a href="/guides/time-of-use-electricity-rates-explained/">time-of-use electricity rates explained</a>. For winter output elsewhere, see <a href="/guides/solar-panels-in-winter-by-state/">solar panels in winter by state</a>.</p>
<p>These are illustrations for one reference city per state; your roof, shade and utility will move them.</p>
<h2 id="decide-with-confidence">Decide with confidence</h2>
<p>In California, the question is less whether solar pays than which export tariff and price you get, so spend your effort on the credit terms and on comparing bids. In Oregon and Washington, payback sits in the back half of the 25-year horizon we model, so a quote is worth pursuing only if it shows local winter production, a price per watt you can justify, and an export credit your utility confirms in writing. If any of those three is missing, expect the system to struggle to earn back its cost.</p>
