22:Tad0,<div class="short-answer"><p class="short-answer-label">Short version</p>
<p>The three states share a coastline and almost nothing else that matters for solar. California has expensive power and strong sun, but new customers of its large utilities are on net billing, which credits exports below retail. Oregon and Washington charge less than the national average for power and get the weakest sunshine in the lower 48, and Washington, at least, still requires net metering.</p>
<p>Sized for each state&#39;s average household, our model gives:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>California:</strong> about 12.4 years for a 4 kW system under net billing.</li>
<li><strong>Oregon:</strong> about 17.6 years for a 9 kW system, assuming full retail credit.</li>
<li><strong>Washington:</strong> about 18.9 years for a 10 kW system under net metering.</li>
</ul>
<p>Alaska and Hawaii, the other two Pacific states, are compared in <a href="/guides/solar-in-alaska-and-hawaii-compared/">solar in Alaska vs. Hawaii</a>.</p>

<h2 id="california-net-billing-and-small-systems">California: net billing and small systems</h2>
<p>The CPUC applies the Net Billing Tariff to PG&amp;E, SCE and SDG&amp;E customers who submit an interconnection application on or after April 15, 2023. Exports earn credits based on Avoided Cost Calculator values rather than the retail rate. Because a typical California home is small, the best response is usually a system sized to daytime use. <a href="/guides/is-solar-worth-it-in-california/">Is solar worth it in California</a> runs 2 to 5 kW systems and the effect of shifting use into daylight hours.</p>
<h2 id="washington-net-metering-with-two-dates-to-know">Washington: net metering, with two dates to know</h2>
<p>Washington law (RCW 80.60.020) requires utilities to offer net metering until June 30, 2029, or until net-metered capacity reaches 4% of the utility&#39;s 1996 peak demand, whichever comes first. Under RCW 80.60.030, excess generation is credited to the next bill, and unused credits from the previous year go to the utility on March 31, with no payment to the customer.</p>
<p>That March 31 date suits Northwest weather. Credits built up from spring through summer can be spent on the dark months from October to March before the reset. A system sized to produce close to your yearly use loses little at the true-up.</p>
<p>The statute we read sets the 2029 date for offering net metering; it does not tell you how your utility will treat existing customers after that. Ask before you sign, because at half credit the 10 kW Washington system above no longer pays back within 25 years.</p>
<h2 id="oregon-check-the-crediting-terms-first">Oregon: check the crediting terms first</h2>
<p>With the weakest January output of the three and a price of 15.73¢, Oregon&#39;s result swings from 17.6 years to more than 25 depending on how exports are credited. Ask your utility how it credits excess generation, when the annual settlement falls and what happens to leftover credits. DSIRE lists programs by state and utility.</p>
<h2 id="questions-for-any-pacific-coast-quote">Questions for any Pacific coast quote</h2>
<ul>
<li>Which crediting arrangement does the savings estimate assume, and when does it settle?</li>
<li>What does the estimate show for December and January, not just the yearly total?</li>
<li>How does the system size compare with my last 12 months of use?</li>
<li>What is the price per watt? <a href="/guides/solar-panel-cost-by-state/">Solar panel cost per watt</a> shows the most you can pay in each state for a given payback.</li>
</ul>
<h2 id="where-to-spend-your-effort">Where to spend your effort</h2>
<p>In California, settle the system size and how much of your use falls in daylight hours. In Washington, the decision rests on the installed price and on how long net metering lasts for you. In Oregon, get the crediting terms before anything else. Then run your own bill through the <a href="/solar-calculator/">solar calculator</a>.</p>
