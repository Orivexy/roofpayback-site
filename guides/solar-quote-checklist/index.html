Solar Quote Checklist: What to Verify Before You Sign

Check price per watt, yearly kWh and your utility's export credit before signing a solar quote. The cash median is $3.50 per watt; loans run $4.70.

Published November 17, 2026·Updated November 18, 2026·5 min read·RoofPayback editorial·Figures checked against EIA and PVWatts data

Short version

Check three things on any solar quote: the price per watt, the production estimate, and how your utility credits the power you send back. A quote of $3.50 per watt matches the cash-purchase median Berkeley Lab reports for 2024 installs; loan-financed systems had a median of $4.70. If the installer can't show how it got its yearly kWh figure or name your credit rule, keep shopping.

Before you ask for quotes

Do some homework first, so you can tell a fair quote from a flattering one.

Pull twelve months of bills and add up the kilowatt-hours. Your usage sets how big a system makes sense, and the solar calculator lets you test a system size against it. Then find the price you pay per kWh. The U.S. average residential price was 18.16¢ in August 2026, but your own rate matters far more than the average. Your state's page on electricity prices shows where you stand.

That rate is the first thing that decides how fast solar pays back. The second is how your utility pays for the extra power your panels send to the grid, and it is easy to miss. Where exports earn only a fraction of the retail price, the credit rule can matter more than a high rate. The table further down shows how far full, half and quarter credit pull the same system apart.

Finally, decide whether you would pay cash or borrow. The two routes produce different prices for the same roof. Our loan vs. cash guide covers the trade-off.

Questions to ask the installer

Ask these in writing, and compare the answers across at least three quotes.

  • What is the total price, and what is it per watt after dividing by system size?
  • Does that price change if I pay with a loan? Berkeley Lab found loan-financed systems had a median of $4.70 per watt versus $3.50 for cash.
  • What yearly kWh do you expect, and which tool produced the number? PVWatts is the common public benchmark, and our methodology shows how we use it.
  • What roof pitch, direction and shading did you assume? Our guide on roof pitch shows why this matters.
  • What does my utility pay for exported power, and is that rate locked or can it change?
  • Which incentives are you counting? The IRS says the federal 30% residential credit (Section 25D) does not apply to property placed in service after December 31, 2025, so a quote that still includes it needs a very good explanation.

Berkeley Lab's national numbers give you a yardstick: a median of $4.00 per watt for host-owned systems installed in 2024, with the 20th to 80th percentile running from $3.00 to $5.20. A quote outside that band isn't automatically wrong, but the installer should be able to say why. Our guide to solar panel cost by state shows what each price level does to payback in different states.

Red flags

Watch for these warning signs before you sign anything.

  • A savings promise with no assumptions attached, such as a rate or an export rule.
  • Pressure to sign today, or a discount that "expires" at the end of the visit.
  • A production estimate with no tool named, or one that ignores shade.
  • A net price that quietly subtracts the federal credit.
  • No mention of what happens to your bill when the sun is down. Our guide on daytime electricity use explains why this gap shapes real savings.
  • Vague language about incentives. Programs vary by state and utility, so ask for the program name and look it up at DSIRE.

One more tell: a quote that never says what the system costs per watt. Without it you can't compare offers, and that is sometimes the point.

Numbers to check yourself

You don't need a spreadsheet for a first pass. Divide the price by the first-year savings the installer projects. That gives a rough simple payback: it leaves out rising electricity prices, which shorten payback, and the slow loss of panel output, which lengthens it. Use it only to spot a quote that is far off.

For a fuller comparison, the paybacks below come from our example home, a 7 kW system at $3,500 per kW with no incentives (setup on the methodology page). Each row shows the same system under three export rules.

State (city) Price Full credit Half credit Quarter credit
California (Los Angeles) 29.75¢/kWh 7.4 years 9.8 years 12.5 years
New York (New York City) 28.65¢/kWh 9 years 12 years 14.3 years
Ohio (Columbus) 19.38¢/kWh 12.9 years 17 years 20.1 years
Texas (Houston) 15.91¢/kWh 14.3 years 19.2 years 23.1 years
Arkansas (Little Rock) 14.37¢/kWh 15.8 years 21.1 years not within 25 years

Read down the full-credit column first. Payback lengthens as the rate falls, from 7.4 years in Los Angeles to 15.8 years in Little Rock, because price per kWh is the first thing that decides payback.

Then read across a row. In Ohio, going from full to quarter credit moves payback from 12.9 years to 20.1 years. In Arkansas, a quarter credit pushes payback past the 25-year horizon. Notice too that New York at quarter credit (14.3 years) lands where Texas sits at full credit: a weak export rule can cancel much of a high rate's advantage. Net metering vs. net billing explains the rules behind those columns.

Now set your quote against the row closest to your rate and credit. If your state has its own page under solar panels by state, use its numbers instead. If an installer quotes a payback much shorter than the table suggests for your price level, ask which assumption differs: a higher rate, a better credit, an incentive, or a rosier production estimate.

Watch the decline and the price path

Panels lose output slowly, and our model assumes 0.5% a year. Electricity prices in the model rise 2.5% a year. A quote that assumes faster price growth will show a shorter payback. Our guide on payback if electricity prices rise shows how sensitive the result is.

Decide with confidence

Line up your quotes and write down four numbers for each: price per watt, yearly kWh, the export credit assumption, and the payback the installer claims. Then run your own version through the solar calculator with your utility's real rate.

A good quote survives that check with only small differences. A weak one falls apart the moment you ask where a number came from. Sign the quote whose price per watt you can place in the national band, whose production figure names its tool, and whose export credit matches what your utility confirms in writing.
