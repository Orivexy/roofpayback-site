37:T126e,<div class="short-answer"><h2 id="the-answer">The answer</h2>
<p>Yes, solar panels keep working in winter. They just make less electricity than in summer, and how much less comes down to where you live.</p>
<p>Take one kilowatt (kW) of panels. In Florida it makes 332 kWh over December, January and February, and 395 kWh over June, July and August: a summer/winter ratio of just 1.2. In Washington, the same kilowatt makes 128 kWh in winter and 417 kWh in summer, a ratio of 3.3. In Alaska the ratio reaches 6.6.</p>
<p>All figures are in <a href="/guides/what-is-a-kilowatt-hour/">kilowatt-hours</a> per kW of panels. Whether a weak winter hurts your savings depends mostly on one thing: how your utility credits the summer surplus.</p>
</div><h2 id="why-do-panels-make-less-in-winter">Why do panels make less in winter?</h2>
<p>You get less sunlight to work with. The U.S. Energy Information Administration (EIA) notes that sunlight is strongest &quot;at solar noon—on clear, cloudless days.&quot; In winter the sun sits lower and the days are shorter.</p>
<p>Where you are matters too. &quot;Locations in lower latitudes and in arid climates generally receive higher amounts of insolation than other locations,&quot; the EIA says. Insolation is just the amount of sunlight that reaches a surface over a period of time. Weather chips in: &quot;Clouds, dust, volcanic ash, and pollution in the atmosphere affect insolation levels at the surface.&quot;</p>
<p>The line that matters most for winter is this one: &quot;Seasonal (monthly) variations in solar resources increase with increasing distance from the earth&#39;s equator.&quot; Put simply, the farther north you live, the wider the gap between your summer and winter output.</p>

46:T12fa,<h2 id="are-snowy-states-the-worst-for-winter-solar">Are snowy states the worst for winter solar?</h2>
<p>Not necessarily. Minnesota, known for hard winters, has a ratio of 1.9. Its winter output of 231 kWh per kW is close to New York&#39;s 233 kWh.</p>
<p>Idaho is a useful contrast. Its summer is the strongest in the table, at 520 kWh, but its winter drops to 188 kWh, which gives it a ratio of 2.8. A big summer can make the seasonal gap look wider.</p>
<p>Snow sitting on the panels is a separate issue. Like shade and roof angle, it can make real output differ from these modeled figures.</p>
<h2 id="where-is-winter-output-weakest">Where is winter output weakest?</h2>
<p>Outside Alaska, the Pacific Northwest has the biggest gap. Oregon (3.2) and Washington (3.3) have the largest ratios in our data after Alaska. Oregon makes just 35 kWh per kW in January.</p>
<p>Alaska is in a class of its own. At 14 kWh per kW in January, winter solar there is close to nothing.</p>
<h2 id="does-a-weak-winter-hurt-my-savings">Does a weak winter hurt my savings?</h2>
<p>That turns on net metering. Net metering is an arrangement where your utility gives you credit for solar power you send to the grid. The Department of Energy says that &quot;Whether or not your solar system qualifies for net metering payments depends on policies and practices in your state and electric utility.&quot;</p>
<p>In summer, your panels often make more than you use, so you export the extra. In winter, you usually buy more than you export. Then the rules kick in:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Credits roll over to later months.</strong> Some programs let credits carry forward and settle once a year, often called an annual true-up. A dark winter hurts less, because summer surplus covers part of winter use.</li>
<li><strong>Credits are settled each month, or paid at a lower rate.</strong> If exports earn less than you pay for grid power, or do not carry over, winter bills stay higher. States with a big seasonal gap feel this most.</li>
</ul>
<p>The DOE suggests asking about &quot;availability in your service area, eligible system size and customer type, rates, and design of bill credits.&quot; Your utility can answer those, and the DSIRE database tracks net metering and incentive programs by state. Our guide to <a href="/guides/net-metering-vs-net-billing-payback/">net metering vs. net billing</a> shows how these rules change payback.</p>
<h2 id="should-i-buy-a-bigger-system-to-cover-winter">Should I buy a bigger system to cover winter?</h2>
<p>Maybe, but only under the right rules. Covering more of your winter use may take more panels, and that only makes sense if your utility credits the summer surplus fairly. Otherwise you pay for extra summer power that earns little. Our guide on <a href="/guides/how-many-solar-panels-do-i-need/">how many solar panels you need</a> walks through sizing.</p>
<p>Keep cost in view too. Every extra kW adds to the price, and in a state with a steep winter dip, panels added for December spend most of the year making surplus. <a href="/guides/solar-panel-cost-by-state/">Solar panel cost per watt and payback</a> shows how added cost stretches payback.</p>
<h2 id="where-do-these-numbers-come-from">Where do these numbers come from?</h2>
<p>Every figure comes from PVWatts v8, with panels facing south, a 20° roof pitch and 14% system losses, in one reference city per state; the <a href="/methodology/">methodology page</a> has the details. A home elsewhere in your state may see different results.</p>
<p>They describe a typical modeled year. Shade, snow cover, roof angle and your actual equipment all change the outcome. Our <a href="/solar-calculator/">solar calculator</a> uses these monthly numbers rather than just a yearly total, and its example home caps export credits at a year of grid purchases, which matches an annual true-up. If your utility settles monthly or pays less for exports, expect winter to weigh more heavily than the calculator shows.</p>
<h2 id="if-you-remember-one-thing">If you remember one thing</h2>
<p>Solar panels produce in winter, just less. In Florida, Hawaii, Arizona and New Mexico the dip is mild, with ratios from 1.2 to 1.4; in Oregon, Washington and Alaska it is steep. As the EIA notes, the farther you are from the equator, the bigger the swing.</p>
<p>Whether that swing costs you money comes down to your utility&#39;s credit rules. Annual netting lets a strong July pay for a weak January; monthly settlement or low export rates leave each winter bill to stand on its own. If you live in a high-ratio state such as Oregon or Washington, ask your utility which of the two you are on before you size a system, then run your own numbers in the <a href="/solar-calculator/">solar calculator</a> with your price from <a href="/electricity-prices/">electricity prices by state</a>.</p>
