Solar Panels in Winter: Output by State vs. Summer

Winter vs. summer solar output per kW for all 50 states and D.C., and what monthly instead of yearly credit settlement costs in 11 states.

The answer

Solar panels keep working in winter; they make less. How much less depends on where you live. One kW of panels in Orlando makes 332 kWh over December, January and February and 395 kWh over June, July and August, a summer-to-winter ratio of 1.2. In Seattle the ratio is 3.3, and in Anchorage 6.6.

Whether a weak winter costs you money depends less on the ratio than on how your utility settles credits. In our model, settling month by month instead of once a year added two years to the payback in Idaho and less than half a year in Alaska, the state with the darkest winter.

Why do panels make less in winter?

The sun is lower and the days are shorter. The U.S. Energy Information Administration (EIA) adds that "Locations in lower latitudes and in arid climates generally receive higher amounts of insolation than other locations," where insolation is the sunlight reaching a surface, and that "Clouds, dust, volcanic ash, and pollution in the atmosphere affect insolation levels at the surface." For winter, the key line is this one: "Seasonal (monthly) variations in solar resources increase with increasing distance from the earth's equator."

Are snowy states the worst?

Not by this measure. Minnesota, with a reputation for hard winters, has a ratio of 1.9 and makes 231 kWh per kW in winter, about the same as New York (233). The steepest drops are in the cloudy Pacific Northwest and Alaska, where Portland makes 35 kWh per kW in January and Anchorage 14.

Snow lying on the panels is a separate loss that these modeled figures do not include, along with shade and your exact roof angle.

Does a weak winter hurt my savings?

That depends on when your credits are settled. Under net metering or net billing, summer surplus becomes bill credit. If the utility carries credits forward and settles once a year, a sunny July can pay for a dark January. If it settles each month, credit beyond that month's bill is lost or paid at a low rate, and the winter months stand on their own.

To see the size of the difference, we ran our example home (7 kW, 10,500 kWh a year, 40% used in daylight, $3,500 per kW, no incentives, September 2026 EIA prices, full retail credit) two ways. The yearly case lets credits offset up to a year of grid purchases. The monthly case is the strictest version: each month's credit can only offset that month's bill and the rest is forfeited.

What decides the gap is not the ratio but how far summer production overshoots summer use. Boise's 7 kW system makes about as much in a year as the house uses, with a tall summer peak, so monthly settlement throws away a lot of June and July. Anchorage has the darkest winter, but a 7 kW system there makes only about 6,450 kWh a year, well short of the house's use, so its summer surplus is small. Florida loses some even with its flat year: in Orlando the example system already makes more than the house can use in a year.

Should I buy a bigger system to cover winter?

Usually not. Covering December in a state with a ratio above 2 takes a system that makes a large surplus from May to August. Under a yearly cap, the part of that surplus beyond a year's grid purchases earns nothing; under monthly settlement, even less of it counts. How many solar panels do I need shows how payback rises once a system passes 100% of use, and the annual true-up guide explains the yearly settlement.

What should I ask my utility?

  • Are credits carried forward month to month, and for how long?
  • When is the settlement date, and what happens to leftover credit then?
  • Is exported power credited at the retail rate or at a lower rate?

Net metering vs. net billing covers how the credit rate changes payback. With those answers, run your own numbers in the solar calculator, which uses the monthly profile and a yearly cap; if your utility settles monthly, expect a result closer to the second column above.

Winter output in proportion

The farther from the equator, the bigger the swing, from 1.2 in Florida to 6.6 in Alaska. The swing itself rarely decides the investment. What decides it is whether credits earned in summer survive until winter, and whether the system was sized to the year's use rather than to its darkest month.
