68:T719,<div class="short-answer"><h2 id="short-answer">Short answer</h2>
<p>Solar can be worth it in Texas, but the answer depends on your price per watt, your utility&#39;s credit for extra power, and how much you use. In our example home using Houston sunshine, payback is 14.3 years at full export credit. That is close to Florida (14.1 years) and Arizona (13.9 years), and much slower than California (7.4 years).</p>
<p>Texas has strong sun and a below-average electricity price. That mix gives a middle-of-the-pack result: good output, but modest savings per kilowatt-hour (kWh, the unit your bill uses).</p>
</div><h2 id="the-example-home-and-its-assumptions">The example home and its assumptions</h2>
<p>All numbers here come from one example. It is not a quote for your roof.</p>
<p>All figures use our standard example home: a 7 kW system costing $24,500 with no incentives, in a home using 10,500 kWh a year. Every other assumption is listed in the <a href="/methodology/">methodology</a>.</p>
<p>Production comes from PVWatts, a free solar estimator from the U.S. Department of Energy&#39;s National Laboratory of the Rockies. Prices come from the U.S. Energy Information Administration (EIA).</p>
<h2 id="what-texas-sun-and-prices-look-like">What Texas sun and prices look like</h2>
<p>For Houston, PVWatts gives 1421 kWh per kW per year. The EIA reports an average Texas residential price of 15.91¢ per kWh for August 2026, up from 15.4¢ a year earlier (a 3.3% change). The U.S. average was 18.16¢.</p>
<p>With those inputs, year-1 savings in the example home are $1583 at full credit. For price context, see our <a href="/electricity-prices/texas/">Texas electricity prices page</a> and the wider <a href="/guides/why-electricity-prices-differ-by-state/">explanation of why prices differ by state</a>.</p>

<h2 id="texas-cities-houston-fort-worth-el-paso-lubbock">Texas cities: Houston, Fort Worth, El Paso, Lubbock</h2>
<p>Readers often search for solar panel costs in Fort Worth, El Paso, and Lubbock. We only calculated one reference city for Texas, Houston, so we do not give separate payback numbers for the others. West Texas generally gets more sun than the Gulf Coast, which tends to raise output per kW, but we have not run those cities here.</p>
<p>You can run your own address in the <a href="/solar-calculator/">solar calculator</a>, which lets you adjust cost, use, and credit. See our <a href="/solar-panels/texas/">Texas solar page</a> for the state summary.</p>

<li><strong>Tax credit.</strong> The 30% federal Residential Clean Energy Credit (Section 25D) is not available for property placed in service after December 31, 2025. Our example assumes no incentives. See <a href="/guides/is-solar-worth-it-without-tax-credit/">solar without the tax credit</a>.</li>
<li><strong>Daytime use.</strong> The more power you use while the sun is up, the more you keep at full retail value. Read <a href="/guides/solar-daytime-electricity-use/">daytime electricity use</a>.</li>
<li><strong>Rising prices.</strong> Higher rates shorten payback. See <a href="/guides/solar-payback-if-electricity-prices-rise/">payback if prices rise</a>.</li>
<li><strong>Degradation.</strong> Panels lose a little output each year, which our example includes.</li>
</ul>
<h2 id="bottom-line">Bottom line</h2>
<p>Texas solar is a reasonable but not spectacular investment in our example: 14.3 years at $3500 per kW with full credit, or 10.6 years at $2500 per kW. A weak buyback plan can push payback past 23 years. Get several quotes, read the export credit terms closely, and test your numbers in the <a href="/solar-calculator/">solar calculator</a>. This is general information, not financial or tax advice.</p>
