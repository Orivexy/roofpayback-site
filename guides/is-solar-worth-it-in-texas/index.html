Is Solar Worth It in Texas? Buyback Rates and Payback

Texas has no statewide net metering, so your plan's buyback rate decides payback. See payback at buyback rates from 0 to 15.91¢ and in eight cities.

Published November 2, 2026·Updated November 28, 2026·5 min read·RoofPayback editorial·Figures checked against EIA and PVWatts data

Official data: EIA · DOE · IRS · how we check

Short version

Solar can pay off in Texas, but the number that decides it is one most buyers never ask about: the buyback rate. Texas has no statewide net metering. The Public Utility Commission of Texas (PUCT) explains that the rate you are paid for exported power is part of your contract with your electricity provider and is usually lower than the retail price you pay.

In our Houston example, that rate moves payback from 14.3 years, if exports earned the full 15.91¢ retail price, to more than 25 years if they earned nothing. The table below turns any buyback rate into a payback, so you can compare plans in cents rather than in sales language.

What a buyback rate is worth

The example is our standard home: a 7 kW system at $3,500 per kW ($24,500) with no incentives, using 10,500 kWh a year, 40% of it while the sun is up. Production is PVWatts for Houston, 1,421 kWh per kW a year. The retail price is the EIA average for Texas in September 2026, 15.91¢ per kilowatt-hour, up 0.9% from 15.77¢ a year earlier.

Buyback rate for exported power Year-1 savings Payback 25-year gain
0¢ (no buyback) $668 over 25 years −$4,175
3¢ $841 24.2 years $1,067
6¢ $1,013 20.9 years $6,308
9¢ $1,186 18.4 years $11,550
12¢ $1,358 16.4 years $16,792
15.91¢ (equal to retail) $1,583 14.3 years $23,624

Each cent of buyback is worth about $57 a year in this house, because it exports about 5,750 kWh of its 9,950 kWh first-year output. If a plan pays a lower buyback rate but a lower retail price, run both numbers: the retail price applies to everything you still buy from the grid.

Size for a Texas household

Texas homes use more power than the example. EIA-861M data for October 2025 to September 2026 put the average Texas household at 1,133 kWh a month, or 13,592 kWh a year, with an average bill of $181 and a peak of $266 in August. Covering that in Houston would take about 9.6 kW.

System Buyback equal to retail Buyback at 8¢ No buyback
6 kW ($21,000) 14.4 years 17.1 years 20.7 years
8 kW ($28,000) 14.2 years 18.4 years over 25 years
10 kW ($35,000) 14.3 years 19.3 years over 25 years

With a buyback equal to retail, size hardly matters. With a weak buyback, the smaller system wins clearly, because the house uses 64% of a 6 kW system's output directly and only 38% of a 10 kW system's. Sizing to cover the whole year makes sense only if the buyback is close to retail.

Eight Texas cities

Sunshine varies across the state. Same 7 kW system, same statewide average price:

City Output per kW Payback, buyback equal to retail Payback, half of retail
Houston 1,421 kWh 14.3 years 19.2 years
Austin 1,483 kWh 13.8 years 18.7 years
San Antonio 1,497 kWh 13.7 years 18.6 years
Dallas 1,506 kWh 13.6 years 18.5 years
Corpus Christi 1,523 kWh 13.5 years 18.4 years
Fort Worth 1,535 kWh 13.4 years 18.3 years
Lubbock 1,743 kWh 13.2 years 16.9 years
El Paso 1,831 kWh 13.2 years 16.4 years

El Paso panels make 29% more than Houston's, yet with full buyback the payback improves by only about a year. The reason is size: in the sunnier cities, a 7 kW system makes more than this home uses in a year, and credits stop at a year of grid purchases. With a half-retail buyback, the extra sun counts for more, and El Paso and Lubbock pull further ahead. A smaller system would suit those cities better.

Retail prices also differ by provider and utility, and this table uses the statewide average for all eight. City pages for Fort Worth, El Paso and Lubbock show local production.

Questions for your provider and installer

For your electricity provider:

  • Does my plan buy back exported power, and at what rate per kWh?
  • Is the buyback credited against my bill or paid out, and what happens to credit left at the end of a month or a year?
  • If I switch to a plan with buyback, what retail price and fees come with it?
  • If you live outside the competitive market, ask the utility or co-op the same questions. How electricity choice works explains the difference.

For the installer:

  • What buyback rate does the savings estimate assume, and from which plan?
  • What share of the system's output does the estimate expect the house to use directly?
  • What is the price per watt? Berkeley Lab's 2026 Data Update reports a national median of $3.60 per watt for 2025 installs, $3.00 for cash purchases and $4.50 for loan-financed systems. Solar panel cost per watt shows the most you can pay in Texas for a given payback.
  • Is any federal credit counted? The IRS says the Residential Clean Energy Credit "is not available for any property placed in service after December 31, 2025."

When Texas solar makes sense

In our model it makes sense when three things line up: a buyback rate close to retail, a system sized to the share of your use you can cover directly, and a price per watt at or below the national medians. Without a buyback, the example system does not pay back within 25 years in Houston. With a buyback near retail, it pays back in about 14 years almost anywhere in the state.
