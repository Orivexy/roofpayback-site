Is Solar Worth It in Texas? Cost and Payback

How much do solar panels cost in Texas, and how long until they pay back? See Houston payback by price per watt and export credit, with clear assumptions.

Published November 2, 2026·Updated November 18, 2026·5 min read·RoofPayback editorial·Figures checked against EIA and PVWatts data

Short version

Solar can pay off in Texas, but it starts from a modest base. The electricity price comes first, and in Texas it sits below the national average. How your provider credits the power you send back comes second, and in Texas that varies from one plan to the next. The installed price you negotiate decides the rest.

Using Houston sunshine, our example home pays back in 14.3 years with full export credit. That puts Texas close to Florida (14.1 years) and Arizona (13.9 years), and well behind California (7.4 years).

Texas has strong sun, so panels make plenty of power. Each kilowatt-hour they make, though, replaces grid power that costs less than in most of the country, so it saves a modest amount.

Two numbers show the range a buyer controls. A cheaper install at $2,500 per kW brings payback down to 10.6 years. A weak buyback plan can push it past 23 years. Everything below is about landing at the good end.

Gather your own numbers first

Get your own facts together before any salesperson visits. A padded quote is easier to spot when you already know your usage and rate.

  • Pull your usage. Find your yearly kWh from a year of bills. The example home uses 10,500 kWh a year. If you use much more or less, the right system size changes.
  • Find your real rate. The EIA reports an average Texas residential price of 15.91¢ per kWh for August 2026, up from 15.4¢ a year earlier, a 3.3% change. The U.S. average was 18.16¢. Your own rate may differ. Our Texas electricity prices page has state context, and why prices differ by state explains the gap.
  • Know your buyback plan. Texas has many retail electricity providers and utilities, and their buyback plans for exported power differ. Some credit exports at close to the retail rate. Others pay much less. Find out what your current plan offers, and what others in your area offer. How electricity choice works explains how retail providers differ from the utility that delivers your power.
  • Note when you use power. The more you use while the sun is up, the more solar power you keep at full retail value instead of exporting it. Daytime electricity use explains why.
  • Decide how you'd pay. Cash and loan purchases are priced differently. Read loan vs. cash before talking to anyone.

What to ask every installer

Bring these to every quote, and ask for the answers in writing.

  1. What is the total installed price, and what is it per watt? Per-watt pricing lets you compare quotes of different sizes.
  2. Is that a cash price or a financed price? Financing can raise the effective cost per watt.
  3. What production did you assume, and where did it come from? PVWatts, the estimator behind our numbers (methodology), gives an independent check for your address.
  4. What export credit did you assume? Full, partial or something else? Which provider's plan is it based on?
  5. What electricity price did you use, and did you assume it will rise? Higher rates shorten payback, so the assumption matters.
  6. Did you include panel degradation? Panels lose a little output every year. Our example includes it. A quote that skips it looks rosier than reality.
  7. Did you count any incentives? If so, which ones, and can you show the program's own page confirming they are open today?

Warning signs in a Texas quote

Slow down if a quote shows any of these:

  • The federal tax credit appears in the savings. According to the IRS, the 30% credit no longer covers systems placed in service after 2025, so a quote that subtracts it overstates your savings. Solar without the tax credit covers what help may remain.
  • No written buyback terms. If nobody can show you how exported power will be credited, the payback figure isn't grounded.
  • Full export credit assumed without checking your plan. Some Texas plans pay much less than retail. That gap alone can add years.
  • One payback number for every Texas city. Output varies across the state. A figure from Houston isn't a figure for Lubbock.
  • No per-watt price. A total without a per-watt figure is hard to compare with Berkeley Lab's benchmarks or with other quotes.
  • Pressure to sign the same day. A good offer still stands after you've compared it.

Math to run before you sign

These are the figures that move Texas payback the most. Test each against your quotes.

Price per watt

With the Texas electricity price as a given, the installed price is the lever you control most directly. Same Houston example, three price points:

Installed cost Total for 7 kW Payback (full credit)
$2,500 per kW $17,500 10.6 years
$3,000 per kW $21,000 12.5 years
$3,500 per kW $24,500 14.3 years

For a benchmark, Berkeley Lab reports a median of $4.00 per watt for host-owned residential systems installed in 2024, with a middle range of $3.00 to $5.20 (2024 dollars, before incentives). Cash purchases had a median of $3.50 per watt and loan-financed systems $4.70. Quotes can land above or below these, which is why several are worth collecting. Solar panel cost by state shows how price feeds payback elsewhere.

Export credit

Panels often make more power than the home uses at midday. The extra goes to the grid, and the credit for it shapes the result:

Credit for exported power Example payback (Houston)
Full credit 14.3 years
Half credit 19.2 years
Quarter credit 23.1 years

Check your provider's terms and the program database at DSIRE. The net metering vs. net billing guide explains how these terms work.

Output and savings

PVWatts gives Houston 1,421 kWh per kW per year. With that output and the August 2026 Texas price, year-1 savings in the example home come to $1,583 at full credit.

Houston is the reference city for every Texas figure in this guide. Output per kW changes across the state, and West Texas generally gets more sun than the Gulf Coast. Separate city pages show local production and payback for Fort Worth, El Paso and Lubbock. For your exact address, use the solar calculator, where you can adjust cost, use and credit.

Price trend

Rising electricity prices shorten payback. Payback if prices rise shows the effect.

Decide with confidence

Texas solar is a reasonable but not spectacular investment in our example. At $3,500 per kW with full credit, payback is 14.3 years. At $2,500 per kW it drops to 10.6 years. A weak buyback plan can stretch it past 23 years.

Your checklist before signing:

  • A year of usage and your real rate in hand.
  • Several quotes, each with a per-watt price.
  • Buyback terms in writing from your provider.
  • No federal tax credit counted in the savings.
  • Your own numbers run in the solar calculator.

The Texas solar page has the state summary, and solar in the south-central states compares Texas with its neighbors. In a state with cheap power and many buyback plans, the buyback plan you choose can move payback as much as the price you negotiate.
