22:T1149,<div class="short-answer"><p class="short-answer-label">Short version</p>
<p>Yes for many homes, but not on the terms most older articles describe. If you apply to connect a new system to PG&amp;E, SCE or SDG&amp;E today, you join the Net Billing Tariff, which the California Public Utilities Commission (CPUC) applies to interconnection applications submitted on or after April 15, 2023. Under it, the power your panels send to the grid earns credits based on the CPUC&#39;s Avoided Cost Calculator, which the CPUC describes as &quot;usually lower than import rates.&quot;</p>
<p>That changes the answer. Our 7 kW example home pays back in <strong>7.2 years</strong> if every exported kWh earned the full retail price. That is the old net metering result and only an upper limit for a new system. If exports earn about a quarter of retail, our working assumption for net billing, payback is <strong>12.1 years</strong>. If they earned nothing, it would be 16.5 years.</p>
<p>The rest of this guide shows where those figures come from and how a smaller system changes them.</p>
</div><h2 id="what-the-net-billing-tariff-changes">What the Net Billing Tariff changes</h2>
<p>Here is what the CPUC pages say, as read on November 28, 2026:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Who is on it.</strong> Customers of the three large investor-owned utilities who submit an interconnection application on or after April 15, 2023. The CPUC adopted it in Decision D.22-12-056 in December 2022.</li>
<li><strong>What exports earn.</strong> Credits based on Avoided Cost Calculator values, which vary by hour and are usually below the price you pay for grid power.</li>
<li><strong>How credits settle.</strong> Bills are monthly with an annual true-up, and credits roll over for 12 months.</li>
<li><strong>How long the terms last.</strong> A new customer keeps the tariff for nine years.</li>
<li><strong>A temporary boost.</strong> Residential PG&amp;E and SCE customers who apply to interconnect before the end of 2027 get what the CPUC calls &quot;slightly higher-than-normal bill credits for exported energy for nine years.&quot; SDG&amp;E customers are excluded.</li>
<li><strong>Older systems.</strong> Homes already on NEM 2.0 can stay on it for 20 years from their interconnection date. Those are the homes the full-credit result describes.</li>
</ul>
<p>We have not converted the Avoided Cost Calculator into one average cents-per-kWh figure, because it changes by hour, month and utility. That is why the quarter-credit case below is labeled as an assumption, with full credit and zero credit on either side of it.</p>
<h2 id="the-example-home-under-each-assumption">The example home under each assumption</h2>
<p>The example is a 7 kW system at $24,500 ($3,500 per kW) with no incentives, in a home using 10,500 kWh a year, 40% of it while the sun is up (<a href="/methodology/">methodology</a>). Production is the PVWatts estimate for Los Angeles: 1,677 kWh per kW a year. The price is the EIA average residential price for California in September 2026, 30.86¢ per <a href="/guides/what-is-a-kilowatt-hour/">kilowatt-hour</a>, against a U.S. average of 18.17¢.</p>

Solar cannot avoid that charge. <p>Two things stand out. The smallest system pays back fastest, while the 25-year gain rises only about $700 per added kW, so extra panels add more waiting time than profit. And moving more use into daylight hours, from 40% to 60%, cuts between 1.7 and 2.5 years off each row, about as much as dropping one or two kW. <a href="/guides/solar-daytime-electricity-use/">Solar and daytime electricity use</a> explains the mechanism, and <a href="/guides/net-metering-vs-net-billing-payback/">net metering vs. net billing</a> compares the same effect across states.</p>
<p>One caution: the model charges the same $100 a year of upkeep whatever the size, and real quotes for very small systems often cost more per watt.</p>
<h2 id="the-price-per-watt-still-matters">The price per watt still matters</h2>
<p>Berkeley Lab&#39;s 2026 Data Update reports a national median of $3.60 per watt for host-owned residential systems installed in 2025, with cash purchases at $3.00 and loan-financed systems at $4.50. It also notes that California, which makes up roughly half of its residential sample, &quot;is a relatively low-cost state for residential PV.&quot;</p>

<h2 id="questions-to-put-to-the-installer">Questions to put to the installer</h2>
<ul>
<li>Which tariff does the savings estimate use: the Net Billing Tariff, with or without the export adder, or NEM 2.0?</li>
<li>What export value per kWh did you assume, for which hours, and from which Avoided Cost Calculator year?</li>
<li>What share of the system&#39;s output does the estimate expect my home to use directly?</li>
<li>How does the estimate change if I size the system to my last 12 months of use rather than to the roof?</li>
<li>Does the price include a battery? Berkeley Lab found cash-purchase systems paired with storage had a median price $2.10 per watt higher than stand-alone systems, so ask for both versions. Our <a href="/battery-payback-calculator/">battery payback calculator</a> tests whether storage pays for your rates.</li>
<li>Is any federal credit counted? The IRS says the Residential Clean Energy Credit &quot;is not available for any property placed in service after December 31, 2025.&quot;</li>
</ul>
<p>The <a href="/guides/solar-quote-checklist/">solar quote checklist</a> covers the rest of the paperwork.</p>
<h2 id="where-this-leaves-a-california-buyer">Where this leaves a California buyer</h2>
<p>California&#39;s high price still makes solar one of the better bets in the country: even our no-credit floor pays back within 25 years. But a new system should be judged on the net billing figures, not the 7-year number. For a typical home, a modest system sized to your own use, with as much of that use as possible during the day, gives the shortest payback in our model.</p>
<p>Run your own bill, export value and system size through the <a href="/solar-calculator/">solar calculator</a>, and see the state summary on the <a href="/solar-panels/california/">California solar page</a>.</p>
