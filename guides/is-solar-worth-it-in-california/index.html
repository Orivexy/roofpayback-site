Is Solar Worth It in California in 2026?

California solar payback in our example home: 7.4 years with full export credit, 9.8 with half, 12.5 with quarter. See how credit rules change the answer.

Published November 1, 2026·Updated November 18, 2026·5 min read·RoofPayback editorial·Figures checked against EIA and PVWatts data

Short version

For many California homeowners, solar can pay back faster than in most of the country. High electricity prices put the state ahead; how your utility credits the power you send to the grid decides how far ahead. In our example home, the California payback is 7.4 years with full export credit, 9.8 years with half credit and 12.5 years with quarter credit.

Those are modeled estimates, not quotes. The checklist below helps you test a real offer against them, one line item at a time.

Why California starts ahead

Two facts work in California's favor.

Expensive power. The U.S. Energy Information Administration (EIA) reported an average residential price of 29.75¢ per kilowatt-hour (kWh) in California for August 2026. The U.S. average was 18.16¢/kWh. A year earlier, California's average was 30.92¢, a change of -3.8%. The price dipped, but it is still among the highest in the country. Solar saves you the price of each kWh it replaces, so a high price makes every panel-made kWh worth more.

Strong sun. PVWatts puts a south-facing system in Los Angeles at about 1,677 kWh per kW of panels each year.

The example is a 7 kW system at $24,500 with no incentives, in a home using 10,500 kWh a year (methodology). Under full credit in Los Angeles, its first-year savings come to $3,124.

Prices and solar detail for the state are on our California electricity price page and California solar page.

Before you ask for quotes

Get these in hand first. They make every quote easier to judge.

  • Your last 12 months of bills. Add up your total kWh use. A full year captures summer and winter.
  • Your current rate and plan. Note the price per kWh, any time-of-use periods and fixed monthly charges. If your plan prices evening power higher than midday power, read time-of-use rates explained before you compare quotes, because the hours your panels produce are not the hours your rate peaks.
  • Your utility's export credit rule. Ask how it credits power you send to the grid, and what fixed charges apply. California's rules for new systems have changed, so get the current version from your utility's website and the California Public Utilities Commission rather than from an installer's summary.
  • A sense of when you use power. If most of your use happens during the day, more of your solar output goes straight into your home. Our guide on solar and daytime electricity use explains why that helps.
  • Any local incentives. Programs change often, so check the DSIRE database and your utility's own rebate page for anything open to your address, and confirm it with the program administrator.

Questions to ask the installer

  • What is the total installed price, and what is the price per watt?
  • Is the price different if I pay cash or use a loan? (Berkeley Lab found loan-financed systems had a higher median price; see solar loan vs. cash.)
  • What export credit rate does your savings estimate assume, and where did that rate come from?
  • Does the estimate include my utility's fixed charges?
  • Does the payback figure include any tax credit or incentive? If so, which one, and has it been confirmed for my home?
  • What production per kW does the estimate use, and for which roof direction and pitch?
  • What happens to my savings if export credit is lower than you assumed?

Red flags

  • A savings estimate that never mentions export credit. In California, that rule can change payback by years. A quote that skips it is skipping the biggest unknown.
  • A payback that leans on the 30% federal credit. That credit no longer covers systems placed in service after 2025, according to the IRS. Every payback in this guide already leaves it out; solar without the tax credit covers what help remains.
  • A system sized well past your yearly use. With credits capped at a year of grid purchases, extra panels add cost without adding savings. See oversized systems and the annual true-up.
  • Pressure to sign before you have your utility's rules in writing. Confirm the export rate yourself first.
  • No cash price on a loan offer. Without both numbers, you cannot see what financing really costs.
  • One quote only. Prices vary widely, so a single offer gives you nothing to compare.

Numbers to check yourself

Export credit

Export credit California example payback
Full credit 7.4 years
Half credit 9.8 years
Quarter credit 12.5 years

Full credit means each exported kWh offsets a kWh you would buy. Half and quarter credit are scenarios, not predictions of any specific utility's rate. They show how sensitive the result is. Even at quarter credit, the California example pays back within 25 years, which many states do not manage. Our guide on net metering vs. net billing payback walks through the difference.

Price per watt

Under full credit, the California example moves with the installed price like this:

Installed cost Total for 7 kW Payback
$2,500 per kW $17,500 5.5 years
$3,000 per kW $21,000 6.5 years
$3,500 per kW $24,500 7.4 years

Divide your quote by the system size in watts to get its price per watt, then see where it falls in the table. Each step down the table takes roughly a year off payback in this example. National price benchmarks and the same math for other states are in solar panel cost by state.

How California stacks up

California is not the fastest payback in the country. Hawaii's example is 4.8 years, driven by a price of 47.18¢/kWh. But California beats most large states under the same assumptions:

State (reference city) Price Full credit payback
California (Los Angeles) 29.75¢/kWh 7.4 years
New York (New York City) 28.65¢/kWh 9 years
Arizona (Phoenix) 15.05¢/kWh 13.9 years
Florida (Orlando) 14.99¢/kWh 14.1 years
Texas (Houston) 15.91¢/kWh 14.3 years

Arizona gets more sun than California, yet pays back more slowly. Price is the reason. The full ranking is in best states for solar payback.

Decide with confidence

Solar deserves a serious look in California: power is expensive and the sun is strong. In our example, payback runs from 7.4 years with full credit to 12.5 years with quarter credit, so the export rule alone can add about five years.

You are ready to decide once you have:

  • a year of bills and your total kWh,
  • your utility's export credit and fixed charges in writing,
  • several written quotes with price per watt,
  • your own results from the solar calculator.

In California the price question is already settled in solar's favor. What is left to pin down is the export rule and the quote, and both belong in writing before you sign.
