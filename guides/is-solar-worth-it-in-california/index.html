Is Solar Worth It in California in 2026?

Under California's Net Billing Tariff, our example home pays back in about 12 years, not 7. See the math, the right size for a typical home and what to ask.

Published November 1, 2026·Updated November 28, 2026·6 min read·RoofPayback editorial·Figures checked against EIA and PVWatts data

Short version

Yes for many homes, but not on the terms most older articles describe. If you apply to connect a new system to PG&E, SCE or SDG&E today, you join the Net Billing Tariff, which the California Public Utilities Commission (CPUC) applies to interconnection applications submitted on or after April 15, 2023. Under it, the power your panels send to the grid earns credits based on the CPUC's Avoided Cost Calculator, which the CPUC describes as "usually lower than import rates."

That changes the answer. Our 7 kW example home pays back in 7.2 years if every exported kWh earned the full retail price. That is the old net metering result and only an upper limit for a new system. If exports earn about a quarter of retail, our working assumption for net billing, payback is 12.1 years. If they earned nothing, it would be 16.5 years.

The rest of this guide shows where those figures come from and how a smaller system changes them.

What the Net Billing Tariff changes

Here is what the CPUC pages say, as read on November 28, 2026:

  • Who is on it. Customers of the three large investor-owned utilities who submit an interconnection application on or after April 15, 2023. The CPUC adopted it in Decision D.22-12-056 in December 2022.
  • What exports earn. Credits based on Avoided Cost Calculator values, which vary by hour and are usually below the price you pay for grid power.
  • How credits settle. Bills are monthly with an annual true-up, and credits roll over for 12 months.
  • How long the terms last. A new customer keeps the tariff for nine years.
  • A temporary boost. Residential PG&E and SCE customers who apply to interconnect before the end of 2027 get what the CPUC calls "slightly higher-than-normal bill credits for exported energy for nine years." SDG&E customers are excluded.
  • Older systems. Homes already on NEM 2.0 can stay on it for 20 years from their interconnection date. Those are the homes the full-credit result describes.

We have not converted the Avoided Cost Calculator into one average cents-per-kWh figure, because it changes by hour, month and utility. That is why the quarter-credit case below is labeled as an assumption, with full credit and zero credit on either side of it.

The example home under each assumption

The example is a 7 kW system at $24,500 ($3,500 per kW) with no incentives, in a home using 10,500 kWh a year, 40% of it while the sun is up (methodology). Production is the PVWatts estimate for Los Angeles: 1,677 kWh per kW a year. The price is the EIA average residential price for California in September 2026, 30.86¢ per kilowatt-hour, against a U.S. average of 18.17¢.

Credit for exported power What it represents Year-1 savings Payback
Full retail (30.86¢) Upper limit: NEM 2.0 homes $3,240 7.2 years
Half retail Sensitivity case $2,459 9.4 years
Quarter retail (about 7.7¢) Our working assumption for net billing $1,878 12.1 years
No credit Floor: self-use only $1,296 16.5 years

Why the gap is so wide: this home uses only 36% of what the panels make as it is produced. The other 64% goes to the grid, so the value of an exported kWh decides most of the result.

These paybacks use the state's all-in average price, which includes the Base Services Charge that PG&E, SCE and SDG&E now bill every month whatever you use. Solar cannot avoid that charge. Our fixed charges guide estimates that taking it out can stretch the quarter-credit case from 12.1 to as much as 14 years for a customer paying the full $24.15 a month, so treat the figures here as somewhat optimistic.

Size for a California home, not the example

The example home is larger than most in the state. EIA-861M data for October 2025 to September 2026 put the average California household at 507 kWh a month, or 6,084 kWh a year, the lowest of any state. Under net billing, a system sized to that use exports less of its output at the low rate.

Same price per kW, quarter credit, average California use:

System Payback, 40% daytime use Payback, 60% daytime use 25-year gain, 40% daytime use
2 kW 8.8 years 7.1 years $17,835
3 kW 10.9 years 8.4 years $18,653
4 kW 12.4 years 9.9 years $19,291
5 kW 13.5 years 11.1 years $19,928

Two things stand out. The smallest system pays back fastest, while the 25-year gain rises only about $700 per added kW, so extra panels add more waiting time than profit. And moving more use into daylight hours, from 40% to 60%, cuts between 1.7 and 2.5 years off each row, about as much as dropping one or two kW. Solar and daytime electricity use explains the mechanism, and net metering vs. net billing compares the same effect across states.

One caution: the model charges the same $100 a year of upkeep whatever the size, and real quotes for very small systems often cost more per watt.

The price per watt still matters

Berkeley Lab's 2026 Data Update reports a national median of $3.60 per watt for host-owned residential systems installed in 2025, with cash purchases at $3.00 and loan-financed systems at $4.50. It also notes that California, which makes up roughly half of its residential sample, "is a relatively low-cost state for residential PV."

For the 7 kW example at quarter credit:

Installed price Total Payback, quarter credit Payback, full credit (upper limit)
$2.50 per watt $17,500 9 years 5.3 years
$3.00 per watt $21,000 10.5 years 6.2 years
$3.50 per watt $24,500 12.1 years 7.2 years

Under net billing, each 50¢ per watt is worth about a year and a half of payback, against about one year under full credit. How each state compares at the same prices is in solar panel cost per watt.

Questions to put to the installer

  • Which tariff does the savings estimate use: the Net Billing Tariff, with or without the export adder, or NEM 2.0?
  • What export value per kWh did you assume, for which hours, and from which Avoided Cost Calculator year?
  • What share of the system's output does the estimate expect my home to use directly?
  • How does the estimate change if I size the system to my last 12 months of use rather than to the roof?
  • Does the price include a battery? Berkeley Lab found cash-purchase systems paired with storage had a median price $2.10 per watt higher than stand-alone systems, so ask for both versions. Our battery payback calculator tests whether storage pays for your rates.
  • Is any federal credit counted? The IRS says the Residential Clean Energy Credit "is not available for any property placed in service after December 31, 2025."

The solar quote checklist covers the rest of the paperwork.

Where this leaves a California buyer

California's high price still makes solar one of the better bets in the country: even our no-credit floor pays back within 25 years. But a new system should be judged on the net billing figures, not the 7-year number. For a typical home, a modest system sized to your own use, with as much of that use as possible during the day, gives the shortest payback in our model.
