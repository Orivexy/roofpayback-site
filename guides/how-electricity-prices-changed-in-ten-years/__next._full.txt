33:Tf83,<div class="short-answer"><p class="short-answer-label">Quick verdict</p>
<p>Over the ten years from September 2016 to September 2026, the average U.S. residential price rose from <strong>12.9¢ to 18.2¢ per kWh</strong>, a <strong>41%</strong> increase. The District of Columbia (120.3%) more than doubled, and Maine (92.4%) nearly did. Nebraska (12.5%) barely moved, and Arizona (17.4%) rose less than half as fast as the nation. Where you live decides whether the past decade was a shock or a shrug, and it shapes how much a solar quote can save you.</p>

<p>Two things stand out. The biggest percentage gains are not all in the priciest states: the District of Columbia started the decade at a modest 11.72¢. And the slowest movers are clustered in the Plains and the interior West, where prices were already low or middling.</p>
<h2 id="after-inflation-and-over-full-years">After inflation, and over full years</h2>
<p>The prices above are nominal. Consumer prices as a whole rose 39.6% over the same ten years, by the Bureau of Labor Statistics&#39; Consumer Price Index for All Urban Consumers (CPI-U, U.S. city average), from 241.428 in September 2016 to 336.940 in September 2026. So the national 41.2% rise in home electricity ran only about 1% ahead of inflation.</p>
<p>State by state, that changes the picture. In 27 states the residential price rose less than the CPI, which means it fell in real terms: by about 19% in Nebraska, 16% in Arizona and 15% in Nevada. At the other end, the price rose about 58% in real terms in the District of Columbia, 38% in Maine and 24% in Hawaii.</p>
<p>Two single months can also mislead, because a month carries its own weather and rate changes. So we compared the full calendar year 2016 with the twelve months to September 2026, weighting each month by the kWh homes bought (EIA-861M). On that basis the U.S. average rose 43.8%, from 12.55¢ to 18.05¢, over a span a few months shorter than ten years. The ends of the range hold: the District of Columbia (100.6%), Maine (88.5%) and California (85.3%) rose most, and Kansas (16.5%), Nebraska and Nevada (both 17.9%) least.</p>

40:T15ff,<h2 id="when-the-steep-climb-states-make-the-stronger-case">When the steep-climb states make the stronger case</h2>
<p>If you live in Maine, California, New Hampshire or Pennsylvania, the last ten years are a record of what happens when a rate keeps going up. California went from 18.22¢ to <strong>30.86¢</strong>, and New Hampshire from 18.28¢ to <strong>29.17¢</strong>. A bill that was manageable in 2016 is a different bill now for the same use.</p>
<p>That history favors solar in a specific way. A panel system produces power at a fixed cost once it is installed, so every cent the grid price climbs makes each kWh you generate worth more. The example home on this site (7 kW, 10,500 kWh a year, $24,500 installed, no incentives; see the <a href="/methodology/">methodology</a>) assumes prices rise 2.5% a year. In states that have already climbed this fast, that assumption is not obviously aggressive, although the next ten years need not copy the last ten.</p>
<p>Some things to weigh in these states:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Check the latest year, not just the decade.</strong> Connecticut is up 42.7% over ten years yet fell 10.9% against September 2025, to 27.09¢. A sharp one-year drop can make a rate-hike story look stale.</li>
<li><strong>Ask how your utility treats exported power.</strong> A high price helps only if surplus energy earns a decent credit. See <a href="/guides/net-metering-vs-net-billing-payback/">net metering vs. net billing</a>.</li>
<li><strong>Look at your state page.</strong> The <a href="/solar-panels/california/">California</a> and <a href="/solar-panels/new-hampshire/">New Hampshire</a> pages turn the price into payback years.</li>
</ul>
<h2 id="when-the-slow-movers-make-the-stronger-case">When the slow-movers make the stronger case</h2>
<p>Slow price growth is not bad news for a household. In Nebraska the price went from 12.33¢ to 13.87¢, and in Utah from 11.2¢ to 13.41¢. Bills there still rose, and mostly with the price rather than with use: the average Nebraska bill went from $104 a month in the year to September 2017 to $123 in the year to September 2026, while use stayed near 960 kWh a month (EIA-861M). The low starting point also means there is less to offset.</p>
<p>For solar, a flat, low price is the hard case. Each kWh your panels produce displaces a cheap kWh, so the savings per kWh are small and payback is slow. That is the logic behind <a href="/guides/is-solar-worth-it-where-electricity-is-cheap/">solar where electricity is cheap</a>: the system is the same price, but the thing it replaces is not.</p>
<p>There is an upside. A household in Kansas (13.05¢ to 15.83¢) or Nevada (11.17¢ to 13.25¢) faces less risk that a sudden jump blindsides the budget. If your priority is stable bills rather than the fastest payback, a slow-moving market is its own kind of comfort. <a href="/guides/how-to-lower-your-electric-bill/">How to lower your electric bill</a> shows what a saved kWh is worth in each of these states, which is the number to set against a solar quote.</p>
<h2 id="where-the-two-groups-tie">Where the two groups tie</h2>
<p>Both groups share a few things, and they matter more than the percentages.</p>
<p>First, the national average itself rose <strong>41%</strong>, and even the slowest state, Nebraska (12.5%), did not stand still. No state saw a nominal price decline over the full decade, although 27 got cheaper after inflation.</p>
<p>Second, the percentage change hides the starting level. Texas rose 43.2%, which is close to the national figure, yet its price in September 2026 is 15.91¢, well below the New England states. A larger percentage on a small base can mean fewer cents than a smaller percentage on a large base. Always read both columns.</p>
<p>Third, the past does not set your quote. The Berkeley Lab median installed price for 2025 systems was <strong>$3.6 per watt</strong>, and the federal 30% residential credit (Section 25D) no longer applies to property placed in service after December 31, 2025. Those two facts hit buyers in the steep-climb and slow-mover states alike. For the cost side, see <a href="/guides/solar-panel-cost-by-state/">solar panel cost by state</a>.</p>
<h2 id="how-to-use-these-numbers-on-your-own-bill">How to use these numbers on your own bill</h2>
<p>Pull your latest utility bill and find the price per kWh, which is usually the total charge divided by the kWh used. Compare it to your state&#39;s September 2026 figure on the <a href="/electricity-prices/">electricity prices page</a>. If you are far above the state average, a solar estimate based on the state number will understate your savings. If you are below, it will overstate them.</p>
<p>Then run the <a href="/solar-calculator/">solar calculator</a> with your state and see how payback responds. If you want to test a faster-rising future, <a href="/guides/solar-payback-if-electricity-prices-rise/">solar payback if electricity prices rise</a> walks through what changes.</p>
<h2 id="a-decade-of-increases-is-a-reason-to-check-not-to-rush">A decade of increases is a reason to check, not to rush</h2>
<p>The pattern that matters is simple. If your state sits with the District of Columbia, Maine, Hawaii or California, the grid has already repriced your bill, and solar math deserves a serious look with a real quote. If your state sits with Nebraska, Arizona or Nevada, the case rests on your own rate and your own roof, and cutting use may come first. Either way, compare your own bill to the state figure before you read any payback estimate. The figures here describe averages, and your utility may differ.</p>
