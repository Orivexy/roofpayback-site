32:Ta96,<div class="short-answer"><p class="short-answer-label">In one minute</p>
<p>Using more of your solar power while the panels are producing only shortens payback when exported power earns less than the retail rate. With full retail credit, timing makes no difference to payback in our model. At a quarter of retail, going from 30% to 60% daytime use turns a Texas system that never pays back within 25 years into one that pays back in 18.6.</p>
<p>The reason is simple. A kWh you use as the panels make it saves the full retail price. A kWh you send to the grid earns whatever the export credit is. The gap between the two is what each shifted kWh is worth.</p>
<p><strong>Self-consumption</strong> means using solar power at the moment it is made instead of exporting it. <strong>Daytime share</strong> here means the part of your yearly use that happens while the sun is up. Our example home assumes 40%; that is a modeling assumption, not a measured national figure, so replace it with your own if you can.</p>
</div><h2 id="step-1-find-out-how-your-utility-credits-exports">Step 1: Find out how your utility credits exports</h2>
<p>Read your utility&#39;s solar or customer-generation tariff, or call and ask what an exported kWh is worth and how often credits are settled. Rules differ by state and often by utility:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Maine:</strong> under the Public Utilities Commission&#39;s Net Energy Billing kWh Credit Program, open to all utility customers, participants receive kWh credits on their bills, and unused credits expire after 12 months. That resembles the full-credit case with a yearly cap in our model; ask your utility which parts of the bill the credits offset.</li>
<li><strong>Louisiana:</strong> for customers who apply or install after December 31, 2019, the Public Service Commission rules say exports &quot;shall be credited at Avoided Cost,&quot; while all power bought from the utility is paid at the full retail rate. That is closer to our quarter-credit case; confirm with your utility that these rules apply to it.</li>
</ul>
<p>For other states, your utility tariff and your state utility commission are the places to check. <a href="/guides/net-metering-vs-net-billing-payback/">Net metering vs. net billing</a> explains the main arrangements.</p>
<h2 id="step-2-estimate-your-daytime-share">Step 2: Estimate your daytime share</h2>
<p>Smart-meter data in your utility account shows when you use power, not just how much. Compare what you use during the hours your roof gets direct sun with your daily total. A house that sits empty on weekdays may be near 30%; a household that works from home and runs laundry and cooling during the day may be well above 40%.</p>

<h2 id="step-4-put-a-dollar-value-on-each-shifted-kwh">Step 4: Put a dollar value on each shifted kWh</h2>
<p>Each kWh you move from the evening into the sunny hours is worth the retail price minus the export credit. At quarter credit, that is three quarters of the retail price: about 23.5¢ in Maine, 11.9¢ in Texas, 11.2¢ in Arizona and 10¢ in Louisiana, at September 2026 average prices. Moving 1,000 kWh a year into daylight is then worth about $100 to $235 a year, depending on the state. Your meter data tells you how many kWh you could realistically move.</p>
<p>That is the budget for any effort or equipment aimed at daytime use. A timer on the dishwasher costs nothing; a home battery costs far more, and the <a href="/battery-payback-calculator/">battery payback calculator</a> tests whether it earns that back.</p>
<h2 id="step-5-move-the-loads-that-are-easy-to-move">Step 5: Move the loads that are easy to move</h2>
<ul>
<li><strong>Delay timers.</strong> Run the dishwasher and laundry at midday instead of overnight.</li>
<li><strong>Heating and cooling.</strong> Pre-cool or pre-heat while the sun is out, then let the house coast into the evening.</li>
<li><strong>Electric car.</strong> Charge at home during the day when the car is parked there; <a href="/guides/solar-and-electric-car-charging/">solar and electric car charging</a> shows how much that changes payback.</li>
<li><strong>Water heating.</strong> A heat-pump or electric water heater on a timer can run in daylight.</li>
</ul>
<p>If your utility has time-of-use rates, check them first: shifting into midday can mean buying power at a different price at other hours. <a href="/guides/time-of-use-electricity-rates-explained/">Time-of-use electricity rates</a> explains what to look for.</p>
<h2 id="mistakes-that-cost-money">Mistakes that cost money</h2>
<ul>
<li><strong>Rearranging your routine under full retail credit.</strong> It does nothing for payback there; spend the effort on getting a better installed price.</li>
<li><strong>Guessing your daytime share.</strong> Check your meter data before trusting 40% or any other figure.</li>
<li><strong>Sizing big when exports earn little.</strong> A smaller system sends less to the grid; <a href="/guides/how-many-solar-panels-do-i-need/">how many solar panels do I need</a> shows that at half credit the smallest system pays back first.</li>
<li><strong>Accepting an installer&#39;s model unchecked.</strong> Ask what export value and daytime share the estimate assumes.</li>
</ul>
<p>Then run your own export credit, daytime share and quote through the <a href="/solar-calculator/">solar calculator</a>, and compare with your state&#39;s <a href="/electricity-prices/">electricity prices</a>.</p>
