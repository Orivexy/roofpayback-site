Daytime Power Use and Solar Payback: 30%, 40%, 60%

How using more solar power during the day changes payback under full and reduced export credits, with results for California, Texas, Florida and Arizona.

In one minute

Using more of your solar power while the sun is up can shorten payback a lot. But that only holds when your utility pays you less for the power you export. If exports earn the full retail rate, daytime use barely moves payback in our model. If exports earn a reduced credit, going from 30% to 60% daytime use can cut payback by many years.

Your electricity price sets how much each kWh is worth. Your export rule decides whether timing matters at all. Learn that rule before you decide whether shifting chores to midday is worth the effort.

A quick definition before you start: self-consumption means using solar power at the moment your panels make it, instead of sending it to the grid.

Step 1: Learn how your utility credits exported power

Call your utility or read your rate plan, and find the line about exports. You are looking for one of two broad setups.

  • Full credit. Every exported kWh is credited at the retail price. This is classic net metering.
  • Reduced credit. Exports earn less than retail. Our model tests the case where exports earn a quarter of the retail rate, which is closer to net billing.

Your utility may sit somewhere between these two, so read the actual numbers. Net metering and incentive programs vary by state and utility. DSIRE, the Database of State Incentives for Renewables & Efficiency, tracks current programs. For how the two rules play out over a system's life, see net metering vs net billing payback.

Why this comes first: a kWh you use directly saves you the retail price. A kWh you export earns whatever the credit is. When the two are equal, timing is irrelevant. When the credit is lower, every kWh you use yourself is worth more than one you send out.

Step 2: Estimate your daytime share today

Log in to your utility account and look for hourly or monthly usage data. Smart meter data is best because it shows when power is used, not just how much.

Ask yourself who is home between morning and late afternoon. If nobody is home on weekdays, your daytime share may be lower than you think. If you work from home, you may already use more power in daytime hours.

Write down a rough figure. The three levels this guide tests are 30%, 40% and 60%.

Step 3: Match your situation to the model results

The figures use our 7 kW example home with no incentives (methodology). Payback here means the number of years until savings repay the installed cost.

State Daytime use Credit full Credit quarter
California 30% 7.4 years 14.2 years
California 40% 7.4 years 12.5 years
California 60% 7.4 years 10.1 years
Texas 30% 14.3 years Not within 25 years
Texas 40% 14.3 years 23.1 years
Texas 60% 14.3 years 18.6 years
Florida 30% 14.1 years Not within 25 years
Florida 40% 14.1 years 23.5 years
Florida 60% 14.1 years 19.1 years
Arizona 30% 13.9 years Not within 25 years
Arizona 40% 13.9 years 22.3 years
Arizona 60% 13.9 years 18.3 years

Find your state, or the closest match, and read across. Four lessons come out of it:

  1. Full credit makes timing irrelevant. Texas stays at 14.3 years whatever the daytime share. The other three states behave the same way. Savings come from total production, not from when you use it.
  2. Reduced credit makes timing decisive. In Texas, 30% daytime use does not pay back within 25 years. At 40% it takes 23.1 years; at 60%, 18.6 years. Arizona and Florida follow the same pattern.
  3. High prices amplify the effect. California's full-credit payback is 7.4 years. Under reduced credit, it moves from 14.2 years at 30% to 10.1 years at 60%. Expensive retail power makes each self-used kWh more valuable. Where power is cheap, the same shift still helps, but it starts from a longer wait, as the guide to solar where electricity is cheap shows.
  4. Shifting loads never fully closes the gap. In every row, reduced-credit payback is still longer than full-credit payback, even at 60%.

Step 4: Move flexible loads into sunny hours

If Step 1 showed a reduced credit, this is the step that pays. Start with the easiest changes and work toward the ones that need planning.

  • Appliances with a delay timer. Set the dishwasher and laundry to run at midday instead of overnight.
  • Heating and cooling. Pre-cool or pre-heat the house while the sun is out, if your comfort allows. The house then coasts into the evening.
  • An electric vehicle. Charge during the day when the car is home. Our guide to home EV charging cost by state covers what that charging costs from the grid.
  • Time-of-day rates. Check whether your price changes by hour. Time-of-use electricity rates can change the value of midday power, so read that guide before you rearrange your routine.

These are ideas, not promises. Your daytime share depends on your household and your schedule.

Step 5: Re-run the numbers with your own inputs

Open the solar calculator and enter your bill, your export rule and your new daytime estimate. Compare the result with your state's electricity prices.

Remember what our model cannot see. Roof direction, shading, local rates and your installer's price will all change the answer. Your quote may land above or below the $3,500 per kW we use.

Mistakes that cost money

  • Rearranging your life under full net metering. If exports earn retail, moving laundry to noon does little for payback. Spend your effort on getting a fair price instead.
  • Guessing your daytime share. If nobody is home on weekdays, your share may be lower than you think. Check meter data before trusting a number.
  • Accepting an installer's model without questions. Ask how the quote handles exports and daytime use, and compare the answer with your actual rate plan.
  • Counting on the federal tax credit. The 30% residential credit ended for systems placed in service after December 31, 2025, so a payback that includes it is out of date.
  • Thinking load shifting turns a reduced credit into a full one. It narrows the gap. It never closed it in any of our cases.

Your checklist

  • Export rule confirmed: full credit, reduced credit, or something in between
  • Current programs checked for your state and utility
  • Hourly or monthly usage pulled from your utility account
  • Rough daytime share written down
  • Closest row found in the payback table
  • Flexible loads listed: dishwasher, laundry, heating and cooling, EV
  • Time-of-use rates checked
  • Your own numbers run in the solar calculator
  • Installer asked how they model exports and daytime use

In our example home, raising daytime use from 30% to 60% moved Texas from no payback within 25 years to 18.6 years under reduced credit. That is the size of the lever when exports earn less than retail.
