23:T9f4,<div class="short-answer"><h2 id="short-answer">Short answer</h2>
<p>Using more of your solar power while the sun is up can shorten payback a lot, but only when your utility pays you less for exported power. Where exports earn the full retail rate, daytime use barely changes the payback in our model. Where exports earn a reduced credit, moving from 30% to 60% daytime use can cut payback by many years.</p>
<p>Self-consumption means using the solar power your panels make at the moment they make it, instead of sending it to the grid. This guide compares 30%, 40% and 60% daytime use under two export rules, using the same example home in four states.</p>
</div><h2 id="the-example-home-and-the-two-credit-cases">The example home and the two credit cases</h2>
<p>Our example home has these assumptions:</p>
<ul>
<li>A 7 kW system and 10500 kWh/yr of household use (a kWh, or kilowatt-hour, is the unit your bill charges for; see <a href="/guides/what-is-a-kilowatt-hour/">what is a kilowatt-hour</a>).</li>
<li>40% of use happens while the sun is up in the base case.</li>
<li>Installed cost of $3500/kW ($24500 total), no incentives, and $100/yr maintenance.</li>
<li>Electricity prices rise 2.5%/yr and panels lose 0.5%/yr of output.</li>
<li>A 25-year horizon, with export credits capped at a year of grid purchases (an annual true-up).</li>
<li>Production comes from PVWatts v8, south-facing at a 20° pitch with 14% losses, using one reference city per state. Prices are the EIA August 2026 residential averages.</li>
</ul>
<p>We test two export rules. &quot;Credit full&quot; means every exported kWh is credited at the retail price, like classic net metering. &quot;Credit quarter&quot; means exports are credited at a quarter of the retail price, which is closer to net billing. Your own utility may differ, so check your rate plan. See <a href="/guides/net-metering-vs-net-billing-payback/">net metering vs net billing payback</a> for more on how these rules work.</p>
<h2 id="why-daytime-use-matters">Why daytime use matters</h2>
<p>Every kWh you use directly from your panels is a kWh you do not buy at the retail price. A kWh you export earns whatever your utility credits for it. When the credit equals the retail price, both paths are worth the same, so it does not matter when you use power. When the credit is lower, each directly used kWh is worth more than each exported kWh.</p>
<p>That is why the &quot;credit full&quot; results below do not change as daytime use goes up, while the &quot;credit quarter&quot; results do.</p>

<h2 id="what-the-table-shows">What the table shows</h2>
<p><strong>With full credit, timing does not matter.</strong> In Texas the payback stays at 14.3 years at every daytime share. The same is true in the other three states. Your savings come from total production, not from when you use it.</p>
<p><strong>With reduced credit, timing matters a lot.</strong> In Texas, 30% daytime use does not pay back within 25 years in our model. At 40% it takes 23.1 years, and at 60% it takes 18.6 years. Arizona and Florida follow the same pattern.</p>
<p><strong>California shows the effect in a high-price state.</strong> Its full-credit payback is 7.4 years. With reduced credit it moves from 14.2 years at 30% to 10.1 years at 60%. High retail prices make each self-used kWh more valuable.</p>
<p><strong>Reduced credit never beats full credit.</strong> In every row, the reduced-credit payback is longer than the full-credit payback, even at 60% daytime use.</p>
<h2 id="how-to-raise-your-daytime-use">How to raise your daytime use</h2>
<p>Your daytime share depends on your household, so these are ideas, not promises:</p>
<ul>
<li>Run the dishwasher, laundry and other flexible loads at midday.</li>
<li>Pre-cool or pre-heat the house while the sun is out, if your comfort allows.</li>
<li>Charge an electric vehicle during the day when you can. See <a href="/guides/home-ev-charging-cost-by-state/">home EV charging cost by state</a>.</li>
<li>If you work from home, you may already use more power in daytime hours.</li>
<li>Check whether your rates change by time of day. Our guide to <a href="/guides/time-of-use-electricity-rates-explained/">time-of-use electricity rates</a> explains how that can change the value of midday power.</li>
</ul>
<p>If nobody is home on weekdays, your daytime share may be lower than you think. Your utility bill or smart meter data can show your real pattern.</p>
<h2 id="what-this-model-leaves-out">What this model leaves out</h2>
<p>These results use one set of assumptions. Your roof direction, shading, local rates and installer price will change them. The 30% federal Residential Clean Energy Credit (Section 25D) is not available for property placed in service after December 31, 2025, so we assume no incentives. Berkeley Lab reports a median installed price of $4.0 per watt for host-owned systems installed in 2024, so your quote may be higher or lower than our $3500/kW. Our <a href="/methodology/">methodology page</a> lists every input.</p>
<h2 id="how-to-check-your-own-numbers">How to check your own numbers</h2>
<ol>
<li>Find out how your utility credits exports. Net metering and incentive programs vary by state and utility, and DSIRE keeps a database of current programs.</li>
<li>Look at your hourly or monthly usage if your utility provides it.</li>
<li>Run your numbers in the <a href="/solar-calculator/">solar calculator</a> and compare with your state&#39;s <a href="/electricity-prices/">electricity prices</a>.</li>
<li>Ask installers how they model exports and daytime use, and compare the answer with your rate plan.</li>
</ol>
<h2 id="bottom-line">Bottom line</h2>
<p>If your utility credits exports at the full retail price, daytime use will not change your solar payback much. If it credits exports at less, daytime use becomes one of the biggest levers you control. In our example home, raising daytime use from 30% to 60% moved Texas from no payback within 25 years to 18.6 years under reduced credit. Check your credit rules first, then use the <a href="/solar-calculator/">solar calculator</a> to test your own situation.</p>
