Fixed Charges on Your Electric Bill: Why Using Less Saves Less

Real 2026 fixed charges from California, Georgia Power and FPL, what each adds to the price of a kWh, and how a fixed charge stretches solar payback.

Published November 28, 2026·6 min read·RoofPayback editorial·Figures checked against EIA and PVWatts data

Official data: EIA · DOE · IRS · how we check

Short version

Most residential bills have two parts: a fixed monthly charge you pay however little power you use, and per-kWh charges for the electricity itself. Using less, or making your own power with solar panels, only cuts the second part.

How big the fixed part is depends on your utility. Three real examples from 2026 tariffs:

  • PG&E and SCE (California): a Base Services Charge of $24.15 a month for most customers, lower for low-income customers.
  • Georgia Power: a Basic Service Charge of $0.4603 a day, about $14 a month.
  • Florida Power & Light (FPL): a base charge of $10.52 a month, with a $30 minimum base bill.

At each state's average household use, those charges add between about 1¢ and almost 5¢ to every kWh. That difference decides how much of a bill you can actually avoid.

Three real fixed charges, checked against the tariffs

California's Base Services Charge. The California Public Utilities Commission (CPUC) adopted an income-graduated fixed charge for the three large investor-owned utilities in Decision D.24-05-028 in May 2024, later renamed the Base Services Charge. The amounts are $6.00 a month for CARE customers, $12.08 for FERA customers or residents of qualifying affordable housing, and $24.15 for everyone else. SCE says the change took effect in November 2025, and PG&E's restructured bill started in March 2026; the CPUC scheduled SDG&E for the fourth quarter of 2025. In exchange, per-kWh prices fell: the CPUC estimated summer peak reductions of about 4.7¢ for PG&E, 4.6¢ for SCE and 6.8¢ for SDG&E, and SCE says its per-kWh cost fell by about 10%. SCE also says the charge applies to solar customers.

Georgia Power, Schedule R-31. For bills from June 2026, the standard residential tariff has a Basic Service Charge of $0.4603 per day in every season. The minimum monthly bill is that charge plus three riders (environmental compliance, demand-side management and the municipal franchise fee).

FPL, Rate RS-1. FPL's rate sheet effective September 2026 lists a $10.52 monthly base charge. It adds: "Residential customers whose monthly base electric service costs fall below $30 are subject to a minimum $30 base bill."

What a fixed charge adds to each kWh

EIA's state average price is utility revenue divided by kWh sold, so it spreads fixed charges over every kWh. The fewer kWh a household uses, the more each one carries. The table divides each charge by the state's average household use from EIA-861M (October 2025 to September 2026).

Utility Fixed charge per month State average use Fixed charge per kWh at that use Share of the state's average bill Per kWh at 300 kWh a month
PG&E, SCE (California) $24.15 507 kWh 4.76¢ 14.8% of $163 8.05¢
Georgia Power about $14 1,097 kWh 1.28¢ 8.4% of $167 4.67¢
FPL (Florida) $10.52 1,104 kWh 0.95¢ 6.2% of $169 3.51¢

California combines the largest of the three charges with the lowest household use of any state, so the fixed part is a much bigger slice of a typical bill there than in Georgia or Florida. A small apartment using 300 kWh a month carries about 8¢ of fixed charge per kWh on a PG&E or SCE bill.

These are estimates. The state averages mix many utilities, including municipal ones without these charges, and they include taxes and riders that we do not separate out.

How a fixed charge stretches solar payback

Because the fixed charge stays, the price a solar kWh actually saves is the per-kWh part of the bill, not the all-in average. As an estimate, take the September 2026 state average of 30.86¢ and subtract the 4.76¢ fixed share: about 26.1¢. That is the largest adjustment, because it assumes every customer pays the full $24.15. It fits a customer of PG&E, SCE or SDG&E who is not on CARE or FERA. The state average also includes CARE and FERA customers, who pay $6.00 or $12.08, and municipal utilities without this charge, so the real fixed share in that average is smaller than 4.76¢.

We ran both prices through the example home on our methodology page (7 kW, 10,500 kWh a year, $24,500, no incentives, Los Angeles sunshine). Since April 15, 2023, new solar customers have taken service on the CPUC's Net Billing Tariff, which credits exports at Avoided Cost Calculator values that the CPUC says are "usually lower than import rates." As in our California guide, a quarter of the retail price is our working assumption, half is a sensitivity case, and full credit is only an upper bound.

Export credit Payback at 30.86¢ Payback at 26.1¢ (fixed charge removed) First-year saving at 26.1¢
A quarter of the retail price (working assumption) 12.1 years 14 years $1,588
Half of the retail price (sensitivity case) 9.4 years 11.1 years $2,080
Full retail credit (upper bound only) 7.2 years 8.4 years $2,740

For a customer paying the full $24.15, leaving the fixed charge in the price makes the payback look up to about 2 years shorter in the working case and 1.6 years in the sensitivity case (differences taken before rounding). For CARE and FERA customers, whose charge is lower, the gap is smaller. In Georgia and Florida the effect is smaller, because the charge is a smaller share of each kWh: about 1.3¢ and 1¢ at average use.

What this means for the calculators on this site

The solar calculator and the state pages start from the EIA state average, which includes fixed charges. For a utility with a large fixed charge, that overstates the value of each solar kWh. The electricity cost calculator has a "My bill's rates" mode: enter the per-kWh price from your tariff there, and use the same price in the solar calculator, instead of the state average.

Finding your own fixed charge

  1. Read the bill. Look for a line named customer charge, service charge, basic service charge, base charge, base services charge or meter charge. Some utilities bill it per day, as Georgia Power does.
  2. Check the tariff. The residential rate schedule on your utility's website, approved by the state commission, has the exact figure and any minimum bill.
  3. Split the bill. Divide the total by the kWh used: that is your all-in price. Then divide only the per-kWh charges by the kWh: that is the price you can avoid. The gap is what the fixed charge adds.
  4. Use a low-use month. In a mild spring or fall month the fixed part is the largest share of the bill, so it is easiest to see.

Questions to ask before trusting a savings estimate

  • Does the estimate assume the fixed charge stays on every bill, or does it treat the whole bill as avoidable?
  • Which rate plan and which per-kWh price did it use?
  • Is there a minimum bill, and does it apply when solar exports exceed your use?
  • How are exports credited, and how often are they settled? See oversized solar system annual true-up.

A promise that the bill goes to zero is a warning sign wherever a fixed charge or minimum bill exists. More quote checks are in the solar quote checklist, and the price of the kWh you can avoid is the number to carry into how to lower your electric bill.
