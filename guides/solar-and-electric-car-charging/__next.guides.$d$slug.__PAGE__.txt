22:Ta2f,<div class="short-answer"><h2 id="the-answer-up-front">The answer up front</h2>
<p>An electric car adds a new, steady load to a home. Covering it with solar takes a bigger rooftop system, which costs more up front. In the example homes below, though, the payback time stayed about the same or got slightly shorter once the car arrived.</p>
<p>Payback is the number of years of bill savings needed to repay the system cost. This guide assumes a car adds 3,500 kWh of home charging per year. That is an assumption, not a measurement: your real number turns on how far you drive, which car you own and how you charge.</p>
</div><h2 id="meet-the-household">Meet the household</h2>
<p>Picture one family whose house uses 10,500 <a href="/guides/what-is-a-kilowatt-hour/">kilowatt-hours</a> a year before any car. The system is priced at $3,500 per kW installed, with no incentives (<a href="/methodology/">methodology</a>). Output comes from PVWatts and prices from the EIA&#39;s August 2026 residential averages. We run the same family in four states to see how location shapes the story.</p>
<h2 id="before-the-car-sizing-for-the-house-alone">Before the car: sizing for the house alone</h2>
<p>The family starts by right-sizing. That means choosing the system whose yearly output roughly matches yearly use, rounded to whole kilowatts.</p>
<p>Without a car, the right size and the payback look like this:</p>

<td>7 kW ($24,500)</td>

<p>Sunnier places need fewer kW for the same kWh. That is why the California and Arizona versions of the house get away with smaller systems than the Texas one.</p>
<p>The California figure differs from the 7 kW, 7.4-year result quoted in other guides on this site. Those guides put the same fixed 7 kW system in every state so locations can be compared directly. Here the system is sized to the home&#39;s use instead, and in Los Angeles about 6 kW covers 10,500 kWh. Because that smaller system costs $21,000 rather than $24,500, payback comes in at 6.8 years. Arizona shows the same effect: 12.5 years at a right-sized 6 kW against 13.9 years at a fixed 7 kW. The <a href="/guides/how-many-solar-panels-do-i-need/">solar panels guide</a> walks through the sizing step.</p>

<p>Payback barely moves. In California it holds at 6.8 years; in the other three states it slips down a little. Cost per kW stays fixed in this model, so the system and the savings grow in step. The car does not make solar cheaper. It makes the project bigger. The same logic applies to any large new load, as the <a href="/guides/solar-for-high-electricity-use/">high-usage homes guide</a> shows.</p>
<h2 id="if-the-car-charges-overnight">If the car charges overnight</h2>
<p>The story gets less tidy here. Many cars charge at night, and solar panels only make power while the sun is up. In the model, 40% of the home&#39;s use happens during the day.</p>
<p>So if the family plugs in after dinner, the panels may export power by day while the car pulls from the grid by night. How much that costs depends on the utility. Some programs credit exports at the full retail rate; others pay much less. The example caps export credits at a year of grid purchases, settled once a year in a true-up.</p>
<p>A family that can charge at midday or on weekends uses more solar directly. One that cannot should ask the utility how night charging is priced. Three guides go deeper: <a href="/guides/net-metering-vs-net-billing-payback/">net metering vs. net billing</a>, <a href="/guides/solar-daytime-electricity-use/">daytime electricity use</a> and <a href="/guides/time-of-use-electricity-rates-explained/">time-of-use rates</a>.</p>
<h2 id="if-the-quote-comes-in-higher">If the quote comes in higher</h2>
<p>The model uses $3,500 per kW, which equals $3.50 per watt. Real quotes vary, and a higher one lengthens payback for every household in the tables, with or without the car. Because the with-car system is larger, each extra cent per watt adds more dollars to the total, so comparing quotes on price per watt matters more once the car is in the plan. The guide to <a href="/guides/solar-panel-cost-by-state/">solar panel cost by state</a> shows how price per watt feeds into payback.</p>
<p>Federal help is no longer part of the picture. The 30% residential credit ended for systems placed in service after December 31, 2025, so the example assumes none. State and utility programs are listed in DSIRE.</p>
<h2 id="how-the-family-checks-its-own-numbers">How the family checks its own numbers</h2>
<p>Before signing anything, a household in this spot would:</p>
<ol>
<li>Estimate the car&#39;s yearly kWh, falling back on the 3,500 kWh assumption only if nothing better is available.</li>
<li>Add it to current yearly use from past bills.</li>
<li>Run both totals in the <a href="/solar-calculator/">solar calculator</a> to compare size and payback.</li>
<li>Ask installers to quote with and without the car, then compare price per watt.</li>
<li>Ask the utility how exports are credited and whether it offers an EV rate.</li>
</ol>
<p>Home charging costs also differ by state; the <a href="/guides/home-ev-charging-cost-by-state/">home EV charging cost guide</a> has the comparison. Local rates are on the <a href="/electricity-prices/">electricity prices page</a>.</p>
<h2 id="what-would-change-the-result">What would change the result</h2>
<p>Four things could push this family&#39;s outcome away from the example:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Their real driving.</strong> The 3,500 kWh figure is an assumption. More miles mean a bigger system; fewer mean a smaller one.</li>
<li><strong>When they charge.</strong> Night charging leans on how the utility credits daytime exports. Midday charging uses more solar directly.</li>
<li><strong>Their utility&#39;s rules.</strong> Export credit rates, true-up terms and any EV rate all shift the savings.</li>
<li><strong>The quote.</strong> A price above the $3.50 per watt used here lengthens payback.</li>
</ul>
<p>The takeaway for an EV household: size the system for the car only once the car is real, and expect a bigger bill to the installer, bigger yearly savings and roughly the same payback. Whether those savings hold up depends mostly on when the car charges and what your utility pays for daytime exports.</p>
