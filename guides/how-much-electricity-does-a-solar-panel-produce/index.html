How Much Electricity Does a Solar Panel Make Monthly?

A south-facing kW makes 1,755 kWh a year in Phoenix and 1,088 in Seattle. See output per panel and per month by state, and how to scale it to your system.

In one minute

Solar output is measured per kilowatt (kW) of panels, because panel ratings vary by model. A south-facing kW in Phoenix makes 1,755 kWh a year; the same kW in Seattle makes 1,088 kWh.

To get one panel's output, multiply its rating in kW by your state's figure. A panel rated at 0.4 kW in New York City makes about 0.4 × 1,291, or roughly 520 kWh a year. In Phoenix the same panel would make noticeably more, and in Seattle noticeably less.

Months swing even more than places: a kW in Seattle gives 40 kWh in January and 143 kWh in July. Look up your state below, then scale the numbers to your panel or your whole system.

Step 1: Find your panel rating and system size

Look at your quote for two numbers: the rating of each panel and the total system size in kW. Ratings are printed in watts on the spec sheet, so divide by a thousand to get kW. A quote that says "7 kW" means panels that add up to 7 kW, whatever their count.

All figures here come from PVWatts, for south-facing panels at a 20° roof pitch with 14% system losses, one reference city per state (see the methodology).

If your roof faces east or west, or your pitch is steeper, expect different results. Our guides on east-west-facing panels and roof pitch cover how much that moves the output.

Step 2: Look up your state's yearly output per kW

Yearly output per kW is the anchor number. Multiply it by your system's kW for a year of production, or by one panel's rating in kW for that panel's share. A spread of states compares like this:

State (reference city) Yearly kWh per kW January July
Arizona (Phoenix) 1,755 118 kWh 156 kWh
California (Los Angeles) 1,677 110 kWh 171 kWh
Florida (Orlando) 1,544 117 kWh 132 kWh
Texas (Houston) 1,421 93 kWh 140 kWh
New York (New York City) 1,291 79 kWh 141 kWh
Oregon (Portland) 1,128 35 kWh 155 kWh
Washington (Seattle) 1,088 40 kWh 143 kWh
Alaska (Anchorage) 922 14 kWh 124 kWh

Every other state has its own page with local numbers: start from solar panels by state or jump straight to Arizona, Texas or New York.

Step 3: Check how big the winter drop is

Divide summer output by winter output to see how uneven your year will be. Florida's ratio is 1.2, among the flattest in the country. Texas is 1.5 and New York 1.7. Oregon (3.2) and Washington (3.3) swing much harder, and Alaska is the extreme at 6.6.

A high ratio means strong summers carry the year. Solar panels in winter by state ranks every state and explains why the gap grows farther north.

Step 4: Turn it into a monthly number for your home

Take the January and July figures for your state and multiply each by your system's kW. That gives you a realistic low month and high month.

Picture a Portland, Oregon, home with a 7 kW system, the size of our example home (see the methodology). January gives 35 kWh per kW, so the system makes about 7 × 35, or roughly 250 kWh, that month. July gives 155 kWh per kW, so about 7 × 155, or roughly 1,090 kWh. Spring and fall land in between, and the same system makes more than four times as much in July as in January.

Now set those months beside the same months on your bills. Your meter counts the same unit, the kilowatt-hour, so the comparison is direct: a January bill well above the panels' January output means that month still draws on the grid, even if July produces a surplus.

Step 5: Match output to what you actually use

Panels produce when the sun is up, and most homes use a lot of their power after dark. The example home assumes 40% of its 10,500 kWh yearly use happens while the sun is up. The rest comes from the grid in the evening and at night, and whatever the panels make beyond daytime use flows out to the grid. What that exported power is worth depends on how your utility credits it.

Where credits are full, a big summer surplus can offset a thin winter. Where they are not, surplus is worth less than the power you would have bought. The daytime use guide and the annual true-up guide show how this plays out.

Put the numbers into the solar calculator with your state to see what the output means in dollars. Your local price per kWh matters just as much: the electricity prices page lists it by state.

Step 6: Allow for decline over the years

Panels lose a little output each year. Our example home assumes 0.5% degradation per year, so the first-year number you calculated shrinks slowly. That is normal, and your installer's production estimate should state its own assumption. See degradation and payback for what it does to long-run savings.

Mistakes that cost money

Using a nationwide average. A "typical" panel figure hides a gap of 1,755 versus 1,088 kWh per kW between Phoenix and Seattle. Use your state's number.

Reading one good month as the norm. July in Idaho is 180 kWh per kW; January is 55. Judge output over a full year, or compare each month against that month's bill.

Counting panels instead of kilowatts. Two quotes with different panel counts can have the same kW. Compare the kW and the price per watt; solar panel cost by state shows how price changes payback, and how many solar panels you need turns kW into a panel count.

Trusting a quote's production claim blindly. Ask which tool produced it, what tilt, direction and losses it assumed, and whether shading is included. Compare it with PVWatts for your address.

Ignoring the export rule. A summer surplus is only as valuable as the credit your utility gives it. Check the rules with your utility, and look up state programs on DSIRE.

Your checklist

  • Find your system size in kW and each panel's rating on the quote.
  • Look up your state's yearly output per kW and multiply by your kW, or by one panel's rating for a per-panel figure.
  • Note the January and July figures for your state to see your low and high months.
  • Check your summer-to-winter ratio: a high one means weak winters.
  • Compare your monthly bills to the matching months' production.
  • Ask your utility how it credits surplus power.
  • Ask the installer which production tool and assumptions its estimate uses.
  • Run your numbers through the solar calculator.
