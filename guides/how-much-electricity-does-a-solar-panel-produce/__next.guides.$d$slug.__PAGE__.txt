21:T305e,<div class="short-answer"><p class="short-answer-label">In one minute</p>
<p>Solar output is quoted per kilowatt (kW) of panels because panel ratings vary. A south-facing kW makes 1,779 kWh a year in Albuquerque, 1,291 in New York City and 922 in Anchorage, according to PVWatts.</p>
<p>For one panel, multiply its rating in kW by the figure for your state. A 0.4 kW panel in New York City makes about 0.4 × 1,291, roughly 516 kWh a year: about 56 kWh in July and 26 in December. For a whole system, multiply by its size instead.</p>
<p>The table below gives all 12 months for every state. The month-by-month view matters more than the yearly total when you compare production with your bills.</p>
</div><h2 id="step-1-find-your-panel-rating-and-system-size">Step 1: Find your panel rating and system size</h2>
<p>Your quote lists the rating of each panel, in watts, and the system size in kW. Divide watts by 1,000 to get kW. A &quot;7 kW&quot; quote means panels that add up to 7 kW, whatever their count.</p>
<h2 id="step-2-read-your-state-39-s-row">Step 2: Read your state&#39;s row</h2>
<p>Each figure is kWh made by 1 kW of panels facing south at a 20° pitch, with 14% system losses, in the state&#39;s reference city (PVWatts v8, typical-year weather; see the <a href="/methodology/">methodology</a>).</p>

35:T1098,<h2 id="step-3-note-when-your-best-and-worst-months-fall">Step 3: Note when your best and worst months fall</h2>
<p>December is the weakest month in 47 of the 51 places in the table. The best month is less predictable than most people assume. July comes first in 27 states, but May is the top month in 12, mostly in the South and Southwest plus Alaska, and June in 9. Phoenix, Las Vegas and Albuquerque all peak in May. So does Orlando, which makes less in June and July than in March or October. Honolulu has the flattest profile, with no month below 114 kWh.</p>
<p>Why this matters: if your highest bills come in July and August, a south-facing array in Orlando is not making its most power then. Set each month of production beside the same month on your bills.</p>
<p>How uneven the year is, and what that does to credits under different billing rules, is covered in <a href="/guides/solar-panels-in-winter-by-state/">solar panels in winter by state</a>.</p>
<h2 id="step-4-scale-to-your-system-and-compare-with-your-bills">Step 4: Scale to your system and compare with your bills</h2>
<p>Multiply each month by your system&#39;s kW. A 6 kW system in Milwaukee makes about 6 × 65, or 390 kWh, in January and 6 × 158, or about 950 kWh, in July. Your meter counts the same unit, the <a href="/guides/what-is-a-kilowatt-hour/">kilowatt-hour</a>, so the comparison is direct. A month where your bill shows more kWh than the panels made is a month you still buy from the grid, even if the yearly totals match.</p>
<p>Most homes also use a large share of their power after dark, so even a month with enough production on paper sends some of it to the grid and buys some back later. How that surplus is credited decides what the production is worth; <a href="/guides/solar-daytime-electricity-use/">solar and daytime electricity use</a> shows how much it moves payback.</p>
<h2 id="step-5-turn-kwh-into-dollars">Step 5: Turn kWh into dollars</h2>
<p>A kWh is worth the price you would otherwise pay for it. With the September 2026 EIA residential average of 30.97¢ per kWh, a kW in Boston replaces about $403 of power a year if every kWh is used in the house or credited at retail. The same kW in Phoenix makes more power but, at 14.92¢, replaces about $262. The <a href="/electricity-prices/">electricity prices page</a> has the rate for every state, and the <a href="/solar-calculator/">solar calculator</a> does the full calculation, including exports, upkeep and price changes.</p>
<h2 id="step-6-allow-for-a-slow-decline">Step 6: Allow for a slow decline</h2>
<p>Panels lose a little output every year. Our model assumes 0.5% a year, the median rate in a 2012 review of nearly 2,000 field measurements by researchers at the National Renewable Energy Laboratory, now the National Laboratory of the Rockies. Your first-year figures shrink slowly from there; <a href="/guides/solar-panel-degradation-payback/">solar panel degradation and payback</a> shows what that does to savings.</p>
<h2 id="mistakes-that-cost-money">Mistakes that cost money</h2>
<ul>
<li><strong>Using a national rule of thumb.</strong> The yearly figure ranges from 922 to 1,779 kWh per kW across the table.</li>
<li><strong>Judging the system by one month.</strong> Boise&#39;s best month is 180 kWh per kW and its worst 53. Look at the full year.</li>
<li><strong>Taking a quote&#39;s production claim on trust.</strong> Ask which tool produced it and what tilt, direction, losses and shading it assumed, then compare it with PVWatts for your address.</li>
<li><strong>Assuming the roof matches our setup.</strong> East- or west-facing and steeper roofs change the numbers; see <a href="/guides/east-west-facing-solar-panels/">east- or west-facing panels</a> and <a href="/guides/solar-panel-roof-pitch/">roof pitch</a>.</li>
</ul>
<h2 id="estimate-your-own-output">Estimate your own output</h2>
<ul>
<li>Panel rating and system size from the quote, in kW</li>
<li>Your state&#39;s 12 months from the table, multiplied by your kW</li>
<li>Your best and worst months, set beside the same months on your bills</li>
<li>Price per kWh from your bill, to turn kWh into dollars</li>
<li>The installer&#39;s production tool and assumptions, checked against PVWatts</li>
</ul>
