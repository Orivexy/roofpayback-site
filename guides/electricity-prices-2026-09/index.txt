33:T11a2,<div class="short-answer"><p class="short-answer-label">What we found</p>
<p>The U.S. average residential electricity price in September 2026 was <strong>18.17¢ per kilowatt-hour (kWh)</strong>, up 0.7% from 18.05¢ a year earlier, according to the Energy Information Administration (EIA). That is the smallest year-over-year increase of the last twelve months. Over the full year to September, though, homes paid about 6% more per kWh than in the year before. The calm September number says more about September 2025 than about the trend.</p>
<p>Hawaii (+22.2%) and Idaho (+15.1%) led the increases. Connecticut (−10.9%) and Florida (−6.5%) fell the most. The latest month is preliminary, so expect small revisions.</p>

<p>The September 2026 price barely moved from August (18.16¢ to 18.17¢). A year earlier, September had jumped: from 17.57¢ in August 2025 to 18.05¢. Comparing a flat month with a month that had already spiked produces a small percentage, even though prices stayed near their 2026 level.</p>
<p>Weighting each month by how much power homes used, the average price over October 2025 to September 2026 was <strong>18.05¢</strong>, against 17.04¢ over the previous twelve months, a 5.9% rise. The average household bill over the same twelve months was $160 a month, against $149 the year before.</p>

3f:T1199,<h2 id="one-month-against-twelve-most-september-moves-hold-up-some-do-not">One month against twelve: most September moves hold up, some do not</h2>
<p>A single month can mislead in either direction. Comparing the last twelve months with the twelve before, using the same EIA-861M data:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Connecticut</strong> is the only state where the twelve-month average price fell clearly, down 9.1% to 27.2¢. Nevada was flat (−0.2%).</li>
<li><strong>Florida</strong> fell 6.5% in September but rose 2.6% over twelve months.</li>
<li><strong>New Hampshire</strong> rose only 5.2% in September but 13.3% over twelve months, to 27.06¢.</li>
<li><strong>The District of Columbia</strong> had the biggest twelve-month rise, 18.2%, followed by New Hampshire, Pennsylvania (11.9%), Maryland and Washington (both 11.7%) and Ohio (11.5%).</li>
<li><strong>Idaho</strong>&#39;s 15.1% September jump compares with a 10.8% rise over twelve months.</li>
</ul>
<p>If you are budgeting, the twelve-month change is the better guide to what happened to your bills. The September figure is the better guide to where prices stand now.</p>
<h2 id="business-and-industrial-prices-rose-faster-than-household-prices">Business and industrial prices rose faster than household prices</h2>
<p>Nationally, the commercial price was 14.27¢/kWh in September 2026, up 2.5% from 13.92¢. The industrial price was 9.52¢, up 7% from 8.9¢. The all-sector average was 14.62¢, against 14.13¢ a year earlier.</p>
<p>Industrial prices ranged from 6.18¢ in New Mexico to 40.89¢ in Hawaii. In two states, North Dakota and Nevada, industry paid slightly more than commercial customers. The state-by-state ranking of how much more homes pay than businesses is in <a href="/guides/residential-vs-business-electricity-prices/">residential vs. business electricity prices</a>.</p>
<h2 id="regions-the-islands-rose-most-the-west-coast-fell">Regions: the islands rose most, the West Coast fell</h2>
<p>EIA&#39;s census divisions moved like this in September 2026:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Pacific Noncontiguous</strong> (Alaska and Hawaii): 40.85¢, up 16.6%.</li>
<li><strong>Middle Atlantic</strong>: 25.23¢, up 5.9%.</li>
<li><strong>East North Central</strong>: 19.41¢, up 4.5%.</li>
<li><strong>New England</strong>: 29.56¢, up 0.4%, as Connecticut&#39;s drop offset increases in Maine and New Hampshire.</li>
<li><strong>South Atlantic</strong>: 16.12¢, up 0.3%.</li>
<li><strong>Pacific Contiguous</strong>: 26.34¢, down 1.5%.</li>
</ul>
<p>The cheapest division was East South Central at 14.42¢. Which states sit in each division, and how far apart they are, is in <a href="/guides/electricity-prices-by-region/">electricity prices by region</a>.</p>
<h2 id="what-it-means-for-a-household">What it means for a household</h2>
<p>A price change reaches you through the rate times the kWh you use. At the U.S. average use of 885 kWh a month, September&#39;s year-over-year change of 0.12¢ is about a dollar a month. The twelve-month rise is the one that shows on bills: from $149 to $160 a month for the average household. The <a href="/electricity-cost-calculator/">electricity cost calculator</a> lets you plug in your own state&#39;s rate and use, and <a href="/guides/average-electric-bill-by-state/">average electric bill by state</a> has the twelve-month bill for every state.</p>
<p>Price trends also feed solar math. The example home on our <a href="/methodology/">methodology</a> page assumes prices rise 2.5% a year. The past twelve months ran well above that, the latest month well below. <a href="/guides/solar-payback-if-electricity-prices-rise/">Solar payback if electricity prices rise</a> shows how much the assumption changes the answer.</p>
<h2 id="how-we-measured-it">How we measured it</h2>
<p>State and sector prices are EIA&#39;s average prices to ultimate customers for September 2026 from the Electric Power Monthly (Table 5.6.A), compared with September 2025. The monthly series and twelve-month averages come from EIA-861M, where each state&#39;s price is revenue divided by kWh sold to residential customers; the twelve-month averages weight each month by its kWh. Months from January 2026 are preliminary. Prices are nominal, not adjusted for inflation. Every state&#39;s current price is on the <a href="/electricity-prices/">electricity prices</a> page, and <a href="/guides/how-electricity-prices-changed-in-ten-years/">how electricity prices changed in ten years</a> covers the long view.</p>
