Electricity Prices in September 2026: Winners and Losers

U.S. home electricity averaged 18.17¢/kWh in September 2026, up just 0.7%, the smallest gain in a year. The 12-month view and every big state mover.

Published November 25, 2026·Updated November 28, 2026·5 min read·RoofPayback editorial·Figures checked against EIA and PVWatts data

What we found

The U.S. average residential electricity price in September 2026 was 18.17¢ per kilowatt-hour (kWh), up 0.7% from 18.05¢ a year earlier, according to the Energy Information Administration (EIA). That is the smallest year-over-year increase of the last twelve months. Over the full year to September, though, homes paid about 6% more per kWh than in the year before. The calm September number says more about September 2025 than about the trend.

Hawaii (+22.2%) and Idaho (+15.1%) led the increases. Connecticut (−10.9%) and Florida (−6.5%) fell the most. The latest month is preliminary, so expect small revisions.

The slowdown is mostly a high starting point

The table lists each month's national residential price and its change from the same month a year earlier, from EIA's monthly survey of utilities (EIA-861M).

September 2026 18.17¢ 0.7%

The September 2026 price barely moved from August (18.16¢ to 18.17¢). A year earlier, September had jumped: from 17.57¢ in August 2025 to 18.05¢. Comparing a flat month with a month that had already spiked produces a small percentage, even though prices stayed near their 2026 level.

Weighting each month by how much power homes used, the average price over October 2025 to September 2026 was 18.05¢, against 17.04¢ over the previous twelve months, a 5.9% rise. The average household bill over the same twelve months was $160 a month, against $149 the year before.

Hawaii, Idaho, Maine and South Dakota led the increases

Hawaii's 48.31¢ is the highest price in the country, and it was not Hawaii's peak for the year: its monthly price reached 52.72¢ in June 2026. The reasons the islands pay so much more are covered in why electricity is expensive in Hawaii.

The list of risers mixes cheap and expensive states. Idaho (14.39¢) and South Dakota (16.31¢) still sit below the national average after their jumps. Maine is the opposite case: at 31.29¢ it is the second most expensive state, and it still rose 12.2%.

Seven more places rose by more than 8%: Michigan and the District of Columbia (both 9.2%), Ohio (8.9%), Pennsylvania and Washington (both 8.7%), Colorado (8.4%) and Louisiana (8.1%).

On the way down, Connecticut fell from 30.41¢ to 27.09¢, the largest drop. Florida slid to 14.74¢, Utah to 13.41¢ and California to 30.86¢, with Arizona (−2.2%) and Oregon (−1.9%) also lower. Nevada, Tennessee and Wyoming changed by 0.2% or less.

One month against twelve: most September moves hold up, some do not

A single month can mislead in either direction. Comparing the last twelve months with the twelve before, using the same EIA-861M data:

  • Connecticut is the only state where the twelve-month average price fell clearly, down 9.1% to 27.2¢. Nevada was flat (−0.2%).
  • Florida fell 6.5% in September but rose 2.6% over twelve months.
  • New Hampshire rose only 5.2% in September but 13.3% over twelve months, to 27.06¢.
  • The District of Columbia had the biggest twelve-month rise, 18.2%, followed by New Hampshire, Pennsylvania (11.9%), Maryland and Washington (both 11.7%) and Ohio (11.5%).
  • Idaho's 15.1% September jump compares with a 10.8% rise over twelve months.

If you are budgeting, the twelve-month change is the better guide to what happened to your bills. The September figure is the better guide to where prices stand now.

Business and industrial prices rose faster than household prices

Nationally, the commercial price was 14.27¢/kWh in September 2026, up 2.5% from 13.92¢. The industrial price was 9.52¢, up 7% from 8.9¢. The all-sector average was 14.62¢, against 14.13¢ a year earlier.

Industrial prices ranged from 6.18¢ in New Mexico to 40.89¢ in Hawaii. In two states, North Dakota and Nevada, industry paid slightly more than commercial customers. The state-by-state ranking of how much more homes pay than businesses is in residential vs. business electricity prices.

Regions: the islands rose most, the West Coast fell

EIA's census divisions moved like this in September 2026:

  • Pacific Noncontiguous (Alaska and Hawaii): 40.85¢, up 16.6%.
  • Middle Atlantic: 25.23¢, up 5.9%.
  • East North Central: 19.41¢, up 4.5%.
  • New England: 29.56¢, up 0.4%, as Connecticut's drop offset increases in Maine and New Hampshire.
  • South Atlantic: 16.12¢, up 0.3%.
  • Pacific Contiguous: 26.34¢, down 1.5%.

The cheapest division was East South Central at 14.42¢. Which states sit in each division, and how far apart they are, is in electricity prices by region.

What it means for a household

A price change reaches you through the rate times the kWh you use. At the U.S. average use of 885 kWh a month, September's year-over-year change of 0.12¢ is about a dollar a month. The twelve-month rise is the one that shows on bills: from $149 to $160 a month for the average household. The electricity cost calculator lets you plug in your own state's rate and use, and average electric bill by state has the twelve-month bill for every state.

Price trends also feed solar math. The example home on our methodology page assumes prices rise 2.5% a year. The past twelve months ran well above that, the latest month well below. Solar payback if electricity prices rise shows how much the assumption changes the answer.

How we measured it

State and sector prices are EIA's average prices to ultimate customers for September 2026 from the Electric Power Monthly (Table 5.6.A), compared with September 2025. The monthly series and twelve-month averages come from EIA-861M, where each state's price is revenue divided by kWh sold to residential customers; the twelve-month averages weight each month by its kWh. Months from January 2026 are preliminary. Prices are nominal, not adjusted for inflation. Every state's current price is on the electricity prices page, and how electricity prices changed in ten years covers the long view.
