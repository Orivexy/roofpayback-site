Solar Payback If Electricity Prices Keep Rising

How flat, 2.5% and 4% yearly price growth change solar payback, and what each state's own price trend since 2016 would mean if it continued.

Published October 27, 2026·Updated November 28, 2026·5 min read·RoofPayback editorial·Figures checked against EIA and PVWatts data

Official data: EIA · DOE · IRS · how we check

The answer up front

Rising electricity prices shorten solar payback, because each kWh the panels replace is worth more every year. In our example home, going from flat prices to 4% a year cut payback by about four years in Texas, Florida and Arizona and by about a year and a half in New York.

The past is a poor guide to the size of the effect. Over the last nine years, average residential prices grew about 3.9% a year nationally, but from 1.5% a year in Kansas to 7.6% in the District of Columbia. Plugging a state's own trend into the model moves payback anywhere from 2.6 years earlier (Washington) to about a year later (Nevada) than our standard 2.5% assumption.

The house and the three price paths

The house is our example home: 7 kW of panels at $24,500, 10,500 kWh a year, no incentives, with PVWatts output and September 2026 EIA residential prices (methodology). Only the yearly price change varies. Payback is the number of years until savings equal the cost; the 25-year gain is total savings minus cost and upkeep, in dollars of each year (no inflation adjustment).

State 0% a year 2.5% a year 4% a year
New York 10.2 years, $33,518 9.1 years, $55,158 8.6 years, $72,801
Arizona 16.7 years, $12,165 14 years, $26,512 12.9 years, $38,242
Texas 17.3 years, $10,289 14.3 years, $23,624 13.1 years, $34,494
Florida 17.2 years, $10,381 14.3 years, $23,797 13.1 years, $34,736

All four use full retail credit for exports. Where exports earn less, every figure is longer; net metering vs. net billing shows by how much.

Two patterns:

  • Slow-payback states gain the most years. In Texas the first step, from 0% to 2.5%, saves three years; the next step, to 4%, saves 1.2 more. Each extra point of growth buys back less time.
  • Fast-payback states gain the most dollars. New York gains only 1.6 years between 0% and 4%, but its 25-year gain more than doubles.

What the last nine years actually looked like

EIA's monthly utility data (Form EIA-861M) gives the average residential price for every state. Comparing the 12 months to September 2017 with the 12 months to September 2026, and converting the change to a yearly rate:

  • Fastest: District of Columbia 7.6% a year (12.8¢ to 24.6¢), Maine 7.2% (16¢ to 29.9¢), Rhode Island 5.6%, New York 5.3%, Hawaii 5.1%.
  • Middle: the U.S. average, 3.9% a year (12.8¢ to 18.1¢); Texas 4.3%; Florida 3.3%.
  • Slowest: Kansas 1.5% a year, Nevada and Iowa 1.6%, Nebraska 1.7%, North Dakota 1.9%.

These are nominal prices, before inflation, and the months from January 2026 are preliminary. Using 12-month averages rather than single months keeps one unusual month from distorting the result. How electricity prices changed in ten years looks at the same history state by state.

If your state's trend continued

Here is the example home with each state's own nine-year rate in place of 2.5%, next to the flat and standard cases:

Maine 7.2% 9 years 8.2 years 7.1 years
New York 5.3% 10.2 years 9.1 years 8.3 years
Washington 4.5% not within 25 years 19.2 years 16.6 years
Illinois 4.4% 15.4 years 13 years 11.8 years
Texas 4.3% 17.3 years 14.3 years 13 years
Michigan 3.7% 12.7 years 11.1 years 10.5 years
Louisiana 3.5% 20.2 years 16.2 years 15.2 years
Florida 3.3% 17.2 years 14.3 years 13.6 years
Idaho 2.8% 18.8 years 15.4 years 15 years
Arizona 2.7% 16.7 years 14 years 13.8 years
Utah 2.1% 19.9 years 16.1 years 16.5 years
North Dakota 1.9% 21.7 years 17.2 years 18 years
Nevada 1.6% 19 years 15.5 years 16.6 years

Full retail credit in every row. In Louisiana that is an upper bound: the Public Service Commission credits exports from systems installed after 2019 at avoided cost, so the real paybacks there are longer.

Two cautions. First, the states at the bottom of the list grew more slowly than our 2.5% assumption, so for them the standard case is the optimistic one: Nevada moves from 15.5 to 16.6 years. Second, a fast past trend is not a forecast. Maine's 7.2% a year, held for 25 years, would multiply the price more than fivefold.

Washington: where the price path decides

Washington is the clearest case of a system that depends on price growth. Seattle's panels make 1,088 kWh per kW and power cost 15¢ in September 2026. At flat prices, the example never pays back within 25 years, losing $90 over the period. At 2.5% a year it pays back in 19.2 years, and at Washington's own recent rate, 16.6. Anyone buying solar there is, in effect, betting on rates continuing to rise.

Running the story for your own home

  1. Find your current price on your bill or the electricity prices page.
  2. Enter your system size and quote in the solar calculator.
  3. Run it at 0% first. If the system only pays back with rising prices, treat it as a riskier purchase.
  4. Then try your state's own trend and 2.5%, and see how far apart they land.
  5. Ask your utility how exports are credited and whether rates vary by time of day.

Why real price paths are bumpier

  • Prices move in steps. Some years jump and others stay flat or fall; the September 2026 price report shows recent moves.
  • No incentives are included. A state or utility rebate would shorten every payback above.
  • Financing is not included. Loan interest lengthens payback; see solar loan vs. cash.
  • The quote. The example uses $3.50 per watt; solar panel cost by state shows how a different price changes the result.

If the numbers work at flat prices, rising prices are a bonus. If they only work at your state's best decade, the purchase is a bet on that decade repeating.
