37:T78b,<div class="short-answer"><h2 id="the-answer-up-front">The answer up front</h2>
<p>When electricity prices rise, solar pays back sooner and earns more over 25 years. In our example home, going from flat prices (0% a year) to 4% a year cut payback by roughly three to four years in Texas, Florida and Arizona. The 25-year gain more than doubled or tripled in those states. California and New York, where solar already pays back faster, move less in years but a lot in dollars.</p>
<p>These are modeled estimates, not forecasts. You can try your own numbers in the <a href="/solar-calculator/">solar calculator</a>.</p>
</div><h2 id="meet-the-house">Meet the house</h2>
<p>The house is our 7 kW example home with no incentives (<a href="/methodology/">methodology</a>), with panel output from PVWatts for one reference city per state. Its starting electricity price is the EIA average residential price for August 2026.</p>
<p>We kept the house the same and changed one thing: how fast the price of electricity rises each year. We ran it three ways: 0% (flat prices), 2.5% a year (our base case) and 4% a year.</p>
<p>Two terms come up throughout. <strong>Payback</strong> is the number of years until total savings equal the cost. The <strong>25-year gain</strong> is total savings over 25 years minus the cost and upkeep, before any financing costs.</p>
<h2 id="why-the-price-path-matters-to-this-house">Why the price path matters to this house</h2>
<p>Panels on this roof don&#39;t sell power. They replace power the household would otherwise buy. Each kWh they cover saves the price of that kWh.</p>
<p>The $24,500 is paid on day one and never changes. If the price of grid power climbs every year, the same output is worth more each year. Savings catch up with the cost sooner, then keep piling up after that. For the full mechanics, see <a href="/guides/solar-payback-period-explained/">solar payback period explained</a>.</p>
39:T1422,<h2 id="if-prices-stay-flat-the-house-in-texas">If prices stay flat: the house in Texas</h2>
<p>Start with the toughest case. The house sits in Texas, where electricity is cheaper than in many states, and prices never rise.</p>
<p>It takes 17.3 years for the savings to cover the system. By year 25, the gain is $10,289. That is positive, but thin. A higher installed price, a shaded roof or a less favorable export rule could wipe it out. That fragility is typical of low-price states, as the guide to <a href="/guides/is-solar-worth-it-where-electricity-is-cheap/">solar where electricity is cheap</a> shows.</p>
<p>Florida looks almost the same at 0%: 16.9 years and $11,015. Arizona does slightly better at 16.6 years and $12,506.</p>
<h2 id="if-prices-rise-2-5-a-year-the-base-case">If prices rise 2.5% a year: the base case</h2>
<p>Now the Texas house faces steady price growth. Payback shortens to 14.3 years. The 25-year gain climbs to $23,624.</p>
<p>Florida moves to 14.1 years and $24,658. Arizona reaches 13.9 years and $26,978. The house didn&#39;t change. Only the value of each kWh it saves did.</p>
<h2 id="if-prices-rise-4-a-year-the-fast-case">If prices rise 4% a year: the fast case</h2>
<p>Faster growth helps again, but by less. In Texas, payback drops to 13.1 years and the gain reaches $34,494. Arizona gets to 12.8 years and $38,811. Florida lands at 13 years and $35,783.</p>
<p>Notice the shape. In Texas, the first step (0% to 2.5%) cut payback from 17.3 to 14.3 years. The second (2.5% to 4%) only took it to 13.1. Each step of extra growth buys back less time than the one before.</p>
<h2 id="moving-the-same-house-to-california-and-new-york">Moving the same house to California and New York</h2>
<p>Pick the house up and set it down in California. Prices there are higher to begin with, so payback starts short: 8.1 years at 0%. At 4%, it only falls to 7.1 years.</p>
<p>The dollars tell a different story. The 25-year gain jumps from $51,055 at flat prices to $102,994 at 4%. When yearly savings are already large, a rising price multiplies them.</p>
<p>New York follows the same path. Payback moves from 10.1 to 8.6 years, while the 25-year gain grows from $34,008 to $73,608.</p>

<h2 id="running-the-story-for-your-own-home">Running the story for your own home</h2>
<ol>
<li>Find your utility&#39;s current residential price on the <a href="/electricity-prices/">electricity prices page</a>.</li>
<li>Enter your own system size and quote in the <a href="/solar-calculator/">solar calculator</a>.</li>
<li>Test the 0% case first. If solar only works when prices rise fast, treat it as a riskier bet.</li>
<li>Ask your utility how it credits exported power and whether rates change by time of day.</li>
<li>Compare at least a few written quotes.</li>
</ol>
<h2 id="what-would-change-the-result">What would change the result</h2>
<ul>
<li><strong>A bumpy price path.</strong> Real prices don&#39;t rise smoothly. Some years jump, some stay flat. Recent state-by-state moves are in the <a href="/guides/electricity-prices-2026-08/">August 2026 price report</a>, but they do not predict the future.</li>
<li><strong>Incentives.</strong> The example uses none, since the federal 30% residential credit ended for systems placed in service after December 31, 2025. A state or utility rebate, listed in DSIRE, would shorten every payback in the table. More in <a href="/guides/is-solar-worth-it-without-tax-credit/">is solar worth it without the tax credit</a>.</li>
<li><strong>Export rules.</strong> How your utility credits power you send to the grid can change savings a lot. See <a href="/guides/net-metering-vs-net-billing-payback/">net metering vs net billing payback</a>.</li>
<li><strong>Your quote.</strong> The example uses $3,500 per kW, or $3.50 per watt. A higher quote lengthens payback at every price path; see <a href="/guides/solar-panel-cost-by-state/">solar panel cost by state</a>.</li>
<li><strong>Financing.</strong> Loan interest is not included in these results.</li>
</ul>
<p>Because no one knows the future price path, the flat-price case is the safest test. If the numbers work there, rising prices are a bonus rather than the whole plan.</p>
