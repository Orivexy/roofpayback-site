32:Tdf7,<div class="short-answer"><p class="short-answer-label">The answer up front</p>
<p>Rising electricity prices shorten solar payback, because each kWh the panels replace is worth more every year. In our example home, going from flat prices to 4% a year cut payback by about four years in Texas, Florida and Arizona and by about a year and a half in New York.</p>
<p>The past is a poor guide to the size of the effect. Over the last nine years, average residential prices grew about 3.9% a year nationally, but from 1.5% a year in Kansas to 7.6% in the District of Columbia. Plugging a state&#39;s own trend into the model moves payback anywhere from 2.6 years earlier (Washington) to about a year later (Nevada) than our standard 2.5% assumption.</p>
</div><h2 id="the-house-and-the-three-price-paths">The house and the three price paths</h2>
<p>The house is our example home: 7 kW of panels at $24,500, 10,500 kWh a year, no incentives, with PVWatts output and September 2026 EIA residential prices (<a href="/methodology/">methodology</a>). <p>Two patterns:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Slow-payback states gain the most years.</strong> In Texas the first step, from 0% to 2.5%, saves three years; the next step, to 4%, saves 1.2 more. Each extra point of growth buys back less time.</li>
<li><strong>Fast-payback states gain the most dollars.</strong> New York gains only 1.6 years between 0% and 4%, but its 25-year gain more than doubles.</li>
</ul>
<h2 id="what-the-last-nine-years-actually-looked-like">What the last nine years actually looked like</h2>
<p>EIA&#39;s monthly utility data (Form EIA-861M) gives the average residential price for every state. Comparing the 12 months to September 2017 with the 12 months to September 2026, and converting the change to a yearly rate:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Fastest:</strong> District of Columbia 7.6% a year (12.8¢ to 24.6¢), Maine 7.2% (16¢ to 29.9¢), Rhode Island 5.6%, New York 5.3%, Hawaii 5.1%.</li>
<li><strong>Middle:</strong> the U.S. average, 3.9% a year (12.8¢ to 18.1¢); Texas 4.3%; Florida 3.3%.</li>
<li><strong>Slowest:</strong> Kansas 1.5% a year, Nevada and Iowa 1.6%, Nebraska 1.7%, North Dakota 1.9%.</li>
</ul>
<p>These are nominal prices, before inflation, and the months from January 2026 are preliminary. Using 12-month averages rather than single months keeps one unusual month from distorting the result. <a href="/guides/how-electricity-prices-changed-in-ten-years/">How electricity prices changed in ten years</a> looks at the same history state by state.</p>

<h2 id="washington-where-the-price-path-decides">Washington: where the price path decides</h2>
<p>Washington is the clearest case of a system that depends on price growth. Seattle&#39;s panels make 1,088 kWh per kW and power cost 15¢ in September 2026. At flat prices, the example never pays back within 25 years, losing $90 over the period. At 2.5% a year it pays back in 19.2 years, and at Washington&#39;s own recent rate, 16.6. Anyone buying solar there is, in effect, betting on rates continuing to rise.</p>
<h2 id="running-the-story-for-your-own-home">Running the story for your own home</h2>
<ol>
<li>Find your current price on your bill or the <a href="/electricity-prices/">electricity prices page</a>.</li>
<li>Enter your system size and quote in the <a href="/solar-calculator/">solar calculator</a>.</li>
<li>Run it at 0% first. If the system only pays back with rising prices, treat it as a riskier purchase.</li>
<li>Then try your state&#39;s own trend and 2.5%, and see how far apart they land.</li>
<li>Ask your utility how exports are credited and whether rates vary by time of day.</li>
</ol>
<h2 id="why-real-price-paths-are-bumpier">Why real price paths are bumpier</h2>
<ul>
<li><strong>Prices move in steps.</strong> Some years jump and others stay flat or fall; <a href="/guides/electricity-prices-2026-09/">the September 2026 price report</a> shows recent moves.</li>
<li><strong>No incentives are included.</strong> A state or utility rebate would shorten every payback above.</li>
<li><strong>Financing is not included.</strong> Loan interest lengthens payback; see <a href="/guides/solar-loan-vs-cash/">solar loan vs. cash</a>.</li>
<li><strong>The quote.</strong> The example uses $3.50 per watt; <a href="/guides/solar-panel-cost-by-state/">solar panel cost by state</a> shows how a different price changes the result.</li>
</ul>
<p>If the numbers work at flat prices, rising prices are a bonus. If they only work at your state&#39;s best decade, the purchase is a bet on that decade repeating.</p>
