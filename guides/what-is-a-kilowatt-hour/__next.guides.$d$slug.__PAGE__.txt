22:T905,<div class="short-answer"><h2 id="plain-english-answer">Plain-English answer</h2>
<p>A kilowatt-hour (kWh) is how your electric company measures the electricity you use. One kWh means one kilowatt of power used for one hour. Your bill is mostly a count of these units times a price.</p>
<p>That price swings widely across the country. In August 2026, the average home price ran from 12.72¢ per kWh in Nevada to 47.18¢ per kWh in Hawaii, according to the U.S. Energy Information Administration (EIA). Your own cost also hinges on your utility&#39;s rate plan.</p>
</div><h2 id="how-it-works">How it works</h2>
<p>Electricity has two sides worth keeping apart: how hard something pulls power right now, and how much energy it uses over time.</p>
<p>The first is <strong>power</strong>, measured in watts. A bulb, a fridge or a heater each draws a certain number of watts while it runs. A kilowatt is simply a bigger unit. As the EIA puts it, &quot;One kilowatt is equal to 1,000 Watts.&quot;</p>
<p>The second is <strong>energy</strong>, measured in watt-hours or kilowatt-hours. You get it by multiplying power by time. The EIA gives this example: &quot;One kWh is one kilowatt generated for one hour. For example, if you use a 40-Watt (0.04 kW) light bulb for five hours, you have used 200 Wh, or 0.2 kWh, of electrical energy.&quot;</p>
<p>So a small device left on for many hours can use as much energy as a big one switched on briefly. Your bill cares about the total, not the peak.</p>
<h2 id="picture-it-as-a-road-trip">Picture it as a road trip</h2>
<p>A car makes this easier to see.</p>
<ul>
<li>Your <strong>speed</strong> is like watts. It tells you how fast you are going at one moment.</li>
<li>The <strong>miles on the odometer</strong> are like kilowatt-hours. They show how far you actually went.</li>
<li>The <strong>price per kWh</strong> is like a toll per mile.</li>
</ul>
<p>Nobody charges you for driving fast for a second. You pay for the distance covered. Electricity works the same way: the utility bills you for the kWh you piled up during the month.</p>
<p>The analogy also explains a common surprise. A space heater may &quot;go fast&quot; (high watts), but if it runs only a short while, the miles stay low. A modest device that never turns off keeps adding miles all month.</p>
24:T1b6f,<h2 id="how-your-utility-counts-your-kwh">How your utility counts your kWh</h2>
<p>A meter on your home tallies kWh like that odometer. In the EIA&#39;s words, &quot;Electric utility companies use meters to measure how much electricity their customers use.&quot; Older meters were read by hand. Many utilities now use smart meters that send readings automatically.</p>
<p>At the end of each billing period, the utility multiplies your kWh by your <strong>rate</strong>, the price per kWh it charges. According to the EIA, &quot;Most consumers pay prices based on the seasonal average cost of providing electricity,&quot; and &quot;Some utilities offer their customers time-of-day pricing to encourage electricity conservation and to reduce peak demand for electricity.&quot; <a href="/guides/time-of-use-electricity-rates-explained/">Time-of-use rates explained</a> shows how those plans work.</p>
<p>For scale, the EIA reports that in 2022, &quot;the average annual amount of electricity sold to (purchased by) a U.S. residential electric-utility customer was 10,791 kilowatthours (kWh), an average of about 899 kWh per month.&quot; Home size, climate, heating and cooling, and charging an electric car at home can push your own number well above or below that. Your bill, or a call to your utility, shows the real figure.</p>

<p>Same 900 kWh, very different bills. Hawaii, California and much of New England sit far above much of the South and Mountain West. Prices also shift year to year: Hawaii&#39;s was up 22.5%, while Connecticut&#39;s was down 18.8%.</p>
<p>Every state is on our <a href="/electricity-prices/">electricity prices by state</a> page, and the <a href="/guides/electricity-prices-2026-08/">August 2026 electricity price update</a> covers that month in detail. You can also go straight to <a href="/electricity-prices/california/">California</a>, <a href="/electricity-prices/texas/">Texas</a> or <a href="/electricity-prices/florida/">Florida</a>.</p>
<h3>Why the price differs from place to place</h3>
<p>The EIA explains that &quot;Electricity prices generally reflect the cost to build, finance, maintain, and operate power plants and the electricity grid.&quot; Fuel, power lines, weather and state rules all feed in. Some states have utility commissions that fully regulate prices; others mix regulated and unregulated pricing.</p>
<p>Season plays a part as well. &quot;Prices are usually highest in the summer when total demand is high because more expensive generation sources are added to meet the increased demand,&quot; the EIA notes.</p>
<h2 id="where-people-get-confused">Where people get confused</h2>
<p><strong>&quot;kW and kWh are the same thing.&quot;</strong> They are not. A kW is a rate of power at one moment. A kWh is energy used over time. Solar adds to the mix-up, because a system&#39;s size is given in kW while its yearly output is counted in kWh.</p>
<p><strong>&quot;The state average is my price.&quot;</strong> The figures above are averages, and the latest months are preliminary, so EIA can revise them. To estimate your own price, divide the energy charges on your bill by the kWh you used, or look up your utility&#39;s rate schedule.</p>
<p><strong>&quot;Every kWh my solar panels make is worth the same.&quot;</strong> A solar kWh you use at home saves you the full price of a grid kWh. A kWh you send back to the grid is worth whatever your utility&#39;s rules say. Under net metering, exports earn a bill credit; under net billing, they may be credited at a different rate. These rules and incentive programs vary by state and utility, and DSIRE keeps a database of them. Our guide on <a href="/guides/net-metering-vs-net-billing-payback/">net metering vs. net billing</a> sets the two side by side.</p>
<p>Because the solar savings come down to kWh and price, a higher price per kWh makes each self-used solar kWh worth more. The <a href="/solar-calculator/">solar calculator</a> lets you test that with your own use and local price. It draws on PVWatts production estimates, and our <a href="/methodology/">methodology</a> page lays out the assumptions.</p>
<h2 id="quick-glossary">Quick glossary</h2>
<ul>
<li><strong>Watt (W):</strong> a unit of power; how much electricity a device draws at a given moment.</li>
<li><strong>Kilowatt (kW):</strong> 1,000 watts. Also how solar system size is usually stated.</li>
<li><strong>Kilowatt-hour (kWh):</strong> a unit of energy; one kilowatt used for one hour. The unit on your bill.</li>
<li><strong>Rate:</strong> the price per kWh your utility charges.</li>
<li><strong>Smart meter:</strong> a meter that sends your usage to the utility automatically.</li>
<li><strong>Time-of-day pricing:</strong> a plan where the price per kWh changes by the hour.</li>
<li><strong>Net metering:</strong> a setup where solar power you export earns a credit on your bill.</li>
</ul>
<h2 id="the-short-version">The short version</h2>
<p>A kilowatt-hour is one kilowatt of power used for one hour, and it is what your utility bills you for. In August 2026, the EIA average home price ranged from 12.72¢ per kWh in Nevada to 47.18¢ per kWh in Hawaii. Your true cost per kWh is on your own bill, since fixed fees and rate plans differ by utility. If you are weighing solar, that price is one of the most important numbers in the decision.</p>
