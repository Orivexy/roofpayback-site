23:T932,<div class="short-answer"><h2 id="short-answer">Short answer</h2>
<p>A bigger electric bill does not automatically mean a faster solar payback. In our math, higher-use homes did get a slightly shorter payback, but the gain was small. In Texas, payback was 14.9 years for a 6000 kWh/yr home and 14.1 years for a 16000 kWh/yr home. The bigger gain from high use is larger yearly savings in dollars, not a much faster payback.</p>
<p>What matters most is your local electricity price, how much sun your roof gets, and what the system costs. Your own utility quote can change every number below.</p>
</div><h2 id="why-usage-alone-does-not-set-payback">Why usage alone does not set payback</h2>
<p>Payback is the installed cost divided by the money the system saves each year. A kilowatt-hour (kWh) is the unit your utility bills you for. If you use more kWh, you need a bigger system to cover them. A bigger system costs more.</p>
<p>So cost and savings both grow with usage. That is why payback barely moves. A home that uses twice as much power needs roughly twice the panels, and it saves roughly twice as much.</p>
<p>Price per kWh and sun matter more. That is why California looks so different from Texas below. See our <a href="/guides/solar-payback-period-explained/">solar payback period explained</a> guide for the basic math.</p>
<h2 id="how-we-compared-small-average-and-large-homes">How we compared small, average and large homes</h2>
<p>We used right-sized systems, meaning the system size is matched to yearly use and local solar output. These are the assumptions:</p>
<ul>
<li>Installed cost of $3500/kW, with no incentives.</li>
<li>$100/yr maintenance.</li>
<li>Electricity price rising 2.5%/yr.</li>
<li>Panel output falling 0.5%/yr from degradation.</li>
<li>A 25-year horizon.</li>
<li>40% of use happens while the sun is up.</li>
<li>Export credits are capped at a year of grid purchases (an annual true-up).</li>
<li>Production from PVWatts v8, south-facing, 20° pitch, 14% losses, one reference city per state.</li>
<li>Prices from the EIA August 2026 average residential data.</li>
</ul>
<p>The 14000 and 16000 kWh/yr homes include an EV charging assumption of +3500 kWh/yr. That is an assumption, not a measurement. Our <a href="/guides/home-ev-charging-cost-by-state/">home EV charging cost guide</a> covers it in more detail.</p>

<p>These are estimates from a model, not quotes. They are not a promise of savings.</p>
<h2 id="what-the-table-shows">What the table shows</h2>
<p><strong>Savings in dollars grow with usage.</strong> In Texas, year-1 savings rise from $905 to $2488 as use goes up. That is real money, even though payback barely changes.</p>
<p><strong>Payback improves a little, then flattens.</strong> In Texas and Arizona, the last two homes have the same or nearly the same payback. Once a system is sized to the home, adding more use mostly scales everything up in step.</p>
<p><strong>The state matters far more than the usage.</strong> California&#39;s payback is about half of Texas&#39;s for every usage level. The reason is price per kWh. Each kWh a panel makes is worth more where the grid charges more. Check <a href="/electricity-prices/">electricity prices by state</a> and the <a href="/guides/best-states-for-solar-payback/">best states for solar payback</a>.</p>
<p><strong>Small homes are not shut out.</strong> The lowest-use home in Arizona still shows a payback of 13.2 years in this model, close to the larger homes.</p>
<h2 id="things-that-can-change-your-result">Things that can change your result</h2>
<ul>
<li><strong>Net metering rules.</strong> Programs vary by state and utility. DSIRE keeps a database of current programs at <a href="https://programs.dsireusa.org/">programs.dsireusa.org</a>. Read <a href="/guides/net-metering-vs-net-billing-payback/">net metering vs. net billing payback</a> before you compare quotes.</li>
<li><strong>Oversizing.</strong> In this model, export credits are capped at a year of grid purchases. A system bigger than your use may earn little for the extra output. See the <a href="/guides/oversized-solar-system-annual-true-up/">oversized system and annual true-up guide</a>.</li>
<li><strong>Time of use.</strong> If your utility charges more at certain hours, the value of solar output changes. Our <a href="/guides/time-of-use-electricity-rates-explained/">time-of-use rates guide</a> explains how.</li>
<li><strong>Daytime use.</strong> Our model assumes 40% of use happens while the sun is up. Homes that use more power in the day can do better. See <a href="/guides/solar-daytime-electricity-use/">solar daytime electricity use</a>.</li>
<li><strong>Installed price.</strong> Berkeley Lab reports a median installed price of $4.0 per watt for host-owned residential systems installed in 2024, with a 20th to 80th percentile range of $3.0 to $5.2 per watt, in 2024 dollars, before incentives. Our $3500/kW assumption is $3.5 per watt, in line with the cash-purchase median Berkeley Lab reports. Loan-financed systems had a median of $4.7 per watt. Read more in <a href="/guides/solar-cost-per-watt-payback/">solar cost per watt and payback</a>.</li>
<li><strong>Incentives.</strong> The 30% federal Residential Clean Energy Credit (Section 25D) is not available for property placed in service after December 31, 2025, according to the IRS. Our model includes no incentives. Local or utility programs may still exist, so check your state.</li>
<li><strong>Rising prices.</strong> We assume electricity prices rise 2.5%/yr. If they rise faster or slower, payback shifts. See <a href="/guides/solar-payback-if-electricity-prices-rise/">solar payback if electricity prices rise</a>.</li>
</ul>
<h2 id="steps-to-take-if-your-bill-is-high">Steps to take if your bill is high</h2>
<ol>
<li><strong>Look at your last 12 months of bills.</strong> Find your total kWh for the year, not just one summer month.</li>
<li><strong>Cut waste first.</strong> Some savings cost nothing. Our guide on <a href="/guides/how-to-lower-your-electric-bill/">how to lower your electric bill</a> is a good start.</li>
<li><strong>Run your numbers.</strong> Use the <a href="/solar-calculator/">solar calculator</a> with your own usage and price.</li>
<li><strong>Get several written quotes.</strong> Compare price per watt, system size and what happens to extra output.</li>
<li><strong>Check your state page.</strong> Start from <a href="/solar-panels/">solar panels by state</a> for local context.</li>
</ol>
<p>PVWatts is a free production estimator from the U.S. Department of Energy&#39;s National Laboratory of the Rockies (formerly NREL). You can use it to check a quote&#39;s production claim. Our <a href="/methodology/">methodology</a> page explains how we build our own estimates.</p>
<h2 id="bottom-line">Bottom line</h2>
<p>A high bill gives you more to save, so yearly dollar savings can be large. But payback depends mostly on your local price per kWh, your sun, and the system price. In our model, going from a small home to a large home changed payback by less than a year or so in Texas, Florida and Arizona, and by under one year in California.</p>
<p>If you use a lot of power, solar can still be worth a close look. Just do not assume a bigger bill means a faster payback. Compare real quotes and run your own numbers first.</p>
