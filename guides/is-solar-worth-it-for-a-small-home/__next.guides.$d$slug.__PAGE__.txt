24:Ta4a,<div class="short-answer"><h2 id="the-answer">The answer</h2>
<p>Yes, a small home can still come out ahead, but the margin shrinks as your bill does. A household using 6,000 kWh a year (kWh, or kilowatt-hour, is the unit your meter counts) pays back in <strong>7.6 years</strong> in California but <strong>14.9 years</strong> in Texas, using a right-sized system with no incentives. The state you live in matters far more than the size of your house.</p>
</div><h2 id="how-big-a-system-does-a-6-000-kwh-home-actually-need">How big a system does a 6,000 kWh home actually need?</h2>
<p>Much smaller than the example home. A right-sized system produces about what you use in a year, so the size is your yearly kWh divided by what one kilowatt of panels makes where you live.</p>
<p>In Texas, Florida and California the 6,000 kWh home lands at 4 kW, which is <strong>$14,000</strong> at $3,500 per kW. In Arizona, where each kW makes more, it is 3 kW, or $10,500. The example home in our <a href="/methodology/">methodology</a> uses 7 kW and 10,500 kWh a year, so this is roughly the cost of a smaller package, not a different kind of purchase.</p>
<p>Fewer panels also means an easier fit. A 3 or 4 kW array needs less roof, which helps if one good south-facing plane is all you have. Our <a href="/guides/how-many-solar-panels-do-i-need/">panel-count guide</a> shows how to size it.</p>
<h2 id="does-a-smaller-bill-mean-a-slower-payback">Does a smaller bill mean a slower payback?</h2>
<p>Slightly, in most places. The table compares payback years for three right-sized homes. Each one is assumed to have no incentives and to get full credit for power sent back to the grid.</p>

32:T108f,<h2 id="how-much-will-a-low-use-home-save-in-the-first-year">How much will a low-use home save in the first year?</h2>
<p>Not a lot of dollars in a cheap-power state, and a healthy amount in an expensive one. Year-one savings for the 6,000 kWh home are <strong>$905</strong> in Texas, $899 in Florida and $792 in Arizona. In California they are <strong>$1,785</strong>, because power costs far more there (29.75¢ per kWh in Los Angeles against 15.91¢ in Houston).</p>
<p>Those dollars are what the system keeps off your bill. They do not include any fixed monthly charge your utility adds, which panels cannot reduce. Ask your utility what that charge is before you sign, because a larger fixed fee eats into what a small home can save. Our guide to <a href="/guides/time-of-use-electricity-rates-explained/">time-of-use rates</a> covers another bill detail worth checking.</p>
<h2 id="who-should-skip-solar">Who should skip solar?</h2>
<p>Homes in states with low electricity prices should look hard at the numbers first. Using the 7 kW example home with full credit, payback runs <strong>16.7 years</strong> in Louisiana (12.87¢ per kWh), 16.4 years in Idaho, 18.3 years in North Dakota and 19.6 years in Washington. A small home in those places has little to gain from a purchase that long.</p>
<p>You should also hold off if your utility pays little for exported power. In the example home, a quarter credit pushes Arkansas, Idaho, Louisiana and Nevada beyond 25 years. A small home that sends less power to the grid is somewhat shielded, but the credit rule is still the first thing to confirm. Our guide on <a href="/guides/net-metering-vs-net-billing-payback/">net metering versus net billing</a> explains the difference.</p>
<p>The federal 30% residential credit is no longer available for systems placed in service after December 31, 2025, so do not count on it. <a href="/guides/is-solar-worth-it-without-tax-credit/">Solar without a tax credit</a> is what the table above assumes.</p>
<h2 id="who-still-comes-out-ahead">Who still comes out ahead?</h2>
<p>Small homes in high-price states. Hawaii pays back in <strong>4.8 years</strong> at 47.18¢ per kWh, and Maine in 8 years at 32.11¢. Massachusetts, New York and Rhode Island land between 8.5 and 9.2 years for the example home. A low-use household there faces a far shorter payback than the Texas figures above.</p>
<p>Sunshine helps less than you would think. Our <a href="/guides/solar-payback-sun-vs-electricity-price/">sun versus price comparison</a> shows Alaska, with the weakest sun, beating Arizona. For a state-by-state view, open the <a href="/solar-calculator/">solar calculator</a>, or check the cost of power where you live on the <a href="/electricity-prices/">electricity prices page</a>.</p>
<h2 id="what-should-you-check-before-getting-a-quote">What should you check before getting a quote?</h2>
<p>Three things matter most for a small system.</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Price per watt.</strong> Berkeley Lab reports a median of $3.5 per watt for cash purchases of systems installed in 2024, and $4.7 for loan-financed ones. Our table uses $3.50. A quote far above those medians is worth questioning.</li>
<li><strong>Your credit rule.</strong> Ask whether exports earn full retail credit, and whether credits reset at an annual true-up.</li>
<li><strong>Your real use.</strong> Use a full year of bills, not one season. A planned electric car or heat pump changes the right size, and <a href="/guides/solar-and-electric-car-charging/">EV charging</a> is the usual surprise.</li>
</ul>
<p>Compare at least two quotes, and ask each installer to show how it estimated yearly kWh. PVWatts, the free production estimator from the U.S. Department of Energy&#39;s National Laboratory of the Rockies, is a good cross-check.</p>
<h2 id="if-you-remember-one-thing">If you remember one thing</h2>
<p>Your electricity price decides whether a small home should go solar, not the size of the bill. At 6,000 kWh a year, payback is 7.6 years in California and 14.9 in Texas, and the savings in a cheap-power state are modest. Run your own state in the <a href="/solar-calculator/">solar calculator</a> and read your utility&#39;s credit rule before you ask for a quote.</p>
