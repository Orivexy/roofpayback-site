Electricity Prices by U.S. Region: September 2026 Compared

Home, business and industrial electricity prices for the ten EIA regions in September 2026, which states each region holds, and how they moved in 10 years.

Published October 14, 2026·Updated November 28, 2026·5 min read·RoofPayback editorial·Figures checked against EIA and PVWatts data

At a glance

The average U.S. home paid 18.17¢ per kilowatt-hour (kWh) in September 2026, according to the U.S. Energy Information Administration (EIA). By region, homes paid from 14.42¢ in East South Central to 40.85¢ in Pacific Noncontiguous (Alaska and Hawaii).

The regions are the nine census divisions defined by the U.S. Census Bureau. EIA splits the Pacific division into its mainland states and Alaska plus Hawaii, so there are ten rows. A regional average is useful for one thing, placing you on the map, and misleading for another: several regions hold states whose prices are far apart. This guide shows both.

Three tiers stand out. Alaska and Hawaii sit alone above 40¢. New England, the Middle Atlantic and the Pacific mainland form a second tier between 25¢ and 30¢. The other six divisions fall between 14.42¢ and 19.41¢. Only the Pacific mainland got cheaper over the year, and only Alaska and Hawaii rose faster than the Middle Atlantic.

What the regional averages hide

The Pacific mainland is two markets. California's 30.86¢ is about twice Washington's 15¢. The division's 1.5% decline came from California (−3.2%) while Washington rose 8.7% and Oregon fell 1.9%. A Seattle household should ignore the division figure entirely.

The South Atlantic holds both the fastest and some of the slowest risers. The District of Columbia more than doubled in ten years (120.3%), while South Carolina rose 27.1% and Florida 32.3%. Its prices run from 14.74¢ in Florida to 25.82¢ in D.C., the widest range of any mainland division after the Pacific.

The Plains and the Mountain West climbed slowest. Every West North Central and Mountain state rose less than the national 41.2% over ten years; Nebraska (12.5%) and Arizona (17.4%) moved least. The Middle Atlantic climbed fastest as a group: all three of its states rose more than 50%.

New England's calm year is an average of opposites. The division rose only 0.4% because Connecticut fell 10.9% while Maine rose 12.2% and New Hampshire 5.2%. Over ten years, every New England state rose at least as fast as the nation, and Maine nearly doubled.

Business prices tell their own story. In East South Central, commercial customers paid 13.35¢, close to the 14.42¢ homes paid, the narrowest home-versus-business gap of any division. In West South Central they paid 9.56¢, while homes paid 15.4¢. The state-by-state version of that comparison is in residential vs. business electricity prices.

Why the region matters for solar

Rooftop solar saves money by replacing power you would otherwise buy, so a region's price level sets the starting point for payback, and the gaps inside a division mean the state matters more than the region. The second factor is how your utility credits exported power; in California and Hawaii, new customers no longer get full retail credit. Net metering vs. net billing payback explains the difference, and the state pages under solar panels by state apply both to each state. Regional guides such as solar in the Midwest and solar in the South Central states go further.

Checking your own price

  1. Find your real rate: total bill divided by total kWh, so fees are included.
  2. Compare it with your state, not just your region, on electricity prices by state.
  3. Use twelve months of bills; cheapest and most expensive states for electricity shows how much prices swing within a year.
  4. Enter your usage and rate in the electricity cost calculator.
