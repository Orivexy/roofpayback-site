38:T853,<div class="short-answer"><h2 id="at-a-glance">At a glance</h2>
<p>The average U.S. home paid 18.16¢/kWh in August 2026, according to the U.S. Energy Information Administration (EIA). That single number hides a wide spread. Pacific Noncontiguous (Alaska and Hawaii) was the most expensive region at 40.79¢/kWh. East South Central was the cheapest at 14.47¢/kWh.</p>
<p>Prices are quoted per <a href="/guides/what-is-a-kilowatt-hour/">kilowatt-hour</a> (kWh). The regions here are the nine U.S. census divisions, which are groups of neighboring states; EIA reports the Pacific division in two parts, so the tables show ten rows. They give a fast read on broad patterns before you zoom in on your own state.</p>
<p>All prices are EIA averages for August 2026. &quot;Change&quot; compares August 2026 with August 2025. Your bill depends on your own utility and rate plan.</p>
</div><h2 id="pacific-noncontiguous-the-far-end-of-the-scale">Pacific Noncontiguous: the far end of the scale</h2>
<p>Alaska and Hawaii form a division of their own, and it tops every table. Homes paid 40.79¢/kWh, up from 34.72¢/kWh a year earlier. That 17.5% jump was the largest of any division.</p>
<p>This is also the only division where factories paid more than shops and offices. Industrial customers paid 34.69¢/kWh against 34.46¢/kWh for commercial ones. Everywhere else, industry paid the least. How Alaska and Hawaii compare for rooftop panels is covered in <a href="/guides/solar-in-alaska-and-hawaii-compared/">solar in Alaska vs. Hawaii</a>.</p>
<h2 id="new-england-pricey-and-the-only-region-to-fall">New England: pricey, and the only region to fall</h2>
<p>Homes in New England paid 28.31¢/kWh, the highest of any mainland division. Yet the price did not climb: it slipped from 29.01¢/kWh, a 2.4% dip, and New England was the only division where homes paid less than a year earlier.</p>
<p>That dip is worth reading correctly. A falling price does not make a price low. Businesses here also pay a lot by national standards: 22.24¢/kWh commercial and 17.56¢/kWh industrial, the highest industrial rate outside Alaska and Hawaii.</p>

43:T2249,<h2 id="pacific-contiguous-one-average-three-very-different-states">Pacific Contiguous: one average, three very different states</h2>
<p>The West Coast division averaged 25.59¢/kWh for homes, up 0.7%, the smallest increase of any division. That figure describes none of its big states well.</p>
<p>California&#39;s residential price was 29.75¢/kWh. Washington sat at 14.63¢/kWh and Oregon at 15.92¢/kWh. Neighbors in the same division can face very different bills. Industrial customers in this division paid 17.41¢/kWh, second only to New England on the mainland.</p>
<h2 id="middle-atlantic-a-steep-climb">Middle Atlantic: a steep climb</h2>
<p>Homes here paid 25.17¢/kWh, up from 23.73¢/kWh. The 6.1% rise was one of the fastest in the country.</p>
<p>The gap between customer types is striking. Commercial users paid 19.62¢/kWh, while industrial users paid 11.04¢/kWh. Households carry far more of the cost per kWh than factories do.</p>
<h2 id="east-north-central-the-fastest-riser-on-the-mainland">East North Central: the fastest riser on the mainland</h2>
<p>No mainland division rose faster. The residential price went from 18.11¢/kWh to 19.6¢/kWh, an 8.2% increase. Only Pacific Noncontiguous climbed more.</p>
<p>At 19.6¢/kWh, this Great Lakes division sits above the U.S. average of 18.16¢/kWh. Commercial customers paid 13.95¢/kWh and industrial customers 10.02¢/kWh. What those prices mean for panels in each state is in <a href="/guides/solar-panels-in-the-midwest/">solar in the Midwest</a>.</p>
<h2 id="south-atlantic-modest-price-rising-slower-than-average">South Atlantic: modest price, rising slower than average</h2>
<p>Residential power cost 16.04¢/kWh, below the national figure. It also rose 2.6%, slower than the U.S. average increase of 3.4%.</p>
<p>Business rates were moderate too: 12.31¢/kWh commercial and 9.77¢/kWh industrial.</p>
<h2 id="west-north-central-middle-of-the-pack">West North Central: middle of the pack</h2>
<p>Homes paid 15.92¢/kWh, up 4.9% from 15.17¢/kWh. The price sits below the national average, but it rose faster than the U.S. average of 3.4%. Commercial customers paid 12.07¢/kWh and industrial customers 9.16¢/kWh.</p>
<h2 id="east-south-central-the-cheapest-homes-in-the-country">East South Central: the cheapest homes in the country</h2>
<p>This division had the lowest residential price of any region, 14.47¢/kWh, up 3.4% from 13.99¢/kWh.</p>
<p>One detail stands out. Commercial customers paid 13.41¢/kWh, not far below what homes paid. In most other divisions the gap between homes and businesses is wider. Industrial customers paid 7.53¢/kWh.</p>
<h2 id="west-south-central-cheap-for-business">West South Central: cheap for business</h2>
<p>Residential customers paid 15.3¢/kWh, up 3.4%. The business side is where this division stands out. Commercial customers paid 9.54¢/kWh and industrial customers 7.16¢/kWh, the lowest of any division in both categories. For homeowners in Texas, Oklahoma, Arkansas and Louisiana weighing panels, see <a href="/guides/solar-panels-in-the-south-central-states/">solar in the South Central states</a>.</p>
<h2 id="mountain-slow-change-wide-spread">Mountain: slow change, wide spread</h2>
<p>The Mountain division averaged 14.86¢/kWh for homes. It rose just 2%; only Pacific Contiguous rose less, and New England fell.</p>
<p>Like the West Coast, its average blurs real differences. Colorado was at 17.17¢/kWh, while Idaho was at 13.37¢/kWh and Utah at 13.31¢/kWh. Commercial customers paid 12.14¢/kWh and industrial customers 8.71¢/kWh.</p>

<h2 id="why-the-region-matters-for-solar">Why the region matters for solar</h2>
<p>Rooftop solar saves money by replacing power you would otherwise buy, so the price per kWh is the first thing that decides payback. Change only the local price in our example home (a 7 kW system with no incentives, described on the <a href="/methodology/">methodology</a> page) and the payback moves a lot.</p>
<p>The second factor is the export rule: how your utility credits power your panels send to the grid. Some credit exports at the full retail rate and others pay less, and where credits are low, that rule can outweigh a high regional price. <a href="/guides/net-metering-vs-net-billing-payback/">Net metering vs. net billing payback</a> explains the difference.</p>
<p>Run your own case in the <a href="/solar-calculator/">solar calculator</a>. The <a href="/guides/best-states-for-solar-payback/">best states for solar payback</a> guide ranks states using the same approach.</p>
<h2 id="checking-your-own-price">Checking your own price</h2>
<ol>
<li>Find your real rate: total bill cost divided by total kWh, so fees are included.</li>
<li>Compare it with your division above and your state on <a href="/electricity-prices/">electricity prices by state</a>.</li>
<li>Look at twelve months of use, not one. Summer and winter bills can differ a lot.</li>
<li>Enter your usage and rate in the <a href="/electricity-cost-calculator/">electricity cost calculator</a>.</li>
</ol>

<h2 id="the-pattern-across-the-region">The pattern across the region</h2>
<p>Set the nine census divisions side by side and four patterns stand out.</p>
<p><strong>Three price tiers.</strong> Alaska and Hawaii sit alone at 40.79¢/kWh. The coastal Northeast and the West Coast form a second tier, from the Middle Atlantic at 25.17¢ up to New England at 28.31¢. Everything else, from the South Atlantic to the Mountain states, falls between 14.47¢ and 19.6¢, a band that the U.S. average of 18.16¢ sits inside.</p>
<p><strong>Almost everyone paid more.</strong> Residential prices rose in every division except New England, which dipped 2.4%. The increases ranged from 0.7% in Pacific Contiguous to 17.5% in Pacific Noncontiguous, with the Great Lakes (East North Central, 8.2%) leading the mainland.</p>
<p><strong>Homes carry the highest rate.</strong> In every division, households paid more per kWh than businesses. Industry paid the least everywhere except Alaska and Hawaii. The home-versus-shop gap was narrowest in East South Central, where commercial customers paid 13.41¢ against 14.47¢ for homes.</p>
<p><strong>Division averages blur their states.</strong> California (29.75¢) and Washington (14.63¢) share a division, as do Colorado (17.17¢) and Utah (13.31¢). The regional number tells you which part of the map you live in; your state page and your own bill tell you what you pay.</p>
