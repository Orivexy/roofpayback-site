Solar Panel Degradation: 0.5% vs 0.8% Over 25 Years

How much output do solar panels lose over 25 years? Compare 0.5% and 0.8% yearly degradation and see the effect on payback and savings in five states.

The verdict

Solar panels do lose a little output every year. The industry word for this is degradation. It is worth knowing about, but it is rarely the thing that decides whether solar pays off.

In our example home, moving from 0.5% to 0.8% yearly degradation added at most 0.3 years to payback across five states. It did trim the 25-year gain, by different amounts in each state. By year 25, a system degrading at 0.5% a year still makes 88.7% of its year-1 output. At 0.8% a year it makes 82.5%.

Your electricity price, your sunshine and your installed cost matter more. Below are the most common worries about degradation, checked against the numbers.

Myth: Solar panels wear out and stop working after a couple of decades

Fact: Panels fade slowly. They do not quit.

Heat, sunlight and weather wear the cells over time, so each year a panel makes slightly less electricity than the year before. That is all degradation means. Even at the faster of the two rates we tested, the system was still producing 82.5% of its first-year output in year 25.

Manufacturers usually publish a yearly degradation rate in the warranty. Two common figures are 0.5% and 0.8% per year. Your panels may differ, so read the warranty for the exact model and ask the installer for the warranted output at year 25.

Myth: A faster degradation rate wrecks your payback

Fact: In five very different states, the faster rate moved payback by 0.3 years or less.

Payback is the point where your bill savings add up to what the system cost. The 25-year gain is total savings minus the system cost, over 25 years. Here is how both changed in the model:

State Rate 25-yr gain Payback Year-25 output
Texas 0.5%/yr $23,624 14.3 years 88.7%
Texas 0.8%/yr $21,683 14.6 years 82.5%
California 0.5%/yr $79,631 7.4 years 88.7%
California 0.8%/yr $77,399 7.4 years 82.5%
Florida 0.5%/yr $24,658 14.1 years 88.7%
Florida 0.8%/yr $22,726 14.3 years 82.5%
Arizona 0.5%/yr $26,978 13.9 years 88.7%
Arizona 0.8%/yr $26,708 13.9 years 82.5%
New York 0.5%/yr $55,823 9 years 88.7%
New York 0.8%/yr $52,649 9.2 years 82.5%

The results come from our example home with no incentives, PVWatts production for one reference city per state and each state's EIA August 2026 average residential price; the methodology page lists every input.

Look at the payback column. California does not move at all. Arizona does not move either. The biggest shift is in Texas, from 14.3 to 14.6 years.

Myth: Every year of lost output costs you the same

Fact: Degradation compounds slowly, so most of the loss lands late in the system's life, often after payback.

Payback usually happens in the first half of a system's life. In those early years, the panels have barely faded. The bigger losses build up in the later years, when you have often already earned your money back.

California shows this clearly. Payback is 7.4 years at both rates. The only difference shows up later, as a lower 25-year gain at 0.8%.

Arizona adds a twist. Its 25-year gain is $26,978 at the slower rate and $26,708 at the faster one, almost the same. The likely reason is size. In Phoenix, about 6 kW would cover the example home's yearly use, so the 7 kW system makes more than the house needs, and credits stop at a year of grid purchases. Much of the output lost to aging is surplus that was earning nothing anyway. Rising electricity prices also make each kilowatt-hour the panels still produce worth more, a point our guide to solar payback if electricity prices rise explores.

Myth: Degradation matters the same everywhere

Fact: The faster rate hit some states harder than others, in payback and in 25-year gain.

In the model, Texas, Florida and New York were the only states where payback moved at all. Texas dropped from $23,624 to $21,683 and gained 0.3 years of payback.

Degradation also deserves more attention when:

  • Your system cost is high. Payback already sits late in the system's life, so late losses bite harder.
  • Your electricity price is low. Each lost kWh is worth less, and the margin is already thin.
  • Your system is oversized and you rely on export credits. Our guide to oversized solar systems and the annual true-up explains why.

Installed cost varies a lot from one home to the next, and it moves payback far more than degradation does. Our guide to solar panel cost by state shows how much a cheaper or dearer quote changes the result.

Myth: The warranty rate tells you exactly what your panels will do

Fact: It is a useful number, but real panels do not follow one neat line.

Your actual rate depends on the panel model and your climate. Our model also uses one steady rate for every year. Real panels may lose more in the first year and less after that.

The model leaves out other things, too. It ignores inverter replacement, which can be a real cost over 25 years. Shading, roof direction and snow can all change output. Our methodology page explains what is and is not included.

How to use this when you get a quote

  1. Ask for the panel's yearly degradation rate and the warranted output at year 25.
  2. Enter that rate in the solar calculator, then compare it with a slower rate.
  3. Check your state's price on the electricity prices page.
  4. Look up your state under solar panels by state for local context.
  5. Confirm how your utility credits exported power. Net metering and incentive programs vary by state and utility, and DSIRE keeps a database of current programs at programs.dsireusa.org.

The figures above already assume no federal help: per the IRS, the 30% residential credit (Section 25D) is not available for systems placed in service after December 31, 2025. Is solar worth it without the tax credit covers what that means for payback.

What actually decides it

A faster degradation rate lowers lifetime savings. In our example, though, it changed payback by no more than 0.3 years in any of the five states.

Put most of your attention on three bigger levers:

  • The price per watt in your quote.
  • Your local electricity rate.
  • How your utility credits solar exports.

Then treat the degradation rate in your warranty as one more input, not the deciding one.
