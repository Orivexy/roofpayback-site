Solar Panel Degradation: 0.5% vs 0.8% Over 25 Years

How much output panels lose over 25 years, where the 0.5% a year figure comes from, and what a faster rate does to payback and savings in six states.

Published October 28, 2026·Updated November 28, 2026·4 min read·RoofPayback editorial·Figures checked against EIA and PVWatts data

Official data: EIA · DOE · IRS · how we check

The short answer

Solar panels lose a small share of their output every year, a process called degradation. The best-known figure comes from a 2012 review by researchers at the National Renewable Energy Laboratory (now the National Laboratory of the Rockies), which assembled "nearly 2000 degradation rates, measured on individual modules or entire systems," and found "a median value of 0.5%/year." Our model uses that median.

At 0.5% a year, a system still makes 88.7% of its first-year output in year 25. To show what a worse-than-median panel would do, we also ran 0.8% a year, which leaves 82.5% in year 25. In six states, the faster rate delayed payback by 0 to 0.4 years. It mattered more for the 25-year total than for the payback date.

What degradation looks like in kWh

Take the example home in Houston: a 7 kW south-facing system that makes about 9,950 kWh in its first year. At 0.5% a year it makes about 8,820 kWh in year 25; at 0.8%, about 8,200. Over 25 years the slower rate delivers the equivalent of 23.6 full first years of output, the faster rate 22.7, a difference of about 4% of lifetime production.

The model applies one steady rate every year. Real panels can lose more in the first year and less afterward, and some models carry a lower or higher warranted rate. And a median means half of the rates in the review were higher than 0.5%.

Effect on payback and the 25-year gain

The table runs our example home (7 kW, 10,500 kWh a year, $3,500 per kW, no incentives, prices rising 2.5% a year) with September 2026 EIA residential prices. The 25-year gain is total savings minus the system cost and upkeep.

Minnesota Full credit $24,828 14 years $22,842 14.3 years
Arizona Full credit $26,512 14 years $26,244 14 years
Texas Full credit $23,624 14.3 years $21,683 14.6 years
Florida Full credit $23,797 14.3 years $21,897 14.6 years
Louisiana Full credit $17,130 16.2 years $15,439 16.6 years

Three things stand out.

Payback barely moves. It lands in the first half of the system's life, when the panels have hardly faded. Most of the lost output comes later.

Arizona hardly notices. In Phoenix about 6 kW covers this home's use, so the 7 kW system makes more than the credit cap allows and part of its surplus already earns nothing. Losing some of that surplus to aging costs only $268 over 25 years.

A low export credit makes the rate cost less in dollars but leaves less margin. In Texas, the faster rate trims the 25-year gain by $1,941 with full credit and by $485 at quarter credit, because much of the lost output would have been exported at a low value. But at quarter credit the gain was thin to begin with. Louisiana is the case where this applies to new systems: its Public Service Commission credits exports from systems installed after 2019 at avoided cost, and at quarter credit the example does not pay back within 25 years at either rate.

When degradation deserves more attention

  • High installed cost. A late payback means the faded years carry more of the burden. Solar panel cost by state shows how much the price per watt moves the result.
  • Cheap power. Each lost kWh is worth little, but so is every kWh, and the margin over 25 years is thin; see solar where electricity is cheap.
  • Long financing. If loan payments run past year 15, the system's later output pays for them. Solar loan vs. cash covers the trade-off.

What the model leaves out can matter more than the rate: inverter replacement during 25 years, shade from growing trees, and snow or soiling. The methodology page lists what is included.

What to check in a quote

  1. The warranted yearly degradation rate and the guaranteed output at year 25, for the exact panel model.
  2. Whether the installer's production estimate assumes degradation at all, and at what rate.
  3. Who pays for an inverter replacement, and when its warranty ends.

Then enter the warranted rate in the solar calculator and compare it with 0.5%. If the payback only works at the slower rate, the project has little room for anything else to go wrong; the quote checklist covers the other terms to compare.

Where degradation ranks

Among the inputs that set payback, degradation is one of the smallest. Your price per kWh, how exports are credited and your installed price each move the result by years; going from 0.5% to 0.8% moved it by 0.4 years at most in our six states. Treat the warranty figure as a check on panel quality and a modest adjustment to lifetime savings, not as the deciding number.
