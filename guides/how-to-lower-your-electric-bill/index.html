How to Lower Your Electric Bill: Find the Cause, Then Cut

Diagnose a high electric bill with EIA monthly data, then see what a saved kWh and a heat pump water heater are worth in every state at September 2026 prices.

In one minute

An electric bill is the kilowatt-hours (kWh) you use, times the price of each one, plus any fixed monthly charge. Over the twelve months to September 2026, the average U.S. household used 885 kWh a month and paid $160, according to the U.S. Energy Information Administration (EIA).

So a high bill has one of three causes: you use a lot of kWh, you pay a high price per kWh, or one season pushes your use up. Find out which before spending money, because each has a different fix. Then put a dollar value on a saved kWh where you live: in September 2026 it ranged from 13.25¢ in Nevada to 48.31¢ in Hawaii.

Step 1: Find out why your bill is high

You need three numbers from a year of bills: your monthly kWh, your total paid, and the month with the highest bill. Compare them with your state's figures on the electricity prices page or in average electric bill by state.

High kWh, normal price: a usage problem. Texas homes averaged 1,133 kWh a month, but the year was lopsided. Use ran from 807 kWh in November 2025 to 1,670 kWh in August 2026, and the average bill from $129 to $266. A summer peak like that points to air conditioning.

A winter peak points to electric heat. In Maryland, the average home used 658 kWh in October 2025 and 1,317 kWh in January 2026, when the average bill hit $271, more than the $147 of October.

Normal kWh, high price: a rate problem. Hawaii homes used almost exactly the same power in September 2026 as in October 2025 (553 against 554 kWh), yet the average bill rose from $220 to $267, because the price rose from 39.74¢ to 48.31¢. No amount of conservation explains that change; the rate does.

Same bill, different causes. Alabama's average bill, $191, is 18% below Hawaii's $232. Alabama homes use more than twice the kWh at about a third of the price. Comparing bills in dollars alone hides which problem you have.

All figures are EIA-861M averages per residential account; months from January 2026 are preliminary.

The same water heater is worth about $500 a year in Nevada and more than $1,800 in Hawaii. At the September 2026 U.S. average of 18.17¢, ENERGY STAR's 3,760 kWh would be worth about $683, more than its own $550 estimate, because prices have risen above the 14.6¢ it assumed.

Two cautions. The state average includes fixed charges spread over every kWh, so the price you can actually avoid is a little lower; fixed charges on your electric bill shows how much lower with real tariffs. And the water heater figure applies only if you now heat water with electric resistance, which is ENERGY STAR's comparison.

Step 3: Go after the biggest loads first

Heating and cooling. ENERGY STAR says "almost half the annual energy bill goes to heating and cooling – more than $900 a year." If your bills peak in summer or winter, start here: a thermostat schedule, clean filters, and a check of the system's age.

Air leaks and insulation. ENERGY STAR says sealing and insulating can "provide up to a 10% savings on your annual energy bills." For a home that heats and cools with electricity and pays the U.S. average of $160 a month, 10% would be up to about $192 a year. Treat that as a ceiling, not a promise; a home that is already tight will save less.

Water heating. When an electric resistance water heater nears the end of its life, the heat pump column above shows what replacing it with a certified heat pump model could be worth where you live. It costs more up front and needs space around it.

EV charging. A car that charges at home can add thousands of kWh a year. Home EV charging cost by state prices it state by state.

Small loads. Lighting and standby devices are cheap to fix but rarely decide the bill. Do them, but do not stop there.

Step 4: Check whether a different rate plan fits your habits

If your utility offers a time-of-use plan and you can move laundry, dishwashing or EV charging to off-peak hours, you can pay less per kWh without using fewer. If most of your use falls in the peak window, the same plan can cost more. Time-of-use rates explained works through real 2026 tariffs from PG&E and Georgia Power. In some states you can also shop for your supply; see electricity choice states explained.

Step 5: Judge progress in kWh, not dollars

Prices move on their own. Over the twelve months to September 2026, the average U.S. bill rose from $149 to $160 a month. Average use rose less than 2%; most of the increase came from a price per kWh about 6% higher. Compare each month's kWh with the same month a year earlier; if the kWh fell, your changes worked, whatever the dollar total did.

Step 6: Weigh solar after the cheaper steps

Rooftop solar replaces kWh you would buy, so the price column above sets what each solar kWh is worth, and a home that has already cut its use needs a smaller system. How your utility credits exported power matters as much; in California and Hawaii, new customers no longer get full retail credit. Test your own bill in the solar calculator; the assumptions are on the methodology page.

Where to start this month

  • Write down twelve months of kWh and dollars from your bills, and mark the highest month.
  • Compare your kWh and your all-in price (bill divided by kWh) with your state's averages.
  • If the price is the outlier, ask your utility about rate plans; if usage is, list your two biggest loads.
  • Look up your state's row in the table to see what 100 saved kWh a month is worth to you.
  • Next month, compare kWh with the same month last year.
