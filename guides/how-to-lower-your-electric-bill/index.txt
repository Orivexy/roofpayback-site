38:Tae7,<div class="short-answer"><h2 id="in-one-minute">In one minute</h2>
<p>Your electric bill comes down two ways: buy fewer <a href="/guides/what-is-a-kilowatt-hour/">kilowatt-hours</a> (kWh), or pay less for each one.</p>
<p>In practice, that means cutting your biggest loads, shifting use to cheaper hours if your utility allows it, and making sure your rate plan fits your habits. Rooftop solar is a fourth path, but it is a large purchase, not a quick fix.</p>
<p>How much each step saves depends on your home, your climate, your habits and your utility&#39;s rates, so the steps below say where to look and what to change rather than promising a percentage.</p>
</div><h2 id="step-1-learn-what-one-kwh-is-worth-where-you-live">Step 1: Learn what one kWh is worth where you live</h2>
<p>Start by putting a price on the thing you are trying to save. Each kWh you avoid is worth about your state&#39;s average residential price. The U.S. Energy Information Administration (EIA) publishes those averages. They equal utility revenue divided by kWh sold, so taxes and fees are folded in. Your own bill may differ.</p>

<h2 id="step-2-find-where-your-kwh-go">Step 2: Find where your kWh go</h2>
<p>You can&#39;t cut what you can&#39;t see. Pull up your bill and read your monthly kWh. Compare a summer month with a winter month. A big gap usually points to heating or cooling.</p>
<p>Next, list the large loads in your home. The usual suspects:</p>
<ul>
<li>Heating and cooling.</li>
<li>Water heating.</li>
<li>Clothes dryers and other large appliances.</li>
<li>Electric vehicle (EV) charging, if you have one.</li>
<li>Devices that run all day, such as an older refrigerator or a second freezer.</li>
</ul>
<p>Ask your utility whether it offers a home energy audit or an online usage breakdown. Many do. Availability and cost vary by utility.</p>

47:T17b2,<h2 id="step-3-go-after-the-biggest-loads-first">Step 3: Go after the biggest loads first</h2>
<p>Small tweaks feel productive. Big loads decide the bill. Spend your effort where the kWh are.</p>
<h3>Heating and cooling</h3>
<p>Set a thermostat schedule so the house is warmed or cooled less while everyone is asleep or out, instead of adjusting by hand. A programmable or smart thermostat does this automatically. Keep filters clean and vents clear so the system is not working against a blockage.</p>
<p>Then stop paying to heat or cool the outdoors. Weatherstrip doors, caulk gaps around window frames, and seal openings where pipes and wires pass through walls. Attic hatches and gaps around recessed lights are common leaks that are easy to miss. In summer, closing blinds on sun-facing windows during the hottest hours keeps heat out before the air conditioner has to remove it.</p>
<h3>Water heating</h3>
<p>Turn the water heater down if it runs hotter than you need. Fix dripping hot water taps. Wash clothes in cold water when the label allows.</p>
<p>When an electric water heater nears the end of its life, look at a heat-pump water heater. It moves heat from the surrounding air into the tank instead of making all of it with a resistance element, so it uses less electricity for the same hot water. It costs more up front and needs some space around it, so ask the installer whether your home suits one.</p>
<h3>Lighting and small devices</h3>
<p>Replace old incandescent and halogen bulbs with LEDs, starting with the lights that stay on longest, such as porch, kitchen and living-room lamps. Put electronics that sit on standby, like game consoles and TV boxes, on a power strip you can switch off. Each change is minor, but all are cheap and easy.</p>
<h3>EV charging</h3>
<p>Home charging is a large load. As an illustration, 3,500 kWh of home charging per year costs about $572 in Alabama and about $1,041 in California at average state prices. That usage figure is an assumption. See <a href="/guides/home-ev-charging-cost-by-state/">home EV charging cost by state</a> for your state.</p>
<h2 id="step-4-check-whether-a-different-rate-plan-pays-less-per-kwh">Step 4: Check whether a different rate plan pays less per kWh</h2>
<p>Some utilities charge different prices at different times of day. This is a time-of-use (TOU) rate. If you can run the dishwasher, laundry or EV charger during cheaper hours, a TOU plan can lower your bill. Delay-start buttons on dishwashers and washers, and scheduled charging in many EVs, make the shift automatic. If your use can&#39;t move, a TOU plan can cost you more.</p>
<p>Read the rate sheet before switching. Note the peak hours, the peak price and any fixed monthly charges. Our guide to <a href="/guides/time-of-use-electricity-rates-explained/">time-of-use electricity rates</a> shows how to compare plans side by side.</p>
<h2 id="step-5-track-price-changes-not-just-your-usage">Step 5: Track price changes, not just your usage</h2>
<p>Prices shift from year to year, so a bill can rise even when your use stays flat. EIA data show that between August 2025 and August 2026 the average residential price rose 10.7% in Idaho and fell 18.8% in Connecticut. Most states went up; a few went down. The full list is in our <a href="/guides/electricity-prices-2026-08/">August 2026 electricity price report</a>.</p>
<h2 id="step-6-weigh-solar-with-realistic-math">Step 6: Weigh solar with realistic math</h2>
<p>Rooftop solar makes electricity during the day and can cut the kWh you buy from the grid. It costs money up front. Whether it pays off rests first on your electricity price, then on your local net metering rules (how your utility credits power you send back to the grid), plus your roof and your quote.</p>
<p>Do the cheaper steps first: a home that uses less needs a smaller system. Local programs vary, and DSIRE keeps a database of them. Test your own numbers in the <a href="/solar-calculator/">solar calculator</a>; our example home and its assumptions are on the <a href="/methodology/">methodology</a> page.</p>
<h2 id="mistakes-that-cost-money">Mistakes that cost money</h2>
<ul>
<li><strong>Judging progress in dollars.</strong> Prices move on their own. Compare kWh with the same month last year, adjusting for weather, to see what you actually changed.</li>
<li><strong>Starting with the small stuff.</strong> Switching off standby devices is worth doing, but heating, cooling and water heating usually matter more.</li>
<li><strong>Switching to time-of-use without a plan.</strong> If you can&#39;t move laundry, dishes or EV charging out of peak hours, the new plan may raise your bill.</li>
<li><strong>Ignoring fixed charges.</strong> The per-kWh price is only part of a plan. Check the fixed monthly charges on the rate sheet too.</li>
<li><strong>Treating the 900 kWh table as your bill.</strong> It is an example for comparing states. Your use and your utility&#39;s rates set your real number.</li>
<li><strong>Buying solar as a quick fix.</strong> It is a long-term purchase. Run the math with your own bill and written quotes first.</li>
</ul>
<h2 id="your-checklist">Your checklist</h2>
<ul>
<li>Write down your last 12 months of kWh from your bills.</li>
<li>Find your state&#39;s price per kWh so you know what a saved kWh is worth.</li>
<li>Pick the two biggest loads in your home.</li>
<li>Make one change to each, such as a thermostat schedule or a lower water heater setting.</li>
<li>Seal the drafts you can feel around doors, windows and the attic hatch.</li>
<li>Swap the longest-burning bulbs for LEDs.</li>
<li>Ask your utility about audits, usage breakdowns and time-of-use options.</li>
<li>Compare next month&#39;s kWh with the same month last year.</li>
<li>If solar interests you, run your bill through the calculator before talking to installers.</li>
</ul>
<p>The value of each kWh you save runs from 12.87¢ in Louisiana to 47.18¢ in Hawaii in the EIA data above. Whatever your state, the first saving worth chasing is the load that tops your own bill.</p>
