33:T1e42,<div class="short-answer"><p class="short-answer-label">In one minute</p>
<p>An electric bill is the <a href="/guides/what-is-a-kilowatt-hour/">kilowatt-hours</a> (kWh) you use, times the price of each one, plus any fixed monthly charge. Over the twelve months to September 2026, the average U.S. household used <strong>885 kWh a month and paid $160</strong>, according to the U.S. Energy Information Administration (EIA).</p>
<p>So a high bill has one of three causes: you use a lot of kWh, you pay a high price per kWh, or one season pushes your use up. Find out which before spending money, because each has a different fix. Then put a dollar value on a saved kWh where you live: in September 2026 it ranged from 13.25¢ in Nevada to 48.31¢ in Hawaii.</p>
</div><h2 id="step-1-find-out-why-your-bill-is-high">Step 1: Find out why your bill is high</h2>
<p>You need three numbers from a year of bills: your monthly kWh, your total paid, and the month with the highest bill. Compare them with your state&#39;s figures on the <a href="/electricity-prices/">electricity prices</a> page or in <a href="/guides/average-electric-bill-by-state/">average electric bill by state</a>.</p>
<p><strong>High kWh, normal price: a usage problem.</strong> Texas homes averaged 1,133 kWh a month, but the year was lopsided. Use ran from 807 kWh in November 2025 to 1,670 kWh in August 2026, and the average bill from $129 to $266. A summer peak like that points to air conditioning.</p>
<p><strong>A winter peak points to electric heat.</strong> In Maryland, the average home used 658 kWh in October 2025 and 1,317 kWh in January 2026, when the average bill hit $271, more than the $147 of October.</p>
<p><strong>Normal kWh, high price: a rate problem.</strong> Hawaii homes used almost exactly the same power in September 2026 as in October 2025 (553 against 554 kWh), yet the average bill rose from $220 to $267, because the price rose from 39.74¢ to 48.31¢. No amount of conservation explains that change; the rate does.</p>
<p><strong>Same bill, different causes.</strong> Alabama&#39;s average bill, $191, is 18% below Hawaii&#39;s $232. Alabama homes use more than twice the kWh at about a third of the price. Comparing bills in dollars alone hides which problem you have.</p>
<p>All figures are EIA-861M averages per residential account; months from January 2026 are preliminary.</p>

40:Te38,<h2 id="step-3-go-after-the-biggest-loads-first">Step 3: Go after the biggest loads first</h2>
<p><strong>Heating and cooling.</strong> ENERGY STAR says &quot;almost half the annual energy bill goes to heating and cooling – more than $900 a year.&quot; If your bills peak in summer or winter, start here: a thermostat schedule, clean filters, and a check of the system&#39;s age.</p>
<p><strong>Air leaks and insulation.</strong> ENERGY STAR says sealing and insulating can &quot;provide up to a 10% savings on your annual energy bills.&quot; For a home that heats and cools with electricity and pays the U.S. average of $160 a month, 10% would be up to about $192 a year. Treat that as a ceiling, not a promise; a home that is already tight will save less.</p>
<p><strong>Water heating.</strong> When an electric resistance water heater nears the end of its life, the heat pump column above shows what replacing it with a certified heat pump model could be worth where you live. It costs more up front and needs space around it.</p>
<p><strong>EV charging.</strong> A car that charges at home can add thousands of kWh a year. <a href="/guides/home-ev-charging-cost-by-state/">Home EV charging cost by state</a> prices it state by state.</p>
<p><strong>Small loads.</strong> Lighting and standby devices are cheap to fix but rarely decide the bill. Do them, but do not stop there.</p>
<h2 id="step-4-check-whether-a-different-rate-plan-fits-your-habits">Step 4: Check whether a different rate plan fits your habits</h2>
<p>If your utility offers a time-of-use plan and you can move laundry, dishwashing or EV charging to off-peak hours, you can pay less per kWh without using fewer. If most of your use falls in the peak window, the same plan can cost more. <a href="/guides/time-of-use-electricity-rates-explained/">Time-of-use rates explained</a> works through real 2026 tariffs from PG&amp;E and Georgia Power. In some states you can also shop for your supply; see <a href="/guides/electricity-choice-states-explained/">electricity choice states explained</a>.</p>
<h2 id="step-5-judge-progress-in-kwh-not-dollars">Step 5: Judge progress in kWh, not dollars</h2>
<p>Prices move on their own. Over the twelve months to September 2026, the average U.S. bill rose from $149 to $160 a month. Average use rose less than 2%; most of the increase came from a price per kWh about 6% higher. Compare each month&#39;s kWh with the same month a year earlier; if the kWh fell, your changes worked, whatever the dollar total did.</p>
<h2 id="step-6-weigh-solar-after-the-cheaper-steps">Step 6: Weigh solar after the cheaper steps</h2>
<p>Rooftop solar replaces kWh you would buy, so the price column above sets what each solar kWh is worth, and a home that has already cut its use needs a smaller system. How your utility credits exported power matters as much; in California and Hawaii, new customers no longer get full retail credit. Test your own bill in the <a href="/solar-calculator/">solar calculator</a>; the assumptions are on the <a href="/methodology/">methodology</a> page.</p>

<li>If the price is the outlier, ask your utility about rate plans; if usage is, list your two biggest loads.</li>
<li>Look up your state&#39;s row in the table to see what 100 saved kWh a month is worth to you.</li>
<li>Next month, compare kWh with the same month last year.</li>
