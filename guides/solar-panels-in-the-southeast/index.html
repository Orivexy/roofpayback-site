Solar Payback in the Southeast: Sun Meets Cheap Power

Southeast solar paybacks run from 14.1 to 17.6 years in our example. See why cheap power, not sunshine, sets the pace in eight Southern states.

At a glance

The Southeast has strong sun and cheap electricity, and the cheap electricity wins. Across eight Southern states, example solar paybacks run from 14.1 to 17.6 years. Florida, South Carolina, Alabama and Georgia sit at the quick end. Tennessee and Louisiana sit at the slow end.

"Payback" is the number of years it takes for bill savings to add up to what the system cost. The figures here come from one example home (methodology) with full export credit, meaning the utility credits every extra kWh you send to the grid at the full retail price. That is the best case, and some utilities pay less.

Why cheap power sets the pace

On a map of average residential prices, the Southeast looks steady. No state here comes close to the prices in New England or California. Each kilowatt-hour (kWh) costs a homeowner here roughly 13 to 16 cents.

A solar panel earns money by replacing kWh you would otherwise buy. When those kWh are cheap, each one replaced saves little. The example systems in this region make plenty of power. That power is simply worth less per unit than in a high-price state.

For the national backdrop, see why electricity prices differ by state and the electricity prices hub.

Florida: first across the line

Orlando, Florida's reference city, produces 1,544 kWh per kW of panels a year, the most of any city on this tour. Pair that with a price of 14.99¢ and the example pays back in 14.1 years, the fastest in the group. Year-1 savings come to $1,574.

The Sunshine State's edge is real but narrow. North Carolina charges almost the same price, 14.95¢. Florida's stronger output gets it to payback about 0.8 years sooner.

That lead depends on full export credit. At half credit, Florida's example stretches to 19.2 years. At quarter credit it reaches 23.5 years. Florida homeowners can find local detail on the Florida solar page.

The Carolinas: same sun, different price

South Carolina and North Carolina make a neat pair. Their reference cities, Columbia and Charlotte, both produce 1,446 kWh per kW. Same output, so the price decides the gap.

South Carolina's average price is 15.76¢, and the example pays back in 14.2 years, with $1,595 in year-1 savings. That is the largest first-year figure on the tour. North Carolina's is 14.95¢, and the example takes 14.9 years, with $1,514 in year-1 savings.

If you want one place in the region that shows how price drives payback, this is it. Nothing else changes between the two rows.

Alabama and Georgia: the middle of the pack

Birmingham has the lowest output of the quick-end states, at 1,367 kWh per kW. Alabama also has the highest price on the tour, 16.33¢. Those two pull against each other and land Alabama at 14.5 years, with $1,562 in year-1 savings.

Atlanta ends up in the same place by a different road. Georgia's reference city produces more, 1,402 kWh per kW, but at a lower 15.9¢. Payback is also 14.5 years, with $1,560 in year-1 savings.

Alabama shows the export credit effect too. Its example moves from 14.5 years at full credit to 19.2 years at half credit and 22.9 years at quarter credit.

Mississippi: a step slower

Jackson produces 1,414 kWh per kW, more than Birmingham or Atlanta. But Mississippi's price is 14.43¢, and the example pays back in 15.7 years. Year-1 savings are $1,428.

Mississippi marks the point where the region shifts from the low teens toward the slower end.

Louisiana: lots of sun, little to save

New Orleans produces 1,479 kWh per kW, more than Alabama's or Georgia's reference cities. Yet Louisiana pays back more slowly, at 16.7 years, with $1,332 in year-1 savings. The reason is price. At 12.87¢, power here is the cheapest on the tour, against 16.33¢ in Alabama.

Louisiana is also the warning about export credits. In the example, at quarter credit the system does not pay back within 25 years at all. The state also appears, next to Texas, Oklahoma and Arkansas, in solar in the south-central states.

Tennessee: the cautionary stop

Nashville brings up the rear. Cooler, cloudier weather gives it the lowest output on the tour, 1,339 kWh per kW. A low price of 13.42¢ adds to it. The example takes 17.6 years to pay back, with $1,257 in year-1 savings.

That does not make solar wrong in Tennessee. It means the bar for a good quote is higher.

Before you get quotes anywhere in the region

Ask about export credit before you collect quotes. Your utility can tell you what it credits for power you send to the grid. Full retail credit, reduced credit and no credit lead to very different results. Our guides on net metering versus net billing and the annual true-up explain how.

Plan without the federal credit. According to the IRS, the 30% federal credit no longer covers systems placed in service after 2025, and the example counts no incentives at all, which is one reason paybacks look long. State, utility or town programs can shorten the wait; DSIRE keeps a database at programs.dsireusa.org, and solar without the tax credit explains how to check them.

Check the price per watt. The example uses $3,500 per kW. A quote well above that stretches payback; one below it shortens it.

Do the homework:

  1. Pull a year of bills and add up the kWh. Solar is sized to your use, not your roof.
  2. Get several quotes and compare price per watt, not the monthly payment.
  3. Check shading and roof direction. The roof pitch guide shows how tilt changes output.
  4. Run your real rate through the solar calculator, and see what you spend today with the bill calculator.

The pattern across the region

Across all eight states, more sun does not always win; price does. Louisiana out-produces Alabama and still pays back later. The Carolinas share the same output and split on price alone. Florida, the sunniest stop, leads by only a fraction of a year.

Solar here is a slow, steady investment rather than a quick win. The best-placed states reach payback in the low-to-mid teens, in years, and only when exports earn full credit. With weak export credit or a high quote, payback can run past the 25 years the model covers.

None of that rules solar out. It sets the order of questions: your own rate first, because price set every row of this table; the export rule second, because it decides whether a 14-year payback stays near 14; and the quote third.
