23:Tbcf,<div class="short-answer"><h2 id="short-answer">Short answer</h2>
<p>In the Southeast, solar payback is a story about sunshine meeting cheap power. The sun is strong, but electricity is inexpensive, so most of the eight states below land between 14.1 and 17.6 years in our example. Florida, South Carolina, Alabama and Georgia sit at the quick end. Tennessee and Louisiana sit at the slow end.</p>
<p>Your own number depends on your utility&#39;s rates, how it credits exported power, and your quote. Treat the table as a starting point, not a promise.</p>
</div><h2 id="why-the-southeast-is-its-own-case">Why the Southeast is its own case</h2>
<p>Look at a map of average residential prices and the region stands out for being steady. No state here comes near the prices in New England or California. A kilowatt-hour (kWh) is the unit your meter counts, and in this region each one costs a homeowner roughly 13 to 16 cents.</p>
<p>That matters because a solar panel earns money by replacing kWh you would otherwise buy. If each kWh is cheap, each kWh replaced saves little. Strong sun helps, but only up to a point. The example systems here make plenty of power. They just make power that is worth less per unit than in a high-price state.</p>
<p>For the wider national picture, see <a href="/guides/why-electricity-prices-differ-by-state/">why electricity prices differ by state</a> and the <a href="/electricity-prices/">electricity prices hub</a>.</p>

37:T10ae,<h2 id="what-the-table-teaches">What the table teaches</h2>
<p><strong>More sun does not always win.</strong> Louisiana&#39;s reference city produces more per kilowatt than Alabama&#39;s or Georgia&#39;s. Yet Louisiana pays back more slowly, because its price is 12.87¢ against 16.33¢ in Alabama. Price beats production in this region.</p>
<p><strong>Small price gaps move payback a lot.</strong> Florida and North Carolina have nearly the same price, 14.99¢ and 14.95¢. Florida&#39;s stronger output gets it to payback about 0.8 years sooner.</p>
<p><strong>Tennessee is the cautionary case.</strong> Cooler, cloudier weather and a low price of 13.42¢ combine into the longest payback in the group. That does not make solar wrong there. It means the bar for a good quote is higher.</p>
<h2 id="full-credit-is-the-big-assumption">Full credit is the big assumption</h2>
<p>The table assumes the best case: every exported kWh earns the full retail price. Many utilities pay less. Some credit exports at a lower rate, and the difference changes the result sharply.</p>
<p>In our example, Alabama moves from 14.5 years at full credit to 19.2 years at half credit and 22.9 years at quarter credit. Florida goes from 14.1 to 19.2 and then 23.5. In Louisiana, quarter credit does not pay back within 25 years at all.</p>
<p>So before you compare quotes, ask your utility one question: what do you credit me for power I send to the grid? Our guides on <a href="/guides/net-metering-vs-net-billing-payback/">net metering versus net billing</a> and the <a href="/guides/oversized-solar-system-annual-true-up/">annual true-up</a> explain how the answer changes the math.</p>
<h2 id="the-tax-credit-is-gone-so-price-your-quote-carefully">The tax credit is gone, so price your quote carefully</h2>
<p>The IRS states that the 30% federal Residential Clean Energy Credit (Section 25D) is not available for property placed in service after December 31, 2025. Our example therefore assumes no incentives, and that is why paybacks look long.</p>
<p>If your state, utility or town offers something, it can shorten the wait. DSIRE keeps a database of current programs at <a href="https://programs.dsireusa.org/">programs.dsireusa.org</a>. Read <a href="/guides/solar-incentives-after-federal-tax-credit/">solar incentives after the federal tax credit</a> for what is still worth checking.</p>
<p>Quote price matters just as much. Berkeley Lab reports a median installed price of $4.0 per watt for host-owned residential systems installed in 2024, with cash purchases at a median of $3.5 per watt. Our example uses $3500 per kW. A quote well above that range will stretch your payback. One below it will shorten it.</p>
<h2 id="a-practical-checklist-for-southeast-homeowners">A practical checklist for Southeast homeowners</h2>
<ol>
<li><strong>Pull a year of bills.</strong> Add up the kWh. Solar is sized to your use, not to your roof.</li>
<li><strong>Find your utility&#39;s export rule.</strong> Full retail credit, reduced credit and no credit produce very different outcomes.</li>
<li><strong>Get several quotes</strong> and compare the price per watt, not the monthly payment.</li>
<li><strong>Check shading and roof direction.</strong> Our <a href="/guides/solar-panel-roof-pitch/">roof pitch guide</a> shows how tilt changes output.</li>
<li><strong>Run your own numbers</strong> in the <a href="/solar-calculator/">solar calculator</a> using your real rate.</li>
</ol>
<p>If you live in one of these states, your <a href="/solar-panels/florida/">state page</a> has the full local detail, and the <a href="/electricity-cost-calculator/">bill calculator</a> can show what you spend today.</p>
<h2 id="bottom-line">Bottom line</h2>
<p>The Southeast has sun but not expensive power, so solar here is a slow, steady investment rather than a quick win. In our example, the best-placed states reach payback in the low-to-mid teens, in years, and only when the utility credits exports generously. If your utility pays little for exports, or your quote is high, payback can run past the system&#39;s practical life.</p>
<p>That is not a reason to rule solar out. It is a reason to ask about export credit first, compare quotes second, and use your own rate instead of an average.</p>
