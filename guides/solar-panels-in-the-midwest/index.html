Is Solar Worth It in the Midwest? State-by-State Payback

Midwest solar payback depends more on your electric rate than on sunshine. Compare twelve states using August 2026 EIA prices and PVWatts output.

Published November 9, 2026·Updated November 18, 2026·6 min read·RoofPayback editorial·Figures checked against EIA and PVWatts data

At a glance

Solar in the Midwest splits along price lines. In the higher-price states, Michigan, Ohio, Illinois and Wisconsin, our example system pays itself back in about 11 to 13 years with full export credit. In low-price North Dakota and Nebraska, the same system takes much longer. Winter sun is weaker across the region, but here the power price matters more than the sunshine.

Every figure below uses our example home, a 7 kW system at $24,500 with no incentives (methodology). Each kilowatt-hour (kWh) your panels make saves you what the grid would have charged for it.

Federal data (EIA, August 2026) splits the region in two:

  • East North Central (Illinois, Indiana, Michigan, Ohio, Wisconsin): 19.6¢/kWh, up from 18.11¢ a year earlier, a change of 8.2%.
  • West North Central (Iowa, Kansas, Minnesota, Missouri, Nebraska, North Dakota, South Dakota): 15.92¢/kWh, up from 15.17¢, a change of 4.9%.

With the U.S. residential average at 18.16¢/kWh, the eastern half sits above the national figure and the western half below it. The wider picture is in electricity prices by region.

Michigan: the region's priciest power and its quickest payback

Michigan tops the Midwest on price at 22.99¢ per kWh. That single fact explains its lead. Its panels are not especially productive, at 1,291 kWh per installed kW each year, which is among the lowest output in the region.

Even so, the example system pays back in 11.1 years with full export credit, the shortest in the Midwest. At half credit, where exported power earns half the retail rate, it stretches to 14.7 years.

Michigan's price also rose 10.9% over the past year in the data we use. Local detail is on the Michigan solar page.

Illinois, Ohio and Wisconsin: three states, nearly one answer

These three neighbors look almost like copies of each other. Prices sit within a fraction of a cent: Wisconsin at 19.41¢, Ohio at 19.38¢ and Illinois at 19.32¢. Output is close too, from 1,308 kWh per kW in Illinois to 1,334 kWh in Wisconsin.

The paybacks follow suit. Illinois and Ohio both reach 12.9 years with full credit and 17 years with half. Wisconsin edges ahead at 12.6 and 16.8 years, helped by slightly better output and a marginally higher price.

Ohio's price climbed 10.4% over the year. For a closer look, see the Illinois and Ohio pages.

Kansas and Missouri: the sun is there, the price is not

Kansas has the strongest output in this group, 1,526 kWh per kW. If sunshine ruled, it would lead the Midwest. Instead it pays back in 13.8 years with full credit, slower than Michigan, because its price is 15.47¢ against Michigan's 22.99¢.

Missouri tells a similar story with smaller numbers. Its output of 1,433 kWh is the second best here, but at 16.02¢ per kWh its payback is 14.1 years. Half credit pushes the two states to 18.9 and 19 years.

Indiana and Iowa: a natural experiment

Indiana and Iowa pay almost the same for power, 17.02¢ and 17.06¢. That makes them a clean test of what output alone does.

Iowa's panels make 1,370 kWh per kW, Indiana's 1,335 kWh. The result: Iowa pays back in 13.9 years and Indiana in 14.3 with full credit. A small edge in output, a small edge in payback. At half credit, they land at 18.5 and 18.8 years.

Iowa's price rose 10.2% over the year.

Minnesota: steadier prices in the north

Minnesota sits mid-pack at 17.2¢ per kWh and 1,333 kWh of output, for a payback of 14.1 years at full credit and 18.7 at half. What sets it apart is the trend. Its price rose just 1.8% over the year, well below the jumps in Ohio, Michigan and Iowa. A slow-rising price is easier on household budgets, though it also gives solar less of a tailwind.

The Dakotas and Nebraska: cheap power, long waits

The western edge of the region has the lowest prices and the longest paybacks.

  • South Dakota: 15.15¢ per kWh, 1,409 kWh of output, 15.1 years at full credit, 20.2 at half.
  • Nebraska: 13.54¢, 1,423 kWh, 16.6 years full, 22.1 half.
  • North Dakota: 13.31¢, 1,294 kWh, 18.3 years full, 23.7 half.

North Dakota is the starkest case. Its output is close to Illinois, but its price is 13.31¢ against 19.32¢. That gap alone stretches payback from 12.9 to 18.3 years. When power is this cheap, trimming your use first may be worth more than panels; see how to lower your electric bill.

The pattern across the region

Price runs the Midwest map. Payback tracks the rate more closely than the sunshine, and the higher-price eastern states lead. The export rule is the second filter, and it can reorder a close pair of states on its own.

A few forces shift every row:

  • Export credit. Moving from full to half credit adds roughly four to six years across the region. Your utility's rules decide which case applies. Read net metering vs. net billing before you sign anything.
  • Winter. Snow cover and short days cut winter output. The annual figures already average this in, but monthly bills will swing. Our winter solar guide covers the pattern state by state.
  • Price trends. Prices rose in all twelve states over the past year in our data, by very different amounts. The example home assumes 2.5% yearly growth, a modeling choice, not a forecast.
  • Incentives. None of these paybacks count any incentive, and the 30% federal credit no longer covers systems placed in service after 2025, per the IRS. Check DSIRE for state and utility programs that may still apply, and see solar without the tax credit for how to weigh them.
  • Install cost. The model uses $3.50 per watt. A higher quote lengthens every payback in the table and a lower one shortens it.

To test your own home, find your rate on your bill and compare it with your state on the electricity prices page. Ask your utility, in writing, how it credits exported power. Then get several quotes, compare the price per watt, and run the numbers in the solar calculator. If your rate looks more like Michigan's than North Dakota's, solar deserves a quote; if it sits near the bottom of the table, expect a long payback and weigh it against cheaper ways to cut your bill.
