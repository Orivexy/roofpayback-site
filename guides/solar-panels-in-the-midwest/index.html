Is Solar Worth It in the Midwest? State-by-State Payback

Twelve Midwest states compared with September 2026 EIA prices: solar payback, typical home use, slow price growth out west and how Minnesota pays for surplus solar.

Published November 9, 2026·Updated November 28, 2026·5 min read·RoofPayback editorial·Figures checked against EIA and PVWatts data

At a glance

The Census Bureau's Midwest splits into two price zones, and solar payback follows them. In the East North Central states (Illinois, Indiana, Michigan, Ohio, Wisconsin), homes paid 19.41¢ per kWh in September 2026; in the West North Central states (Iowa, Kansas, Minnesota, Missouri, Nebraska, North Dakota, South Dakota), 15.86¢. With full export credit, our example system pays back in 11.1 years in Michigan and 17.2 in North Dakota.

Two regional patterns matter for a buyer. Prices in the western states have risen more slowly than almost anywhere else over the last nine years, so our standard 2.5% yearly increase may be generous there. And the sunniest Midwest state, Kansas, is not the fastest payback.

Same sun, different price: Detroit and Fargo

Detroit and Fargo make almost the same amount of power per kW, 1,291 and 1,294 kWh a year. Michigan's price is 23.17¢ and North Dakota's 14.29¢. That difference alone separates an 11.1-year payback from a 17.2-year one. Kansas shows the reverse: Wichita has the region's best output, 1,526 kWh per kW, but at 15.83¢ it pays back later than Milwaukee, Chicago or Columbus.

Indiana and Iowa, which had nearly identical prices in August, no longer do: in September Indiana stood at 17.05¢ and Iowa at 14.99¢, and Iowa's payback is 1.4 years longer despite slightly better output.

A North Dakota home needs a system about two thirds larger than a Michigan one and still waits more than five years longer for payback. Accounts include apartments and second homes, so treat these as averages. How many solar panels do I need covers sizing from your own bills.

Slow price growth in the western states

Comparing 12-month averages ending September 2017 and September 2026 in EIA's utility data, residential prices rose about 1.5% a year in Kansas, 1.6% in Iowa, 1.7% in Nebraska, 1.9% in North Dakota, 2.1% in Missouri and 2.3% in South Dakota, before inflation. Those are among the slowest rates in the country. The eastern states ran faster: 4.4% a year in Illinois and Ohio, 3.7% in Michigan and 3.6% in Indiana.

Our model assumes prices rise 2.5% a year. In the western half of the region that is above the recent trend, so the paybacks above lean optimistic there. Solar payback if electricity prices rise shows the same house under different price paths.

How exports are credited

Minnesota is the state where we could confirm the rule from the regulator's own page. The Minnesota Public Utilities Commission says a rooftop system under 40 kW AC can choose to be paid the "average retail utility energy rate" for surplus energy, with use and generation balanced each month and payment by check or bill credit. The rate is updated every year. Cooperative and municipal utilities follow different rules, and the commission notes that their year-end excess credits are canceled with no extra pay.

For the other eleven states, ask your utility how an exported kWh is credited and how often credits are settled, and check its tariff with your state utility commission, such as the Illinois Commerce Commission, the Michigan Public Service Commission, the Indiana Utility Regulatory Commission or the Public Utilities Commission of Ohio. The half- and quarter-credit columns show what is at stake: in Iowa, the difference between 15.6 and 24.5 years. Net metering vs. net billing explains the arrangements.

Winter

Midwest winters cut output more than in the South: the summer-to-winter output ratio is 2 or more in Michigan, Wisconsin, Illinois, Ohio and North Dakota. Where credits are settled monthly instead of yearly, that costs more; in our Minnesota example, monthly settlement added half a year. Solar panels in winter by state compares every state.

Before you get quotes

  1. Total 12 months of kWh and compare with your state's average above.
  2. Find out how your utility credits exports and when credits are settled.
  3. Compare quotes on price per watt; the example uses $3.50.
  4. Run your numbers in the solar calculator, at flat prices as well as rising ones.

If your rate is closer to Michigan's than to Nebraska's, solar deserves a quote. Near the bottom of the table, weigh it against cheaper ways to cut your bill. Local detail is on the Michigan, Illinois and Minnesota pages.
