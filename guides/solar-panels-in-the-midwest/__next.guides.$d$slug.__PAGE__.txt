23:Tcc6,<div class="short-answer"><h2 id="short-answer">Short answer</h2>
<p>Solar in the Midwest is a tale of two halves. Where power prices run high, as in Michigan, Ohio, Illinois and Wisconsin, the example system pays itself back in about 11 to 13 years with full export credit. Where prices are low, as in North Dakota and Nebraska, the same system takes much longer. Winter sun is weaker, but price matters more than sunshine here.</p>
<p>The numbers below use our example home: a 7 kW system costing $24,500, 10,500 kWh a year of use, and no incentives. See the <a href="/methodology/">methodology</a> for the rest.</p>
</div><h2 id="start-with-the-price-not-the-sun">Start with the price, not the sun</h2>
<p>A kilowatt-hour (kWh) is the unit your utility bills you for. Each kWh a panel makes saves you what the grid would have charged. So a higher rate means faster payback.</p>
<p>The Midwest splits into two price zones in the federal data (EIA, August 2026):</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>East North Central</strong> (Illinois, Indiana, Michigan, Ohio, Wisconsin): 19.6¢/kWh, up from 18.11¢ a year earlier, a change of 8.2%.</li>
<li><strong>West North Central</strong> (Iowa, Kansas, Minnesota, Missouri, Nebraska, North Dakota, South Dakota): 15.92¢/kWh, up from 15.17¢, a change of 4.9%.</li>
</ul>
<p>The U.S. residential average was 18.16¢/kWh. So the eastern Midwest sits above the national figure and the western Midwest sits below it. For a wider view, see <a href="/guides/electricity-prices-by-region/">electricity prices by region</a>.</p>

37:Tce7,<h2 id="what-the-table-shows">What the table shows</h2>
<p>Compare Kansas and Michigan. Kansas gets more output per kW, 1526 kWh against 1291 kWh. Yet Michigan pays back faster, 11.1 years against 13.8 years, because its price is 22.99¢ against 15.47¢. Price beats sunshine.</p>
<p>Now compare Indiana and Iowa. Their prices are nearly the same, 17.02¢ and 17.06¢, and so are their paybacks, 14.3 and 13.9 years. The small gap comes from Iowa&#39;s slightly better output.</p>
<p>Last, look at North Dakota. Its output is close to Illinois, but its price is 13.31¢ against 19.32¢. That gap stretches payback from 12.9 to 18.3 years.</p>
<h2 id="what-changes-the-result">What changes the result</h2>
<p><strong>Export credit.</strong> In every row, payback gets longer when credit drops. Moving from full to half credit adds roughly four to six years in this table. Your utility&#39;s rules decide which case you get. Read <a href="/guides/net-metering-vs-net-billing-payback/">net metering vs. net billing</a> before you sign anything.</p>
<p><strong>Winter.</strong> Snow cover and short days cut winter output. Annual figures already average this in, but monthly bills will swing. Our <a href="/guides/solar-panels-in-winter-by-state/">winter solar guide</a> covers the pattern state by state.</p>
<p><strong>Price trends.</strong> Prices rose in all twelve of these states over the past year in the data we use, though by very different amounts. Ohio rose 10.4%, Michigan 10.9%, Iowa 10.2% and Minnesota 1.8%. Our example home assumes prices climb 2.5% a year, which is a modeling choice, not a forecast.</p>
<p><strong>Incentives.</strong> The 30% federal Residential Clean Energy Credit (Section 25D) is not available for property placed in service after December 31, 2025. These paybacks assume no incentives. Check <a href="https://programs.dsireusa.org/">DSIRE</a> for state and utility programs that may still apply.</p>
<p><strong>Install cost.</strong> Berkeley Lab reports a median installed price of $4.0 per watt for host-owned systems installed in 2024, higher than the $3.5 per watt we model. A higher quote lengthens payback.</p>
<h2 id="practical-next-steps">Practical next steps</h2>
<ol>
<li>Find your rate on your bill. Compare it with your state&#39;s average on the <a href="/electricity-prices/">electricity prices</a> page.</li>
<li>Ask your utility how it credits exported power. Get the answer in writing.</li>
<li>Run your own numbers in the <a href="/solar-calculator/">solar calculator</a>.</li>
<li>Get several quotes and compare the price per watt.</li>
<li>Look at your state page, such as <a href="/solar-panels/michigan/">Michigan</a>, <a href="/solar-panels/illinois/">Illinois</a> or <a href="/solar-panels/ohio/">Ohio</a>.</li>
</ol>
<p>If your rate is on the low side, trimming use first can be worth more than panels. See <a href="/guides/how-to-lower-your-electric-bill/">how to lower your electric bill</a>.</p>
<h2 id="bottom-line">Bottom line</h2>
<p>In the Midwest, your rate and your export credit matter more than your latitude. Michigan, Wisconsin, Illinois and Ohio show the shortest example paybacks. North Dakota and Nebraska show the longest. These are modeled estimates, not quotes. Your utility, roof and installer will change the result.</p>
