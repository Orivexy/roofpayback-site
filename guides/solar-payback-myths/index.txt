36:Tdc4,<div class="short-answer"><h2 id="the-verdict">The verdict</h2>
<p>A five-year solar payback is the exception, not the rule. In the August 2026 numbers, Hawaii is the only state under five years (<strong>4.8 years</strong>), and most states land in the teens. A long payback is not the same as a losing investment, though, and panel wear barely moves the result.</p>
<p>The four claims below are checked against the state figures behind our <a href="/solar-calculator/">solar calculator</a>. Payback means the years it takes for bill savings to cover the up-front cost. Each figure uses the same 7 kW example home with no incentives (see the <a href="/methodology/">methodology</a>).</p>
</div><h2 id="myth-solar-pays-for-itself-in-five-or-six-years">Myth: Solar pays for itself in five or six years</h2>
<p><strong>Fact:</strong> Hawaii gets there, at 4.8 years with full export credit and the nation&#39;s highest residential price, 47.18¢ per kWh. California is next at 7.4 years, then Maine at 8, Massachusetts at 8.5 and New York at 9. For most of the country the figure sits in the teens: Texas at 14.3 years, Alabama and Georgia at 14.5.</p>
<p>So where does a five-year promise come from? Usually from one of two assumptions.</p>
<p>The first is the old federal tax credit. The 30% Residential Clean Energy Credit ended for systems placed in service after December 31, 2025. Applied to the example home as a hypothetical, it would have brought California to 5.4 years, Maine to 5.7 and Massachusetts to 6.1, but Texas only to 10.5. Even with the credit, five or six years was a result for a handful of high-price states. A quote that still counts it is out of date; the <a href="/guides/is-solar-worth-it-without-tax-credit/">no-tax-credit guide</a> covers what is left.</p>
<p>The second is a low installed price. At $2.50 per watt instead of $3.50, California pays back in 5.5 years and New York in 6.6. Texas still needs 10.6 years and Florida 10.5. The <a href="/guides/solar-panel-cost-by-state/">cost by state guide</a> shows how price per watt feeds the result.</p>
<p>Outside Hawaii, a five-year figure therefore needs an expensive grid plus either a subsidy or an unusually cheap system. Ask the seller which one they are assuming.</p>
<h2 id="myth-a-payback-in-the-teens-means-solar-loses-money">Myth: A payback in the teens means solar loses money</h2>
<p><strong>Fact:</strong> Payback marks break-even, not the end of the system&#39;s working life. The example home is modeled over 25 years, and savings keep arriving after the cost is covered.</p>
<p>Take Texas. The $24,500 system saves $1,583 in its first year and pays back in 14.3 years. That leaves more than a decade of the modeled 25 years in which every dollar saved, net of $100 a year of upkeep, is a return rather than a repayment. Florida (14.1 years) and Arizona (13.9 years) follow the same arc. In California, with year-1 savings of $3,124 and a 7.4-year payback, the profit years outnumber the repayment years by more than two to one.</p>
<p>That picture assumes electricity prices rise 2.5% a year. Hold prices flat and the Texas payback stretches to 17.3 years, leaving a much thinner margin. A higher quote, a shaded roof or a weak export credit could then erase most of the gain.</p>
<p>A slow payback leaves less room for error, but it does not mean the money is gone. To see how the price path shapes the outcome, read <a href="/guides/solar-payback-if-electricity-prices-rise/">solar payback if electricity prices rise</a>.</p>

45:Tf28,<h2 id="myth-panels-wear-out-before-they-pay-for-themselves">Myth: Panels wear out before they pay for themselves</h2>
<p><strong>Fact:</strong> Panels lose a little output each year, and the effect on payback is small. The base model assumes a loss of 0.5% a year, so the panels still deliver close to nine-tenths of their first-year output in year 25. A faster rate of 0.8% a year leaves them above four-fifths.</p>
<p>That faster rate moves payback very little:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Texas:</strong> 14.3 years becomes 14.6.</li>
<li><strong>New York:</strong> 9 years becomes 9.2.</li>
<li><strong>California:</strong> 7.4 years either way.</li>
</ul>
<p>Wear shows up later, in the 25-year total rather than in the break-even date. Most of the lost output falls after the system has already paid for itself, because the panels are strongest in the early years when the cost is being repaid.</p>
<p>Check the paperwork instead of worrying in the abstract: ask the installer to put in writing how much output the panel warranty promises over time. The <a href="/guides/solar-panel-degradation-payback/">panel degradation guide</a> walks through the full comparison.</p>
<h2 id="myth-a-bigger-electric-bill-means-a-faster-payback">Myth: A bigger electric bill means a faster payback</h2>
<p><strong>Fact:</strong> A bigger bill brings bigger yearly savings, not a quicker break-even. When each home gets a system sized to its own use, cost and savings grow together.</p>
<p>In Texas, a small home with a right-sized 4 kW system pays back in 14.9 years. A large home with an 11 kW system pays back in 14.1. Yearly savings nearly triple between the two, but the payback barely moves. California shows the same shape: 7.6 years for the small home, 7 years for the large one.</p>
<p>A heavy user has more dollars at stake each year, which is a good reason to look closely. The <a href="/guides/solar-for-high-electricity-use/">high-usage homes guide</a> compares four states in detail.</p>
<h2 id="myths-with-their-own-guides">Myths with their own guides</h2>
<p>Several other popular claims are answered at length elsewhere on the site:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>&quot;Solar only works in sunny states.&quot;</strong> The <a href="/guides/cloudy-states-solar-worth-it/">cloudy states guide</a> shows low-sun states with high prices beating the desert.</li>
<li><strong>&quot;Sunshine sets the ranking.&quot;</strong> The <a href="/guides/best-states-for-solar-payback/">best states for solar payback</a> ranking shows price doing most of the sorting.</li>
<li><strong>&quot;The export credit is a detail.&quot;</strong> <a href="/guides/net-metering-vs-net-billing-payback/">Net metering vs. net billing</a> shows it can add years.</li>
<li><strong>&quot;Fill the roof to save more.&quot;</strong> The <a href="/guides/oversized-solar-system-annual-true-up/">oversized system guide</a> explains why panels beyond a year of use earn nothing in the model.</li>
</ul>
<h2 id="what-actually-decides-it">What actually decides it</h2>
<p>Four inputs do the work, in this order:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Your price per kWh.</strong> It sets what each saved unit is worth and decides most of the ranking.</li>
<li><strong>Your export credit.</strong> It comes second, and where exports earn only a fraction of retail it can outweigh a good price.</li>
<li><strong>Your installed price.</strong> Each step down in cost per kW shortens the wait.</li>
<li><strong>Your sunshine.</strong> It matters, but it rarely overturns the first three.</li>
</ul>
<p>Panel wear and household size, two common worries, sit well below that list. When someone quotes you a payback, ask which price, which export credit and which cost per kW sit behind it. If any of the three is missing, treat the number as a sales line and rebuild it in the <a href="/solar-calculator/">solar calculator</a> with your own bill.</p>
