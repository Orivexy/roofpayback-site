Home EV Charging Cost by State: What It Costs in 2026

What home EV charging costs in every state per year and per mile at September 2026 EIA prices, and how it compares with gasoline at $4.36 a gallon.

In one minute

Charging an electric car at home costs whatever your home electricity costs, so your state sets most of the answer. For an example of 3,500 kWh of home charging a year, the cost at September 2026 prices runs from about $464 in Nevada to about $1,691 in Hawaii.

Per mile, a small electric SUV rated at 26 kWh per 100 miles costs about 4.7¢ to charge at the U.S. average price, against about 13.6¢ a mile for gasoline in a comparable 32-mpg gas model. At the September 2026 national gas price, home charging is cheaper per mile in every state, even Hawaii.

These are estimates built from state averages. The steps below show how to swap in your own rate and your own car.

Step 1: Learn the formula

Price per kWh × kWh per mile = cost per mile. EV efficiency is published in kWh per 100 miles. The Department of Energy's Alternative Fuels Data Center (AFDC) gives this example: "If electricity costs ¢10.7 per kWh and the vehicle consumes 27 kWh to travel 100 miles, the cost per mile is about $0.03."

For a yearly figure, multiply your price by the kilowatt-hours you charge at home in a year.

The spread comes almost entirely from the price per kWh. The bottom of the table is the South and the interior West, the top is the Northeast, California, Alaska and Hawaii. A less efficient car costs proportionally more per mile: a hypothetical model using 39 kWh per 100 miles, half again as much as the bZ, would cost half again as much per mile in every state.

Step 3: Compare with gasoline

The EIA's average U.S. price for regular gasoline was $4.355 a gallon in September 2026, up from $3.166 a year earlier. fueleconomy.gov rates the 2026 Toyota Corolla Cross, a gasoline small SUV, at 32 mpg combined. That works out to about 13.6¢ a mile for fuel.

Driving the same 13,460 miles that 3,500 kWh buys in the bZ would take about $1,832 of gasoline at that price. Home charging the bZ for those miles costs $464 to $1,691 depending on the state, and $636 at the U.S. average electricity price of 18.17¢.

The break-even works out to about 52.3¢ per kWh: below that electricity price, charging this EV at home costs less per mile than fueling the gas model. Hawaii's 48.31¢ is the only state average that comes close. Two limits: gas prices vary by state, and this comparison uses the national average; and it covers energy only, not purchase price, insurance or maintenance.

Step 4: Swap in the rate from your own bill

Your bill's per-kWh price is a better guide than the state average, for one reason: fixed monthly customer charges stay the same however much you charge, while the EIA average spreads them over every kWh. For the extra cost of an EV, use the per-kWh charges only; fixed charges on your electric bill shows how to separate them.

Then estimate your own kWh: your yearly miles times your car's kWh per 100 miles, divided by 100. Recent months of EIA data are preliminary and can be revised, and prices move: Hawaii's average rose 22.2% in a year, while Connecticut's fell 10.9%.

Step 5: Ask your utility about time-of-use or EV rates

The AFDC notes that "Some utilities offer residential time-of-use rates or other rate incentives for charging infrastructure owners." On some plans the overnight price is a fraction of the peak price; time-of-use rates explained prices out moving 3,500 kWh of charging on two real 2026 tariffs.

Step 6: Pick equipment before you pay for any

Try the cord that came with the car first. That is the basic Level 1 charger, and the AFDC says many owners can meet daily needs with it, "requiring no additional cost or installation, provided that a power outlet on a dedicated branch circuit is available near their parking location." A faster Level 2 charger may need an electrician and a permit.

Keep public charging separate in your budget. The AFDC says "Most EV owners do the majority of their charging at home", but public charging is priced differently and is not in this table.

Step 7: Decide whether solar belongs in the plan

The price column above sets what every kWh from rooftop panels is worth, so solar does the most for EV owners in high-price states. But many people charge overnight, after the panels stop, so the value of daytime output depends on how your utility credits power sent to the grid; in California and Hawaii, new customers no longer get full retail credit. Solar and electric car charging works through that case, and how many solar panels you need shows how an EV's kWh change system size. Test your numbers in the solar calculator.

Before you plug in: a checklist

  • Find the per-kWh price on your latest bill, adding supply and delivery charges if they are listed separately.
  • Look up your car's kWh per 100 miles on fueleconomy.gov and multiply by your yearly miles.
  • Multiply the two to get your yearly home charging cost, and check it against your state's row.
  • Ask your utility whether it offers a time-of-use or EV rate, and which hours are off-peak.
  • Try the Level 1 cord for a few weeks before buying a Level 2 charger.
  • If solar is on the table, count the EV's kWh before you size a system.
