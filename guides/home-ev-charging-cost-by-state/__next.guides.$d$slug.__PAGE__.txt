22:T2199,<div class="short-answer"><p class="short-answer-label">In one minute</p>
<p>Charging an electric car at home costs whatever your home electricity costs, so your state sets most of the answer. For an example of 3,500 kWh of home charging a year, the cost at September 2026 prices runs from about <strong>$464 in Nevada to about $1,691 in Hawaii</strong>.</p>
<p>Per mile, a small electric SUV rated at 26 kWh per 100 miles costs about 4.7¢ to charge at the U.S. average price, against about 13.6¢ a mile for gasoline in a comparable 32-mpg gas model. At the September 2026 national gas price, home charging is cheaper per mile in every state, even Hawaii.</p>
<p>These are estimates built from state averages. The steps below show how to swap in your own rate and your own car.</p>
</div><h2 id="step-1-learn-the-formula">Step 1: Learn the formula</h2>
<p><strong>Price per kWh × kWh per mile = cost per mile.</strong> EV efficiency is published in kWh per 100 miles. The Department of Energy&#39;s Alternative Fuels Data Center (AFDC) gives this example: &quot;If electricity costs ¢10.7 per kWh and the vehicle consumes 27 kWh to travel 100 miles, the cost per mile is about $0.03.&quot;</p>
<p>For a yearly figure, multiply your price by the <a href="/guides/what-is-a-kilowatt-hour/">kilowatt-hours</a> you charge at home in a year.</p>

36:T1138,<h2 id="step-3-compare-with-gasoline">Step 3: Compare with gasoline</h2>
<p>The EIA&#39;s average U.S. price for regular gasoline was $4.355 a gallon in September 2026, up from $3.166 a year earlier. fueleconomy.gov rates the 2026 Toyota Corolla Cross, a gasoline small SUV, at 32 mpg combined. That works out to about 13.6¢ a mile for fuel.</p>
<p>Driving the same 13,460 miles that 3,500 kWh buys in the bZ would take about $1,832 of gasoline at that price. Home charging the bZ for those miles costs $464 to $1,691 depending on the state, and $636 at the U.S. average electricity price of 18.17¢.</p>
<p>The break-even works out to about 52.3¢ per kWh: below that electricity price, charging this EV at home costs less per mile than fueling the gas model. Hawaii&#39;s 48.31¢ is the only state average that comes close. Two limits: gas prices vary by state, and this comparison uses the national average; and it covers energy only, not purchase price, insurance or maintenance.</p>
<h2 id="step-4-swap-in-the-rate-from-your-own-bill">Step 4: Swap in the rate from your own bill</h2>
<p>Your bill&#39;s per-kWh price is a better guide than the state average, for one reason: fixed monthly customer charges stay the same however much you charge, while the EIA average spreads them over every kWh. For the extra cost of an EV, use the per-kWh charges only; <a href="/guides/fixed-charges-on-your-electric-bill/">fixed charges on your electric bill</a> shows how to separate them.</p>
<p>Then estimate your own kWh: your yearly miles times your car&#39;s kWh per 100 miles, divided by 100. Recent months of EIA data are preliminary and can be revised, and prices move: Hawaii&#39;s average rose 22.2% in a year, while Connecticut&#39;s fell 10.9%.</p>
<h2 id="step-5-ask-your-utility-about-time-of-use-or-ev-rates">Step 5: Ask your utility about time-of-use or EV rates</h2>
<p>The AFDC notes that &quot;Some utilities offer residential time-of-use rates or other rate incentives for charging infrastructure owners.&quot; On some plans the overnight price is a fraction of the peak price; <a href="/guides/time-of-use-electricity-rates-explained/">time-of-use rates explained</a> prices out moving 3,500 kWh of charging on two real 2026 tariffs.</p>
<h2 id="step-6-pick-equipment-before-you-pay-for-any">Step 6: Pick equipment before you pay for any</h2>
<p>Try the cord that came with the car first. That is the basic Level 1 charger, and the AFDC says many owners can meet daily needs with it, &quot;requiring no additional cost or installation, provided that a power outlet on a dedicated branch circuit is available near their parking location.&quot; A faster Level 2 charger may need an electrician and a permit.</p>
<p>Keep public charging separate in your budget. The AFDC says &quot;Most EV owners do the majority of their charging at home&quot;, but public charging is priced differently and is not in this table.</p>
<h2 id="step-7-decide-whether-solar-belongs-in-the-plan">Step 7: Decide whether solar belongs in the plan</h2>
<p>The price column above sets what every kWh from rooftop panels is worth, so solar does the most for EV owners in high-price states. But many people charge overnight, after the panels stop, so the value of daytime output depends on how your utility credits power sent to the grid; in California and Hawaii, new customers no longer get full retail credit. <a href="/guides/solar-and-electric-car-charging/">Solar and electric car charging</a> works through that case, and <a href="/guides/how-many-solar-panels-do-i-need/">how many solar panels you need</a> shows how an EV&#39;s kWh change system size. Test your numbers in the <a href="/solar-calculator/">solar calculator</a>.</p>
<h2 id="before-you-plug-in-a-checklist">Before you plug in: a checklist</h2>
<ul>
<li>Find the per-kWh price on your latest bill, adding supply and delivery charges if they are listed separately.</li>
<li>Look up your car&#39;s kWh per 100 miles on fueleconomy.gov and multiply by your yearly miles.</li>
<li>Multiply the two to get your yearly home charging cost, and check it against your state&#39;s row.</li>
<li>Ask your utility whether it offers a time-of-use or EV rate, and which hours are off-peak.</li>
<li>Try the Level 1 cord for a few weeks before buying a Level 2 charger.</li>
<li>If solar is on the table, count the EV&#39;s kWh before you size a system.</li>
</ul>
