22:T1dae,<div class="short-answer"><h2 id="in-one-minute">In one minute</h2>
<p>Charging an electric car at home costs whatever your home electricity costs. That makes your state a big part of the answer. For an example of 3,500 kWh of home charging per year, the yearly cost runs from about $445 in Nevada to about $1,651 in Hawaii.</p>
<p>Those are estimates, not quotes. They use each state&#39;s average home electricity price for August 2026 from the U.S. Energy Information Administration (EIA). The steps below show you how to swap in your own bill and your own driving.</p>
</div><h2 id="step-1-learn-the-formula">Step 1: Learn the formula</h2>
<p><strong>Price per kWh × kWh you charge = your charging cost.</strong></p>
<p>Both inputs are counted in <a href="/guides/what-is-a-kilowatt-hour/">kilowatt-hours</a> (kWh), and EV efficiency is often given in kWh per 100 miles. The Department of Energy&#39;s Alternative Fuels Data Center (AFDC) walks through the math with this example: &quot;If electricity costs ¢10.7 per kWh and the vehicle consumes 27 kWh to travel 100 miles, the cost per mile is about $0.03.&quot;</p>
<p>That one line is the whole method. Everything else in this guide is about getting the two inputs right.</p>

35:T166f,<h2 id="step-3-know-what-the-average-hides">Step 3: Know what the average hides</h2>
<p>Treat the state figure as a rough guide. EIA&#39;s average is utility revenue, including taxes and fees, divided by kWh sold. The rate printed on your bill, or an EV time-of-use rate, may be higher or lower.</p>
<p>The 3,500 kWh figure is just an example, not a national average. It also assumes all charging happens at home, with no special EV rate.</p>
<p>Prices move, too. EIA publishes them monthly in its Electric Power Monthly, and recent months are preliminary, so they can be revised. Hawaii&#39;s average was 38.53¢ a year earlier, a change of 22.5%. Maine went from 28.14¢ to 32.11¢. Connecticut fell, from 30.17¢ to 24.51¢. Identical driving can cost more or less next year. Our <a href="/electricity-prices/">electricity prices by state</a> page carries the latest numbers, and the <a href="/methodology/">methodology</a> explains how we use them.</p>
<h2 id="step-4-swap-in-the-rate-from-your-own-bill">Step 4: Swap in the rate from your own bill</h2>
<p>Pull out a recent bill and find the price per kWh. That is the better guide to what charging adds, for one reason: fixed monthly customer charges stay the same no matter how much you charge. The EIA average spreads those fees over every kWh, so it is a rougher guide to the extra cost of an EV.</p>
<p>Next, estimate your own kWh. Drive less than the example and you&#39;ll charge less than 3,500 kWh a year; drive more and the number climbs. Multiply the two, and you have a personal estimate.</p>
<h2 id="step-5-ask-your-utility-about-time-of-use-rates">Step 5: Ask your utility about time-of-use rates</h2>
<p>Call or check your utility&#39;s website. The AFDC notes that &quot;Some utilities offer residential time-of-use rates or other rate incentives for charging infrastructure owners.&quot; A time-of-use rate charges different prices at different hours. If one is offered, charging off-peak may cost less than the state average.</p>
<h2 id="step-6-pick-equipment-before-you-pay-for-any">Step 6: Pick equipment before you pay for any</h2>
<p>Try the cord that came with the car first. That is the basic Level 1 charger, and the AFDC says many owners can meet daily needs with it, &quot;requiring no additional cost or installation, provided that a power outlet on a dedicated branch circuit is available near their parking location.&quot; A faster Level 2 charger may need an electrician, and local permits may be required.</p>
<p>Keep public charging separate in your budget. The AFDC says &quot;Most EV owners do the majority of their charging at home&quot;, but charging away from home is priced differently and isn&#39;t in this table.</p>
<h2 id="step-7-decide-whether-solar-belongs-in-the-plan">Step 7: Decide whether solar belongs in the plan</h2>
<p>Rooftop solar can lower charging costs, but how much varies by home and utility. The state price in the table above sets the value of every kWh the panels replace, so solar does the most for EV owners in the high-price states. Panels make power while the sun is up, though, and many people charge overnight. What the daytime output is worth then comes down to how your utility credits power you send to the grid; <a href="/guides/net-metering-vs-net-billing-payback/">net metering vs. net billing</a> explains the difference. Net metering and solar incentives vary by state and utility, and the DSIRE database lists current programs.</p>
<p>An EV raises your yearly use, which can change what size system makes sense. <a href="/guides/solar-and-electric-car-charging/">Solar and electric car charging</a> works through that case, and <a href="/guides/solar-for-high-electricity-use/">solar for high electricity use</a> covers homes with big loads in general. Test your own numbers in the <a href="/solar-calculator/">solar calculator</a>, read <a href="/guides/how-many-solar-panels-do-i-need/">how many solar panels you need</a>, or browse <a href="/solar-panels/">solar panels by state</a>.</p>
<h2 id="mistakes-that-cost-money">Mistakes that cost money</h2>
<ul>
<li><strong>Budgeting from the state average alone.</strong> Your bill&#39;s rate per kWh is the better guide.</li>
<li><strong>Assuming last year&#39;s price still holds.</strong> Some states rose sharply in a year; a few fell.</li>
<li><strong>Buying a Level 2 charger by reflex.</strong> The included cord may cover daily needs if a dedicated circuit is near where you park.</li>
<li><strong>Ignoring time-of-use plans.</strong> Off-peak charging may be cheaper, but only if your utility offers it and you use it.</li>
<li><strong>Sizing solar before counting the EV.</strong> A system quoted on last year&#39;s bills can come up short once home charging is added.</li>
</ul>
<h2 id="your-checklist">Your checklist</h2>
<ul>
<li>Find the price per kWh on your latest bill, adding supply and delivery charges if they are listed separately.</li>
<li>Estimate how many kWh a year you will charge at home, based on your miles and your car&#39;s kWh per 100 miles.</li>
<li>Multiply the two to get your yearly home charging cost.</li>
<li>Check your result against your state&#39;s row in the table above to see whether your rate runs high or low.</li>
<li>Ask your utility whether it offers a time-of-use or EV rate, and which hours are off-peak.</li>
<li>Try the Level 1 cord that came with the car for a few weeks before buying a Level 2 charger.</li>
<li>Confirm with an electrician whether a Level 2 charger needs a new circuit or a permit where you live.</li>
<li>Set aside a separate budget line for public charging on trips.</li>
<li>If solar is on the table, ask your utility how it credits exported power before you size a system.</li>
</ul>
