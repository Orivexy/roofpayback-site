Solar Panel Cost Per Watt and Payback by State

What a solar quote's price per watt means for payback in your state: national installed-price ranges, a three-quote example and the numbers to check first.

Published October 22, 2026·Updated November 18, 2026·7 min read·RoofPayback editorial·Figures checked against EIA and PVWatts data

Short version

A solar quote is easiest to judge as a price per watt: the installed total divided by the system's size. Berkeley Lab put the median for host-owned home systems installed in 2024 at $4.00 per watt, with the middle of the market (the 20th to 80th percentile) between $3.00 and $5.20, in 2024 dollars and before incentives. Cash purchases had a median of $3.50 per watt; loan-financed systems, $4.70.

The same price per watt pays back at very different speeds from state to state. Your electricity price decides most of that. How your utility credits exported power comes second, and where credits are low it can outweigh a good price. At $3,500 per kW, our example system pays back in 7.4 years in California and 14.3 years in Texas. A bid of $2,500 per kW instead saves about four years in Texas, but less than two in California.

Use the sections below to gather the right facts, question the installer, spot weak quotes and work out the payback yourself.

What "installed price" covers

Installed price is the total for the finished system divided by its size. It usually covers panels, inverter, mounting, wiring, labor, permits and the installer's margin.

Installers quote in watts or in kilowatts (kW, a thousand watts). So $3.00 per watt is the same as $3,000 per kW, and $3.50 per watt is $3,500 per kW. For a 7 kW system, that turns into these totals:

  • $2,500 per kW is $17,500.
  • $3,000 per kW is $21,000.
  • $3,500 per kW is $24,500.

The Berkeley Lab medians come from its Tracking the Sun series and describe the national market. This page does not list an installed price for each state. What shifts most from one state to the next is how much each kilowatt-hour your panels make is worth, and that is where the payback gap comes from. Your own quote can sit above or below the national band depending on the roof, local labor, permits and equipment.

Before you ask for quotes

  • Add up a year of use. Twelve months of bills give you a yearly kilowatt-hour total, which sets the system size. How many solar panels you need turns that total into kW and a panel count.
  • Find your price per kWh. It is the first thing that decides payback. Compare your rate with your state on the electricity prices pages. EIA put the U.S. residential average at 18.16¢ in August 2026, up from 17.57¢ a year earlier.
  • Learn your utility's export rule. It is the second thing that decides payback. Programs vary by state and utility, and DSIRE lists the current ones.
  • Budget with no federal credit. The IRS says the 30% Residential Clean Energy Credit (Section 25D) is not available for property placed in service after December 31, 2025. The IRS page has the current rule.
  • Decide how you would pay. Cash and loan prices differ in the Berkeley Lab data, so know which one you are comparing. Solar loan vs. cash covers the trade-off.

Questions to ask the installer

  1. What is the price per watt? Get it in writing, not just a total or a monthly payment.
  2. What does the price include? Ask for equipment, labor and permits as separate lines.
  3. Is this the cash price or the financed price? The medians were $3.50 per watt for cash and $4.70 for loans.
  4. Which panels and inverter, and how long are the warranties? Two bids at the same price can include very different equipment.
  5. What yearly production does the estimate assume, and which tool produced it? Compare it with PVWatts, the estimator our methodology uses.
  6. What export credit does the savings estimate assume? Ask for the rate and whether your utility can change it.
  7. Does the estimate count any incentive? If so, get the program name and look it up on DSIRE.

Collect several quotes so the answers line up side by side. Our solar quote checklist goes deeper on reading the production and savings pages.

Red flags

  • A savings estimate that still counts the federal 30% credit for a system placed in service after 2025.
  • A price per watt well outside $3.00 to $5.20 with no clear reason. Prices vary, but you should know what drives yours.
  • A low price per watt on a system that doesn't fit. A system sized well below your use, or panels placed on a poor roof face, can save less than a pricier bid. See roof pitch and east- or west-facing roofs.
  • A payback that assumes full export credit without naming your utility's rule.
  • No itemized price. Without it, two quotes can't be compared fairly.
  • A loan framed only as a monthly payment. Financed systems had the higher median price per watt, so ask for the full cost.

Numbers to check yourself

Three bids for the same house

Picture our example home in Houston, a 7 kW system with no incentives (full assumptions on the methodology page), getting three bids for identical work:

  • $3,500 per kW ($24,500): payback in 14.3 years. That bid matches the cash median.
  • $3,000 per kW ($21,000): payback falls to 12.5 years.
  • $2,500 per kW ($17,500): payback drops to 10.6 years, roughly four years sooner than the first bid. The roof, the bills and the panels' output are unchanged; only the upfront price moved.

The same three bids in Texas and four other states:

State $2,500/kW ($17,500) $3,000/kW ($21,000) $3,500/kW ($24,500)
Texas 10.6 years 12.5 years 14.3 years
Florida 10.5 years 12.3 years 14.1 years
Arizona 10.4 years 12.2 years 13.9 years
New York 6.6 years 7.9 years 9 years
California 5.5 years 6.5 years 7.4 years

Why a cheaper bid matters more in some states

Payback is roughly the system cost divided by each year's savings. In Los Angeles the example system saves $3,124 in its first year; in Houston it saves $1,583. With savings that large, California earns back each of the three bids quickly, so the gap between bids shrinks to under two years (7.4 years against 5.5). New York sits in between, moving from 9 years to 6.6.

A lower price per watt is therefore worth most where each solar kWh saves the least. It counts for the most when two things line up:

  • Moderate electricity prices. Texas, Florida and Arizona all move by several years between the cheapest and the dearest bid.
  • No incentives. Nothing else brings the cost down.

Sunshine alone does not close the gap. Phoenix produces 1,755 kWh a year per kW, more than Los Angeles at 1,677. Yet Arizona's average residential price was 15.05¢ per kWh in August 2026 against California's 29.75¢, so each kWh from a Phoenix roof saves about half as much. Why electricity prices differ by state explains where those rates come from.

Payback at the same price, state by state

At $3,500 per kW with full export credit, a wider set of states shows the same pattern:

State (reference city) Price Example payback
Hawaii (Honolulu) 47.18¢/kWh 4.8 years
California (Los Angeles) 29.75¢/kWh 7.4 years
Maine (Portland) 32.11¢/kWh 8 years
Massachusetts (Boston) 30.48¢/kWh 8.5 years
New York (New York City) 28.65¢/kWh 9 years
Nevada (Las Vegas) 12.72¢/kWh 16.1 years
Washington (Seattle) 14.63¢/kWh 19.6 years

Las Vegas produces 1,753 kWh per kW, almost as much as Phoenix, but its low price keeps payback past 16 years. Seattle pairs a modest price with 1,088 kWh per kW, so it waits the longest of the group. In low-price states a cheaper bid is often the main lever a buyer controls; solar where electricity is cheap looks at that case, and best states for solar payback ranks every state.

Then test the export rule

A good price can still be undone by a weak export credit. In Birmingham, Alabama, the example payback is 14.5 years at full credit, 19.2 years at half credit and 22.9 years at quarter credit. Net metering vs. net billing explains the rules behind those cases.

Run your winning bid

  1. Divide the quoted total by the system size in kW.
  2. Set the result against the tables above and the $3.00 to $5.20 per-watt band.
  3. Enter it in the solar calculator with your own electricity rate.
  4. Check your monthly use in the electricity cost calculator.

State pages have the local inputs: Texas, California, Florida, Arizona and New York, with the rest under solar panels by state.

Decide with confidence

You are ready to choose a bid when you can answer four things:

  • What it costs per watt, and whether that is the cash or the loan price.
  • What you pay per kWh today, since that sets how fast any price earns itself back.
  • How your utility credits exported power.
  • Whether the cheapest bid covers the same equipment, size and roof layout as the others.

Where power is expensive, a fair price per watt is usually enough, and the export terms deserve most of your attention. Where power costs near or below the national average, the price per watt is where years of payback are won or lost, so it pays to collect more bids before signing.
