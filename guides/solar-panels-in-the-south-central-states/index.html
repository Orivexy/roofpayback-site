Solar Panels in the South Central States: TX, OK, AR, LA

Texas, Oklahoma, Arkansas and Louisiana have decent sun but low power prices. See solar payback for each, and how export credit rules change it.

The answer up front

Rooftop solar has a mixed payback in Texas, Oklahoma, Arkansas and Louisiana, where good sunshine meets some of the cheapest electricity in the country. In our example home, simple payback runs from 14.3 years in Texas and Oklahoma to 16.7 years in Louisiana, and only if the utility credits every exported kilowatt-hour at the full retail price.

Under weaker credit rules, payback nears or passes 20 years across the region at half credit. At a quarter credit, two states don't reach payback within 25 years at all.

Before the panels go up

Our story follows one household in Oklahoma City, living in our example home: a 7 kW system at $24,500 with no incentives (methodology). Along the way, we check in on the same house in Houston, Little Rock and New Orleans. Texas buyers will find their state covered in more depth, with price-per-watt and buyback-plan checks, in is solar worth it in Texas.

The family starts with a fairly modest bill. At 900 kWh in a month, the Oklahoma statewide average price works out to about $133. The same usage costs about $143 in Texas, about $129 in Arkansas and about $116 in Louisiana. These are illustrations, not forecasts; EIA averages include taxes and fees, and real bills depend on the rate plan.

That low starting point is the whole challenge. Solar saves you the price of every kilowatt-hour (kWh) your panels replace. A cheap kWh means a small saving per panel, however sunny the roof.

EIA data for August 2026 puts the West South Central division, which covers all four states, at 15.3¢ per kWh for homes. The U.S. average was 18.16¢. Businesses in the region paid just 9.54¢ that month. Low prices are kind to monthly bills and hard on solar math. A system that would pay for itself in under ten years in a high-price state needs far longer here; is solar worth it where electricity is cheap looks at that trade-off nationally.

Year 1: the first savings show up

In its first year, the Oklahoma City system saves $1,556, assuming full credit for exports. That is slightly less than the Houston version of the house, which saves $1,583 despite getting less sun. Across the region, with PVWatts output for one reference city per state, the first year looks like this:

State (city) Output per kW a year Residential price Year-1 savings, full credit Payback, full credit
Oklahoma (Oklahoma City) 1,527 kWh 14.82¢ $1,556 14.3 years
Texas (Houston) 1,421 kWh 15.91¢ $1,583 14.3 years
Arkansas (Little Rock) 1,413 kWh 14.37¢ $1,421 15.8 years
Louisiana (New Orleans) 1,479 kWh 12.87¢ $1,332 16.7 years

Oklahoma City has the best sun of the four, yet Houston ties it on payback, because Houston's higher price makes up for lower output. New Orleans gets more sun than Houston but has the lowest price in the group, and it finishes last. In this region, price outweighs sunshine.

The middle years: prices drift

As the years pass, the household's savings depend on what electricity costs. Our example assumes prices rise 2.5% a year. Each kWh the panels make is then worth a bit more than the year before.

Real prices don't move in a straight line. From August 2025 to August 2026, EIA recorded:

  • Arkansas: up 7.8%, from 13.33¢ to 14.37¢
  • Texas: up 3.3%, from 15.4¢ to 15.91¢
  • Oklahoma: up 2.8%, from 14.42¢ to 14.82¢
  • Louisiana: up 2.5%, from 12.55¢ to 12.87¢

Oklahoma's increase over that year sat close to the 2.5% a year the example assumes. A one-year move isn't a trend either way. Our guide on solar payback if electricity prices rise shows how sensitive the result is to that assumption.

Something else quietly shapes these years: when the family uses power. Our example assumes 40% of use happens in daylight. A home that sits empty all day sends more of its output to the grid, so the utility's credit rule matters more.

Break-even: 14.3 to 16.7 years

With full retail credit, the Oklahoma City family reaches payback at 14.3 years, the same moment as the Houston house. Little Rock follows at 15.8 years. New Orleans gets there last, at 16.7 years.

That's the best case. Nowhere in the four-state sample does payback come in under about 14 years.

Past year 20: when credits are weaker

Now rewind and change one thing. Suppose the family's utility pays less than retail for exported power, as some do. Payback slides out:

State Full credit Half credit Quarter credit
Oklahoma 14.3 years 19.6 years 23.8 years
Texas 14.3 years 19.2 years 23.1 years
Arkansas 15.8 years 21.1 years not within 25 years
Louisiana 16.7 years 22.5 years not within 25 years

At half credit, the Oklahoma City house breaks even at 19.6 years. At quarter credit, it waits until 23.8 years. Price set the starting line at 14.3 years; the export rule is what decides whether the family finishes near 14 or near 24. Our guide on net metering vs. net billing payback explains how those rules work.

Year 25: the edge of the horizon

Our model stops at 25 years. In Arkansas and Louisiana, a quarter-rate credit pushes payback beyond that line. That doesn't make solar a bad idea there. It means the result depends heavily on the utility's export policy, which any household can test in the solar calculator once it knows its own rate.

What the family should ask before signing

  • How does my utility credit exports? Get the rate in writing. Full retail, a lower "avoided cost" rate (roughly what the utility would otherwise pay to produce the power) and a time-based rate give very different results.
  • Do we use power while the sun is up? Daytime use cuts exports.
  • What's the all-in price per watt? The example uses $3,500 per kW. Collect several quotes and compare each one's price per watt against that figure.
  • Are there state or local incentives? The IRS ended the 30% federal credit for systems placed in service after 2025, so only state, local and utility programs remain. DSIRE keeps a database of current ones.

If the bill is already low, solar has less to offset, and cheaper steps may deliver more. Our guide on how to lower your electric bill covers them, and electricity prices by state lets you compare.

What would change the result

Solar can work in Texas, Oklahoma, Arkansas and Louisiana, but it's a slower, more policy-dependent investment than in high-price states. For this household, three things would rewrite the timeline: an export credit below retail, which can add close to ten years; a quote below $3,500 per kW, which shortens every row; and a faster rise in local prices than the model assumes. Shifting more use into daylight hours helps too, because it keeps more output away from a weak export rate. Run your own rate and quote through the solar calculator, and check the state page for solar panels where you live before you sign.
