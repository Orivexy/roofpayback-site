Solar Panels in the South Central States: TX, OK, AR, LA

Texas, Oklahoma, Arkansas and Louisiana compared with September 2026 prices, and how a smaller system recovers payback when exports earn avoided cost.

Published November 12, 2026·Updated November 28, 2026·5 min read·RoofPayback editorial·Figures checked against EIA and PVWatts data

Official data: EIA · DOE · IRS · how we check

The answer up front

Texas, Oklahoma, Arkansas and Louisiana, the Census Bureau's West South Central division, pair decent sun with some of the cheapest residential power in the country: 15.4¢ per kWh in September 2026, against 18.17¢ nationally. With exports worth a quarter of retail, our working assumption for this region, our example system takes 23.1 years in Texas, 23.3 in Oklahoma and 24.9 in Arkansas, and does not pay back within 25 years in Louisiana. Full credit for every exported kWh, an upper bound, would bring that down to 14 to 16.2 years.

The quarter case comes from the two rules we could check. In Louisiana, the Public Service Commission's net metering rules credit exports "at Avoided Cost" for customers who install after December 31, 2019, and its 2026 rates are 3.58¢ to 3.99¢ per kWh by utility, close to a quarter of the state's average price; ask your utility whether those rules cover your address. Texas has no statewide net metering, and the buyback depends on your retail plan. We did not verify the rules in Oklahoma or Arkansas. For a household facing a credit like that, the useful question is not whether solar pays back but which system does: in our model, a smaller array used mostly in daylight is the difference between no payback and about 17 years.

The four states at today's prices

Our example home: a 7 kW system at $24,500 using 10,500 kWh a year, no incentives, PVWatts output for one reference city per state, EIA prices for September 2026 (methodology).

Oklahoma City has the best output and Houston the highest price, and they finish within 0.3 years of each other in every column. Louisiana's price rose the most over the year, 8.1%, but is still the lowest of the four.

Big homes, big bills, big systems

Homes here use far more power than the national average. EIA-861M data for October 2025 to September 2026 put the average residential account at 1,231 kWh a month in Louisiana, 1,133 in Texas, 1,107 in Oklahoma and 1,086 in Arkansas, against 885 nationally. Average bills were $161, $181, $154 and $148 a month. Sizing a system to those homes takes 9 to 10 kW, which costs $31,500 to $35,000 at $3.50 per watt. At quarter credit, those systems take 23.1 years in Oklahoma, 23.6 in Texas and 24.9 in Arkansas, and miss 25 years in Louisiana; at full credit, the upper bound, 13.9 to 16 years. Either way, payback changes very little from the example, because cost and savings grow together.

When exports earn far less than retail

The quarter- and half-credit columns are where this region's results diverge from the high-price states. Louisiana's avoided-cost credit is updated each year for each utility; our quarter-credit case is a stand-in for it, slightly below the 2026 rates, not the actual rate. Texas buyers should read is solar worth it in Texas, which covers how credits depend on the retail plan there. In Oklahoma and Arkansas, ask your utility and check its tariff with the Oklahoma Corporation Commission or the Arkansas Public Service Commission.

At quarter credit, two changes make a large difference: a system smaller than the home's use, and more of the use in daylight. For the same 10,500 kWh home:

State 7 kW, quarter credit 4 kW, quarter credit 4 kW, quarter credit, 60% daytime use
Texas 23.1 years 17.8 years 14.9 years
Oklahoma 23.3 years 18.1 years 15 years
Arkansas 24.9 years 19.4 years 16.4 years
Louisiana not within 25 years 20.6 years 17 years

A 4 kW array ($14,000) sends much less to the grid than a 7 kW one, so more of its output replaces power bought at the full retail price. Raising daytime use from 40% to 60% pushes that further. In Louisiana, the combination turns a system that never pays back within 25 years into one that pays back in 17. Solar and daytime electricity use explains how to shift loads, and how many solar panels do I need shows why smaller sizes win when exports are worth little.

The installed price is the other lever. For a home sized to its full use at quarter credit, $3.00 per watt instead of $3.50 shortens payback by about 2.6 to 2.8 years in Texas, Oklahoma and Arkansas.

Prices over the years

Our model assumes 2.5% yearly increases. Comparing 12-month averages ending September 2017 and September 2026 in EIA's utility data, residential prices grew about 4.3% a year in Texas, 3.5% in Louisiana, 3.3% in Arkansas and 2.9% in Oklahoma, before inflation, all above that assumption. A one-year jump like Louisiana's is not a forecast; solar payback if electricity prices rise shows the range.

What the household should ask before signing

  • How are exports credited? Ask for the current rate per kWh and how often credits settle.
  • How much do we use in daylight? Smart-meter data shows it; a home that is empty all day exports more.
  • What size does the installer propose, and why? Where exports earn little, ask for a quote sized to daytime use as well as one sized to the whole year.
  • What is the price per watt? Compare it with the $3.50 used here; solar panel cost by state shows the effect.

Then run your own rate, credit and quote through the solar calculator. Louisiana also borders the Southeast, where prices are similar.
