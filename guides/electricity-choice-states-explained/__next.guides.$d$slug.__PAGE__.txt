24:Tb16,<div class="short-answer"><h2 id="short-answer">Short answer</h2>
<p>Maybe. In some states you can pick the company that supplies the electricity, while your local utility still delivers it and fixes the lines. In other states you cannot. Whether you can switch depends on your state&#39;s rules, so check your state utility commission&#39;s website before you shop. Searches such as &quot;lowest electricity rates in NJ&quot; or &quot;Pennsylvania electric rates&quot; usually come down to one question: is the supplier&#39;s price per kWh lower than what my utility charges?</p>
<p>A kWh, or kilowatt-hour, is the unit your bill uses for energy. Our <a href="/guides/what-is-a-kilowatt-hour/">kilowatt-hour explainer</a> covers it in more detail.</p>
</div><h2 id="who-sends-the-bill-and-who-sends-the-power">Who sends the bill, and who sends the power?</h2>
<p>Start with the bill itself. Look for two parts. One is delivery: the poles, wires, meter reading and outage repair. The other is supply: the electricity itself. Where a state allows retail choice, you can buy the supply part from a competitive supplier. The delivery part stays with the utility either way.</p>
<p>That split is the whole story. A supplier cannot change how fast the utility restores power after a storm. It can only change who sells you the energy and at what price.</p>
<h2 id="what-the-state-averages-tell-you">What the state averages tell you</h2>
<p>Before you compare offers, you need a baseline. The U.S. Energy Information Administration (EIA) publishes average residential prices by state each month. These are averages across all customers, whether or not they chose a supplier. Recent months are preliminary.</p>

37:T1049,<h2 id="how-to-compare-a-supplier-offer-fairly">How to compare a supplier offer fairly</h2>
<p>Suppliers advertise a price per kWh. That number alone can mislead. Work through these checks.</p>
<h3>Match like with like</h3>
<p>Your utility&#39;s &quot;price to compare&quot; is usually printed on your bill or posted on its website. It is the supply rate you would leave behind. Compare the supplier&#39;s rate to that figure, not to the state average. The state average includes delivery charges and other costs, so it is a rough guide, not the right comparison.</p>
<h3>Read the contract terms</h3>
<ul>
<li>Is the rate fixed, or does it change month to month?</li>
<li>Is there an introductory rate that jumps later?</li>
<li>Is there a monthly fee or a charge for leaving early?</li>
<li>Does the contract renew on its own?</li>
</ul>
<p>A low advertised rate with a fee or a variable switch can cost more than the plain utility rate. Ask for the terms in writing.</p>
<h3>Look at your own usage</h3>
<p>Your total bill depends on how many kWh you use, not just the rate. If you use more power in summer, a rate that looks good in spring may not stay good. Our <a href="/electricity-cost-calculator/">electricity cost calculator</a> helps you turn a rate into an annual cost.</p>
<h2 id="why-the-lowest-rate-may-not-be-the-cheapest-bill">Why the lowest rate may not be the cheapest bill</h2>
<p>Price per kWh is one input. Some plans charge different rates at different hours, which matters if you run most appliances in the evening. Our guide to <a href="/guides/time-of-use-electricity-rates-explained/">time-of-use rates</a> explains how those work. If your plan has a fixed monthly fee, it can outweigh a small rate advantage when your usage is low.</p>
<p>Prices also move. The same EIA data shows states where prices rose over the past year and states where they fell. A fixed contract can protect you if prices climb, but it can also lock you in above the market if they drop. Nobody can promise which way it will go. Our <a href="/guides/electricity-price-increases-by-state/">guide on price increases by state</a> shows the recent direction.</p>
<h2 id="where-solar-fits-in">Where solar fits in</h2>
<p>Choosing a supplier changes what you pay for each kWh you still buy. Rooftop solar reduces how many kWh you buy in the first place. The two are separate decisions, and rules differ by utility.</p>
<p>Solar rules matter here. How your utility credits extra power you send to the grid depends on your net metering or net billing policy, and those policies vary by state and utility. The DSIRE database tracks current programs. Our guide on <a href="/guides/net-metering-vs-net-billing-payback/">net metering versus net billing</a> explains the difference.</p>
<p>In our example home (7 kW system, $24,500 cost, 10,500 kWh a year of use, no incentives), a higher electricity price shortens the payback and a lower one lengthens it. That is why the price you actually pay matters. See our <a href="/methodology/">methodology</a> for the rest of the assumptions, or try the <a href="/solar-calculator/">solar calculator</a> with your own numbers.</p>
<h2 id="a-short-checklist-before-you-switch">A short checklist before you switch</h2>
<ol>
<li>Find your utility&#39;s price to compare on your latest bill.</li>
<li>Confirm on your state utility commission&#39;s website that retail choice is offered where you live.</li>
<li>Compare fixed versus variable rates, fees and contract length.</li>
<li>Make sure the offer is for the supply portion only, and your utility still delivers.</li>
<li>Set a reminder for the contract end date.</li>
</ol>
<h2 id="bottom-line">Bottom line</h2>
<p>Retail choice lets you shop for the electricity itself, not for the wires. Whether it is available depends on your state. If it is, compare the supplier&#39;s rate to your utility&#39;s price to compare, read the fees and terms, and think about your own usage. State averages like those from EIA give useful context, but your bill is what counts. For more ways to cut costs, see <a href="/guides/how-to-lower-your-electric-bill/">how to lower your electric bill</a>.</p>
