38:T933,<div class="short-answer"><h2 id="plain-english-answer">Plain-English answer</h2>
<p>Maybe. Some states let you choose the company that supplies your electricity. Your local utility still delivers it, reads the meter and fixes the lines. Other states do not allow this at all, so the utility handles both jobs.</p>
<p>To find out where you stand, check your state utility commission&#39;s website. If shopping is allowed, one comparison settles most offers: is the supplier&#39;s price per <a href="/guides/what-is-a-kilowatt-hour/">kilowatt-hour</a> lower than what your utility charges for the same energy?</p>
</div><h2 id="how-it-works">How it works</h2>
<p>Your electric bill has two jobs bundled into one page.</p>
<p><strong>Delivery</strong> covers the physical system: poles, wires, meter reading, and crews who restore power after an outage. Only your local utility does this. You cannot shop for it.</p>
<p><strong>Supply</strong> is the electricity itself, the energy that flows through those wires. In states with retail choice (the legal right to buy supply from a company other than your utility), this is the part you can buy elsewhere.</p>
<p>When you sign with a competitive supplier, the utility keeps delivering. Same wires, same repair crews, same outage line. What changes is who sets the supply price and the contract terms that come with it.</p>
<p>That is also the limit of what a supplier can do. It cannot make the lights come back on faster after a storm. It can only change what you pay for the energy.</p>
<h2 id="an-everyday-way-to-picture-it">An everyday way to picture it</h2>
<p>Think of a toll road and the cars on it. The utility owns the road. It paves it, plows it and fixes the potholes. In a choice state, you can pick which company sells you the car, but every car still drives on the same road, and everyone pays the road fee.</p>
<p>So when a supplier offers a &quot;cheaper car,&quot; you are only comparing car prices. The road fee is still there either way. That is why a supplier&#39;s rate must be compared with the utility&#39;s supply rate, not with your whole bill.</p>
<p>A second picture helps with contracts. A fixed-rate plan is like locking in a price for heating oil before winter. If prices climb, you are glad you locked. If they fall, you are stuck paying the old price until the term ends.</p>
3a:T18b1,<h2 id="where-people-get-confused">Where people get confused</h2>
<h3>Comparing the offer to the wrong number</h3>
<p>The most common mistake is putting a supplier&#39;s rate next to the state average or next to the total on your bill. Both include delivery charges and other costs. The right figure is your utility&#39;s &quot;price to compare,&quot; which is usually printed on your bill or posted on the utility&#39;s website. It is the supply rate you would be leaving behind.</p>
<p>State averages are still useful as context. The U.S. Energy Information Administration (EIA) publishes average residential prices by state each month. These cover all customers, whether or not they chose a supplier, and the latest months are preliminary. Four states from EIA&#39;s August 2026 figures show how wide the range is:</p>

<h3>Trusting the headline rate</h3>
<p>An advertised rate can hide terms that raise the real cost. Before signing, get the answers in writing:</p>
<ul>
<li>Is the rate fixed, or does it change month to month?</li>
<li>Is there an introductory rate that jumps later?</li>
<li>Is there a monthly fee, or a charge for leaving early?</li>
<li>Does the contract renew on its own?</li>
</ul>
<p>A low rate paired with a monthly fee or a switch to a variable price can end up costing more than staying with the utility.</p>
<h3>Forgetting about your own usage</h3>
<p>Your bill is the rate times the kWh you use, plus any fixed charges. A rate that looks good in spring may look worse in a summer when you run air conditioning. A fixed monthly fee can wipe out a small rate advantage if you use little power. Our <a href="/electricity-cost-calculator/">electricity cost calculator</a> turns a rate into a yearly cost so you can compare with real numbers.</p>
<p>Some plans also charge different prices at different hours. If most of your use happens in the evening, that matters. Our guide to <a href="/guides/time-of-use-electricity-rates-explained/">time-of-use rates</a> explains how those plans work.</p>
<h3>Assuming prices only go one way</h3>
<p>The same EIA data shows some states where prices rose over the past year and others where they fell. A fixed contract protects you if prices climb. It can also lock you above the market if they drop. No one can promise the direction. The <a href="/guides/electricity-prices-2026-08/">latest monthly price report</a> shows the recent trend.</p>
<h3>Mixing up supplier choice and solar</h3>
<p>Choosing a supplier changes the price of each kWh you still buy from the grid. Rooftop solar reduces how many kWh you buy at all. They are separate decisions.</p>
<p>If you have or want solar, the rule that matters is how your utility credits power you send back to the grid. That depends on net metering or net billing policy, which varies by state and utility. The DSIRE database tracks current programs, and our guide on <a href="/guides/net-metering-vs-net-billing-payback/">net metering versus net billing</a> explains the difference. In our example home, described on the <a href="/methodology/">methodology</a> page, a higher electricity price shortens solar payback and a lower one stretches it. You can test your own price in the <a href="/solar-calculator/">solar calculator</a>.</p>
<h2 id="before-you-switch">Before you switch</h2>
<ol>
<li>Find the price to compare on your latest bill.</li>
<li>Confirm on your state utility commission&#39;s website that retail choice is offered where you live.</li>
<li>Compare fixed versus variable rates, fees and contract length.</li>
<li>Make sure the offer covers the supply portion only, and your utility still delivers.</li>
<li>Put the contract end date in your calendar so it does not renew without you noticing.</li>
</ol>
<h2 id="glossary">Glossary</h2>
<ul>
<li><strong>Retail choice:</strong> a state rule that lets households buy electricity supply from a company other than the local utility.</li>
<li><strong>Supplier:</strong> a competitive company that sells the energy portion of your service.</li>
<li><strong>Utility:</strong> the company that owns the wires and delivers power to your home.</li>
<li><strong>Delivery charge:</strong> the part of your bill that pays for poles, wires, meters and repairs.</li>
<li><strong>Supply charge:</strong> the part of your bill that pays for the electricity itself.</li>
<li><strong>Price to compare:</strong> your utility&#39;s supply rate, the number a supplier offer should be measured against.</li>
<li><strong>Fixed rate:</strong> a price per kWh that stays the same for the contract term.</li>
<li><strong>Variable rate:</strong> a price per kWh that can change, often monthly.</li>
<li><strong>kWh:</strong> short for <a href="/guides/what-is-a-kilowatt-hour/">kilowatt-hour</a>.</li>
</ul>
<h2 id="the-short-version">The short version</h2>
<p>Retail choice lets you shop for the electricity, not for the wires. Whether it exists where you live depends on your state. If it does, measure any offer against your utility&#39;s price to compare, read the fees and renewal terms, and think about how much power you use and when. EIA state averages give helpful context, but your own bill is what counts. For other ways to cut costs, see <a href="/guides/how-to-lower-your-electric-bill/">how to lower your electric bill</a>.</p>
