Can You Choose Your Electricity Supplier? States With Retail Choice

Some states let you pick your electricity supplier. Learn how retail choice works and how to compare a supplier's price per kWh with the EIA state average.

Published November 6, 2026·Updated November 28, 2026·7 min read·RoofPayback editorial·Figures checked against EIA and PVWatts data

Official data: EIA · DOE · IRS · how we check

In this article · 4 sections

Plain-English answer

It depends on your state. In at least 13 states and D.C., including Pennsylvania, Ohio, Illinois, New York and New Jersey, you can buy the electricity itself from a competing supplier, while your local utility still delivers it, reads the meter and fixes the lines. Other states do not allow this at all, so the utility handles both jobs.

To find out where you stand, check your state utility commission's website. If shopping is allowed, one comparison settles most offers: is the supplier's price per kilowatt-hour lower than what your utility charges for the same energy?

How it works

Your electric bill has two jobs bundled into one page.

Delivery covers the physical system: poles, wires, meter reading, and crews who restore power after an outage. Only your local utility does this. You cannot shop for it.

Supply is the electricity itself, the energy that flows through those wires. In states with retail choice (the legal right to buy supply from a company other than your utility), this is the part you can buy elsewhere.

When you sign with a competitive supplier, the utility keeps delivering, and outages are still its job. What changes is who sets the supply price and the contract terms that come with it. That is why a supplier's rate must be compared with the utility's supply rate, not with your whole bill.

Which states let homes choose a supplier

Official sources, last checked on November 28, 2026. That day we opened the pages for 13 rows: Connecticut, Delaware, D.C., Illinois, Maine, Maryland, Massachusetts, New Jersey, New York, Ohio, Pennsylvania, California and Oregon. The pages for New Hampshire, Rhode Island, Michigan, Texas and Virginia blocked automated access or did not respond, so we could not re-confirm those five rows that day; check them on the commission's site. In the 13 states and D.C. marked "Yes", homes can buy their electricity supply from a licensed supplier instead of the utility. A few others allow it only in part. States not listed may not allow residential choice at all, or we could not confirm it from an official page: your state utility commission's website settles it.

Even in a "Yes" state, the utility still delivers the power, and some customers, such as those of municipal utilities, may be outside the program.

Where people get confused

Comparing the offer to the wrong number

The most common mistake is putting a supplier's rate next to the state average or next to the total on your bill. Both include delivery charges and other costs. The right figure is your utility's "price to compare," which is usually printed on your bill or posted on the utility's website. It is the supply rate you would be leaving behind. Some competitive markets have no utility supply rate at all, because every customer must pick a retail provider; there, compare offers with each other at your own monthly kWh, including fixed fees.

State averages are still useful as context. The U.S. Energy Information Administration (EIA) publishes average residential prices by state each month. These cover all customers, whether or not they chose a supplier, and the latest months are preliminary. Four states from EIA's September 2026 figures show how wide the range is:

State Average residential price A year earlier Change
New Jersey 24.64¢ per kWh 23.32¢ 5.7%
Pennsylvania 22.2¢ per kWh 20.43¢ 8.7%
Texas 15.91¢ per kWh 15.77¢ 0.9%
Massachusetts 30.97¢ per kWh 30.31¢ 2.2%

Having choice does not make a state cheap, either. Over the twelve months to September 2026, none of the 13 states and D.C. marked "Yes" above had an average residential price below the U.S. average of 18.05¢ (EIA-861M). Delaware matched it, Ohio (18.54¢) and Illinois (18.93¢) came closest, and Massachusetts (30.44¢) and Maine (29.83¢) were highest. Most of these states are in the Northeast and Mid-Atlantic, the most expensive part of the mainland, so the comparison cannot tell you whether prices would be higher or lower without choice. It does tell you not to expect a supplier to bring a Northeastern bill down to Southern levels. Each state has its own page: New Jersey, Pennsylvania, Texas and Massachusetts. The full list is on our electricity prices by state page.

Trusting the headline rate

An advertised rate can hide terms that raise the real cost. Before signing, find these answers in the contract itself:

  • Is the rate fixed, or does it change month to month?
  • Is there an introductory rate that jumps later?
  • Is there a monthly fee, or a charge for leaving early?
  • Does the contract renew on its own?

A low rate paired with a monthly fee or a switch to a variable price can end up costing more than staying with the utility.

Forgetting about your own usage

Your bill is the rate times the kWh you use, plus any fixed charges. A rate that looks good in spring may look worse in a summer when you run air conditioning. A fixed monthly fee can wipe out a small rate advantage if you use little power. Our electricity cost calculator turns a rate into a yearly cost so you can compare with real numbers.

Some plans also charge different prices at different hours. If most of your use happens in the evening, that matters. Our guide to time-of-use rates explains how those plans work.

Assuming prices only go one way

The same EIA data shows some states where prices rose over the past year and others where they fell. A fixed contract protects you if prices climb. It can also lock you above the market if they drop. No one can promise the direction. The latest monthly price report shows the recent trend.

Mixing up supplier choice and solar

Choosing a supplier changes the price of each kWh you still buy from the grid. Rooftop solar reduces how many kWh you buy at all. They are separate decisions.

If you have or want solar, the rule that matters is how your utility credits power you send back to the grid. That depends on net metering or net billing policy, which varies by state and utility. The DSIRE database tracks current programs, and our guide on net metering versus net billing explains the difference. In our example home, described on the methodology page, a higher electricity price shortens solar payback and a lower one stretches it. You can test your own price in the solar calculator.

Before you switch

  1. Find the price to compare on your latest bill, or, where there is no utility supply rate, list two or three offers at your usual monthly kWh.
  2. Confirm on your state utility commission's website that retail choice is offered where you live.
  3. Compare fixed versus variable rates, fees and contract length.
  4. Make sure the offer covers the supply portion only, and your utility still delivers.
  5. Put the contract end date in your calendar so it does not renew without you noticing.
