33:T2e1c,<div class="short-answer"><p class="short-answer-label">What we found</p>
<p>We ran the same house through every state and the District of Columbia. Payback, the years until bill savings repay the system, runs from 4.7 years in Hawaii to 19.2 years in Washington when every exported kWh earns the retail price.</p>
<p>The strongest pattern in the ranking is price. Across the 51 results, the rank of a state&#39;s electricity price and the rank of its payback line up almost perfectly (a rank correlation of −0.91). The rank of its sunshine barely relates to payback at all (+0.09). Fargo and Providence make the point: their reference cities get exactly the same 1,294 kWh a year from each kW of panels, yet Providence pays back in 8.7 years and Fargo in 17.2, because power costs 29.93¢ in Rhode Island and 14.29¢ in North Dakota.</p>
<p>Five states need an asterisk. In California, Hawaii and Louisiana we confirmed in official sources that new customers&#39; exports are credited below the retail price. In Kentucky the credit is a rate the regulator sets for each utility, and in Texas the buyback rate depends on the retail plan. Their working cases are in the last column. In the other 46 places we have not verified the export rule, so the full-credit figure is an upper bound to confirm with your utility.</p>

<p>The full-credit column is an upper limit everywhere: it assumes the most generous crediting a utility can offer. We filled the last column for the five states where an official source shows that new customers&#39; exports are not simply credited at retail, using the same working assumptions as the rest of the site:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>California:</strong> the CPUC&#39;s Net Billing Tariff has applied to new interconnection applications since April 15, 2023. The 12.1 years uses exports worth a quarter of retail, our working assumption; at half of retail, a sensitivity case, it is 9.4 years. See the <a href="/guides/is-solar-worth-it-in-california/">California guide</a>.</li>
<li><strong>Hawaii:</strong> Hawaiian Electric&#39;s daytime export rate on Oahu is 13.5¢, about 28% of the state&#39;s average price.</li>
<li><strong>Louisiana:</strong> the Public Service Commission credits exports from systems installed after 2019 &quot;at Avoided Cost.&quot; Its 2026 table lists rates of 3.58¢ to 3.99¢ per kWh by utility; our working assumption, a quarter of retail (3.33¢), is close to and slightly below those rates. Ask your utility whether the commission&#39;s rules cover your address.</li>
<li><strong>Kentucky:</strong> under the net metering law in effect since 2020, customers receive &quot;dollar credits at a compensation rate set by the PSC in rate proceedings for each utility.&quot; We did not find one statewide figure, so we use a quarter of retail as a labeled assumption; your utility&#39;s current rate is on file with the PSC.</li>
<li><strong>Texas:</strong> there is no statewide net metering, and the buyback rate depends on your retail plan. The working case uses a low buyback, a quarter of retail; the <a href="/guides/is-solar-worth-it-in-texas/">Texas guide</a> shows payback at every rate from zero to retail.</li>
</ul>
<p>With those cases, Hawaii stays first and California drops to 15th, just behind Colorado, while Texas falls to 49th and Kentucky and Louisiana miss 25 years.</p>
<h2 id="1-the-sunniest-states-land-in-the-middle">1. The sunniest states land in the middle</h2>
<p>New Mexico (1,779 kWh per kW), Arizona (1,755) and Nevada (1,753) make the most power per panel. They rank 16th, 25th and 39th. Arizona and Nevada are the cleanest comparison: practically the same output, but power costs 14.92¢ in Arizona and 13.25¢ in Nevada, and that gap alone puts Nevada a year and a half behind.</p>
<p>Alaska shows the reverse. Anchorage&#39;s 922 kWh per kW is the weakest output of any state, yet at 28¢ per kWh the example pays back in 12.7 years, ahead of Arizona. The U.S. Department of Energy makes the same point in its homeowner guide: &quot;Your savings also depend on the electricity rates set by your utility and how much the utility will compensate you for the excess solar energy you send back to the grid.&quot;</p>

40:Tcbf,<h2 id="2-the-top-of-the-table-is-the-northeast">2. The top of the table is the Northeast</h2>
<p>Seven of the ten fastest paybacks are in New England or the Middle Atlantic: Maine, Massachusetts, Rhode Island, New Hampshire, New York, Connecticut and New Jersey. Their reference cities make only 1,252 to 1,319 kWh per kW, but all seven charge between 24.64¢ and 31.29¢. The other three in the top ten are Hawaii and California, which pair high prices with strong sun, and D.C.</p>
<h2 id="3-the-bottom-of-the-table-mixes-two-problems">3. The bottom of the table mixes two problems</h2>
<p>The ten slowest states split into two groups. Utah, Louisiana, Nebraska, Kentucky and Tennessee get ordinary sunshine but charge under 14¢ per kWh. Oregon and Washington combine below-average prices with the two weakest outputs in the lower 48. Mississippi, West Virginia and North Dakota sit in between. All ten charge less than the U.S. average of 18.17¢. <a href="/guides/is-solar-worth-it-where-electricity-is-cheap/">Solar where electricity is cheap</a> and <a href="/guides/cloudy-states-solar-worth-it/">cloudy states</a> look at those groups.</p>
<h2 id="4-a-year-of-price-changes-moves-states-not-the-order">4. A year of price changes moves states, not the order</h2>
<p>Some prices moved sharply between September 2025 and September 2026: Hawaii rose 22.2%, Idaho 15.1%, Maine 12.2% and South Dakota 11.1%, while Connecticut fell 10.9% and Florida 6.5%. Connecticut is still ninth after its drop. Idaho&#39;s rise left it at 14.39¢ and 38th. Single-year moves shift a state a few places; they rarely move it between the top and bottom halves. <a href="/guides/solar-payback-if-electricity-prices-rise/">Solar payback if electricity prices rise</a> tests what steady growth does over 25 years.</p>
<h2 id="5-the-export-credit-can-reorder-the-list">5. The export credit can reorder the list</h2>
<p>The ranking above holds the export credit at retail, outside the five states in the last column. That is the one assumption most likely to be wrong for your house, and it does not hit every state equally: a weak credit costs the most where power is cheap and where a large share of output is exported. <a href="/guides/net-metering-vs-net-billing-payback/">Net metering vs. net billing</a> shows each state&#39;s payback at half, quarter and zero credit, and the export rate needed for a 15-year payback.</p>
<h2 id="how-we-measured-it">How we measured it</h2>
<p>Output is PVWatts v8 for one reference city per state, south-facing at a 20° pitch with 14% system losses; DOE notes that panels &quot;perform best on south-facing roofs with a slope between 15 and 40 degrees.&quot; Prices are EIA averages for September 2026, which include taxes and fees. The model assumes electricity prices rise 2.5% a year, output falls 0.5% a year and upkeep costs $100 a year, over 25 years. No federal credit is included: the IRS says the Residential Clean Energy Credit &quot;is not available for any property placed in service after December 31, 2025.&quot;</p>
<p>Your own rate, export credit and quote decide where you land. The <a href="/solar-calculator/">solar calculator</a> takes all three, and each <a href="/solar-panels/">state page</a> has local inputs.</p>
