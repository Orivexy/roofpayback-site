Best States for Solar Payback: Price, Sun and Export Credits

Solar pays back in 4.8 years in Hawaii and 19.6 in Washington. See the fastest and slowest states, and why price beats sunshine with export credits next.

What we found

Fargo, North Dakota and Providence, Rhode Island get exactly the same sunshine in the model: 1,294 kWh a year from each kilowatt of panels. Providence earns back the cost of a home solar system in 9.2 years. Fargo needs 18.3. The only input that differs is the price of electricity.

That pattern runs through the whole ranking. Hawaii is fastest at 4.8 years, followed by California (7.4 years) and Maine (8 years). Washington is slowest at 19.6 years, behind North Dakota and then Oregon and Tennessee (both 17.6 years).

Payback is the number of years it takes for bill savings to add up to what the system cost. Every state uses the same 7 kW example home, described on the methodology page, so the differences come from location alone. Your own roof and rate can go into the solar calculator.

1. The price of electricity predicts payback better than sunshine does

Each kilowatt-hour the panels make is one the household does not buy. At 32.11¢, Maine's price, that kWh is worth two and a half times what it is worth in Louisiana at 12.87¢. Same panel, same roof, very different savings.

The table puts the seven fastest states next to the six slowest. "Output per kW" is the yearly production of one kilowatt of panels in the state's reference city. Paybacks assume full export credit.

Louisiana stands out in the slow group. Its panels outproduce those in Boston, New York City and Providence, yet the low price leaves it at 16.7 years. Year-1 savings tell the same story in dollars: $4,954 in Hawaii against $1,114 in Washington.

2. The three sunniest states land in the middle of the pack

New Mexico (1,779 kWh per kW), Arizona (1,755) and Nevada (1,753) lead the country on output. Their paybacks are 12.8, 13.9 and 16.1 years. None of them comes close to the top seven.

Arizona and Nevada are the cleanest test, since their output is practically identical. Power costs 15.05¢ in Arizona and 12.72¢ in Nevada, and that gap alone puts Nevada more than two years behind.

The U.S. Department of Energy (DOE) makes the same point in its homeowner guide: "Your savings also depend on the electricity rates set by your utility and how much the utility will compensate you for the excess solar energy you send back to the grid." For low-sun states with high prices, the cloudy states guide runs the numbers, and the Alaska and Hawaii comparison covers the two outliers.

3. Export credits come second, and low credits can override a good price

Around midday, panels often make more than the house is using, and the surplus flows to the grid. DOE defines the classic arrangement: "Net metering is an arrangement between solar energy system owners and utilities in which the system owners are compensated for any solar power generation that is exported to the electricity grid." Many utilities now credit exports at less than the retail price.

The example home uses about 40% of its power while the sun is up, so a large share of output is exported. When exports earn half or a quarter of retail, payback stretches:

  • Hawaii: 4.8 years at full credit, 6.5 at half, 8.3 at a quarter.
  • Arizona: 13.9 years at full credit, 17.7 at half, 22.3 at a quarter.
  • Washington: 19.6 years at full credit, 24.6 at half, and no payback within 25 years at a quarter.

Arizona shows how the credit rule can overturn the price ranking. Its price is higher than Louisiana's, yet Arizona at quarter credit (22.3 years) is far slower than Louisiana at full credit (16.7 years). DOE notes that whether a system "qualifies for net metering payments depends on policies and practices in your state and electric utility." The net metering vs. net billing guide compares the two approaches state by state.

4. One year of price change matters less than the price level

The U.S. average residential price was 18.16¢ in August 2026, up from 17.57¢ a year earlier. Some states moved sharply. Hawaii rose 22.5%, Maine 14.1%, New Hampshire 13.1%, the District of Columbia 12.1% and Virginia 11.3%. Connecticut fell 18.8% from 30.17¢, and California slipped 3.8%.

Those swings shift the ranking less than they seem to. Connecticut, even after its drop, still charges 24.51¢ and pays back in 10.8 years, well ahead of any desert state. Virginia's jump leaves it at 17.64¢ and 13 years. A single year is a snapshot, and prices can reverse. The August 2026 price report tracks the monthly figures, and solar payback if electricity prices rise tests what steady growth does over 25 years.

5. A slow ranking does not rule solar out

States at the bottom are not "bad" for solar. The example system simply takes longer to earn back its cost. Washington and Oregon pair modest output with prices below the national average, and a lower installed price, a local incentive or a different rate plan could shorten the wait. The guide to solar where electricity is cheap looks at that group in detail.

Roof shape also matters. DOE says: "Typically, solar panels perform best on south-facing roofs with a slope between 15 and 40 degrees, though other roofs may be suitable too."

How we measured it

Output comes from PVWatts v8 for one reference city per state, with a south-facing roof at a 20° pitch and 14% system losses; the methodology page has the details. Prices are the EIA average residential price for each state in August 2026.

The system costs $3,500 per kW, or $24,500, with no incentives. Federal help is gone for new installations: the IRS says the Residential Clean Energy Credit "is not available for any property placed in service after December 31, 2025." State and utility programs are listed in the DSIRE database, and the no-tax-credit guide covers the change.

What this means for your bill

Your state's rank is a first filter. Three figures from your own household decide whether you match it:

  • The rate on your bill. Prices differ by utility and plan, not just by state. Look yours up on the electricity prices page.
  • The export credit. Full, half or a quarter of retail can add more years than a cloudy climate does.
  • The quote. Every figure above assumes $3,500 per kW; a higher price lengthens each one.

If your rate sits near the top of the table and your utility still credits exports at retail, you are in the fast group whatever the weather. If your rate is low and exports earn a fraction of it, the solar calculator will show a long wait, and your sunshine will not change that.
