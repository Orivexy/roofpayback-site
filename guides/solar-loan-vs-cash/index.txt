33:Tbbd,<div class="short-answer"><p class="short-answer-label">Quick verdict</p>
<p>Cash costs less, usually by a wide margin. Berkeley Lab found that host-owned home systems installed in 2025 had a median price of $3.00 per watt when bought with cash and $4.50 when financed with a loan, and those prices do not include interest. On a 7 kW system, that is $21,000 against $31,500 before the first loan payment.</p>
<p>A loan can still work where power is expensive. In our Massachusetts example, a loan at the loan-median price still leaves the household between about $15,000 and $46,000 ahead after 25 years, depending on the rate and term. In a low-price state such as Tennessee, none of the loan cases we ran comes out ahead within 25 years.</p>

<p>&quot;Monthly shortfall&quot; is the loan payment minus the first year&#39;s bill savings after $100 of upkeep: what you pay out of pocket each month in year one.</p>
<p>Three patterns:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>The loan price premium is the bigger cost in short loans.</strong> Over 10 years at 6%, interest is $10,466, about the same as the $10,500 gap between the cash and loan medians.</li>
<li><strong>Long terms only work where savings are large.</strong> In Massachusetts, a 20-year loan at 6% roughly matches the payment from the first year. In Illinois the same loan ends slightly behind after 25 years.</li>
<li><strong>In low-price states, financing at the loan-median price does not pay in our model.</strong> Tennessee&#39;s cash case already takes 15.6 years.</li>
</ul>
<p>If your installer quotes the same price for cash and loan, the premium disappears and only interest remains, which changes these results a lot. That is the first question to ask.</p>
<h2 id="when-a-loan-makes-sense">When a loan makes sense</h2>
<ul>
<li>You do not have the cash, or you would have to drain your emergency savings to pay it.</li>
<li>You live where power is expensive, and the payment is close to or below your expected monthly savings.</li>
<li>The loan price matches the cash price, or close to it.</li>
<li>You have read the full terms: APR, fees, term, and whether you can repay early without a penalty.</li>
</ul>
<h2 id="when-cash-makes-sense">When cash makes sense</h2>
<ul>
<li>You have the money and no better use for it.</li>
<li>The installer offers a lower price for cash.</li>
<li>Your state&#39;s payback is already long. Every extra dollar of cost stretches it further; <a href="/guides/solar-panel-cost-by-state/">solar panel cost per watt</a> shows the most you can pay in your state for a 10- or 15-year payback.</li>
</ul>
<h2 id="where-they-tie">Where they tie</h2>
<ul>
<li><strong>Export credit.</strong> A lower credit for exported power cuts savings under both options; a loan just has less room to absorb it. <a href="/guides/net-metering-vs-net-billing-payback/">Net metering vs. net billing</a> shows by how much.</li>
<li><strong>Seasons.</strong> Savings are smaller in winter, but loan payments are not. Check the monthly picture, not just the yearly one.</li>
<li><strong>No federal credit.</strong> The IRS says the Residential Clean Energy Credit &quot;is not available for any property placed in service after December 31, 2025,&quot; for cash and loan buyers alike.</li>
</ul>
<h2 id="questions-for-the-installer-and-lender">Questions for the installer and lender</h2>
<ul>
<li>What is the cash price, and what is the loan price, for the same system?</li>
<li>What is the APR, the term and the total amount I will repay?</li>
<li>Are any lender fees included in the system price?</li>
<li>Can I repay early without a penalty?</li>
<li>What monthly savings do you expect in my first year, and which electricity price and export credit sit behind that figure?</li>
</ul>
<p>Then compare both options with your own numbers in the <a href="/solar-calculator/">solar calculator</a>. This is general information, not financial advice.</p>
