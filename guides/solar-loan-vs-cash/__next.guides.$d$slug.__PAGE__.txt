22:T9a3,<div class="short-answer"><h2 id="quick-verdict">Quick verdict</h2>
<p>Cash usually wins on total cost for rooftop solar. In Berkeley Lab data, loan-financed systems installed in 2024 had a higher median installed price than cash-purchased ones, and a loan adds interest on top of that. A loan can still be the sensible route when you lack the cash and the monthly savings cover the payment. The higher your electricity rate, the easier that test is to pass; you can try your own loan terms in the <a href="/solar-calculator/">solar calculator</a>.</p>

<p>For context, Berkeley Lab puts the overall median at $4.00 per watt for host-owned residential systems installed in 2024, in 2024 dollars and before incentives. The 20th to 80th percentile range is $3.00 to $5.20 per watt. That data shows a gap by payment method. It does not reveal the fee on any single quote.</p>
<h2 id="why-the-loan-price-starts-higher">Why the loan price starts higher</h2>
<p>A lender often charges the installer a fee for offering its loan, sometimes called a <strong>dealer fee</strong>. Installers may fold that fee into the system price. That may be part of why the loan median sits above the cash median, but ask your installer whether the loan price differs from the cash price.</p>
<p>Interest then comes on top. <strong>Interest</strong> is what you pay the lender for borrowing. So financing can cost you twice: once in a higher installed price, and again over the loan term.</p>
<p>This article does not assume any interest rate. Rates, lengths and fees vary by lender and by borrower. Ask for the full terms in writing, including the <strong>APR</strong> (the yearly cost of the loan with fees included), any origination fees, and what happens if you pay the loan off early.</p>
24:T1780,<h2 id="when-cash-wins">When cash wins</h2>
<ul>
<li><strong>You have the money and don&#39;t need it elsewhere.</strong> You avoid both the higher loan price and the interest.</li>
<li><strong>Your state has a long payback.</strong> Every added dollar of cost stretches an already slow return. In our example, Houston needs 14.3 years even when paid in cash.</li>
<li><strong>Your quote shows a cash discount.</strong> If the installer prices the same system lower for cash, that gap goes straight into your pocket.</li>
<li><strong>You want the shortest possible payback.</strong> Price is a big lever, as the table below shows.</li>
</ul>

<p><strong>Payback</strong> is the number of years of savings it takes to earn back the system cost. A higher per-watt loan price pushes you toward the right-hand column. Interest moves your real cost further right still. <a href="/guides/solar-panel-cost-by-state/">Solar panel cost per watt and payback</a> shows the same effect across more states, and our <a href="/guides/solar-payback-period-explained/">solar payback period guide</a> explains the calculation.</p>
<h2 id="when-a-loan-wins">When a loan wins</h2>
<ul>
<li><strong>You don&#39;t have the cash.</strong> Without a loan, there may be no system at all.</li>
<li><strong>Monthly savings comfortably cover the payment.</strong> Then the loan can pay for itself as you go.</li>
<li><strong>Your power is expensive.</strong> Every <a href="/guides/what-is-a-kilowatt-hour/">kilowatt-hour</a> the panels make replaces costlier grid power, which makes a payment easier to cover.</li>
</ul>

To see how your local price compares, look at <a href="/electricity-prices/">electricity prices by state</a> or a state page such as <a href="/electricity-prices/texas/">Texas electricity prices</a>.</p>
<h2 id="where-they-tie">Where they tie</h2>
<p>Some factors hit both options the same way.</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Export credit rules.</strong> How your utility credits power you send to the grid changes savings whether you borrow or not. See <a href="/guides/net-metering-vs-net-billing-payback/">net metering vs. net billing payback</a>.</li>
<li><strong>Seasons.</strong> Savings that cover a payment on paper in year one still have to cover it in low-production months, like winter. Our guide to <a href="/guides/solar-panels-in-winter-by-state/">solar panels in winter by state</a> shows that pattern. With cash, a weak month just slows payback. With a loan, the payment is still due.</li>
<li><strong>No federal credit.</strong> Cash and loan buyers alike can no longer claim the 30% federal credit (Section 25D) for systems placed in service after December 31, 2025, per the IRS. With nothing to cut the net cost, price and financing carry more weight. Read <a href="/guides/is-solar-worth-it-without-tax-credit/">is solar worth it without the tax credit</a>; state and utility programs are listed in the DSIRE database.</li>
<li><strong>Your usage and rate plan.</strong> Changes in either shift savings under both options.</li>
</ul>
<h2 id="which-one-fits-you">Which one fits you</h2>
<p>Lean toward <strong>cash</strong> if:</p>
<ul>
<li>you can pay without draining your emergency savings,</li>
<li>the cash quote is lower than the loan quote for the same system,</li>
<li>your area&#39;s payback is already long.</li>
</ul>
<p>Lean toward a <strong>loan</strong> if:</p>
<ul>
<li>you can&#39;t or don&#39;t want to pay up front,</li>
<li>your monthly savings clearly exceed the payment, including in weaker months,</li>
<li>you have read the full terms and know the total you will repay.</li>
</ul>
<p>Before choosing, ask the installer and lender:</p>
<ul>
<li>What is the cash price, and what is the loan price for the same system?</li>
<li>What is the APR, and are there upfront or dealer fees?</li>
<li>How long is the term, and is there a penalty for early payoff?</li>
<li>What monthly savings do you expect, and what assumptions sit behind that number?</li>
<li>How does my utility credit solar exports?</li>
</ul>
<p>Try different prices and terms with the loan option in the <a href="/solar-calculator/">solar calculator</a>. Berkeley Lab&#39;s medians, $4.70 per watt for loan-financed versus $3.50 per watt for cash, show why comparing both quotes is worth the effort. This is general information, not financial or tax advice.</p>
