Solar Loan vs. Cash: How Financing Changes Payback

Loan-financed solar had a higher median installed price than cash in Berkeley Lab data. See how interest changes payback and when savings cover payments.

Short answer

Paying cash usually gives the lowest total cost for rooftop solar. A loan can still make sense if the monthly savings cover the payment and you do not have the cash. Berkeley Lab data shows that loan-financed systems in 2024 had a higher median installed price than cash-purchased ones, and interest adds more on top. The result depends on your loan terms, your utility rates, and your quote, so run your own numbers in the solar calculator.

Why loan-financed systems cost more up front

Berkeley Lab reports a median installed price of $4.0 per watt for host-owned residential solar systems installed in 2024. That is in 2024 dollars and before incentives. The 20th to 80th percentile range is $3.0 to $5.2 per watt.

The split by payment method is the important part:

How the system was paid for Median installed price (2024 installs)
Cash purchase $3.5 per watt
Loan-financed $4.7 per watt

Why would a loan raise the sticker price? Lenders often charge the installer a fee to offer a loan, sometimes called a dealer fee. Installers may build that fee into the system price. The data shows the gap. It does not tell you the exact fee on any one quote, so ask your installer whether the loan price differs from the cash price.

How interest adds to the cost

The price gap is only the first layer. A loan also charges interest. Interest is the extra money you pay the lender for borrowing. That means you can pay twice for financing: once in a higher installed price, and again in interest over the loan term.

Interest rates, loan lengths and fees differ by lender and by borrower. This article does not assume a rate. Get the full terms in writing, including the APR (the yearly cost of the loan, with fees included), any origination fees, and what happens if you pay the loan off early.

The example home: what the system might save

To compare payment methods, you first need to know what the system saves. Here is the example home used across RoofPayback:

  • A 7 kW system and 10500 kWh per year of electricity use.
  • 40% of use happens while the sun is up.
  • Installed cost of $3500 per kW ($24500 total), with no incentives.
  • $100 per year in maintenance.
  • Electricity prices rise 2.5% per year, and panels lose 0.5% of output per year.
  • A 25-year horizon, with export credits capped at a year of grid purchases (an annual true-up).
  • Production from PVWatts v8, south-facing at a 20° pitch with 14% losses, using one reference city per state. Prices are the EIA July 2026 residential averages.

Under those assumptions, the first-year savings and cash payback vary a lot by state:

State (city) Year-1 savings, full credit Payback, full credit
California (Los Angeles) $3529 6.6 years
New York (New York City) $2703 8.7 years
Texas (Houston) $1580 14.3 years
Florida (Orlando) $1578 14.1 years
Arizona (Phoenix) $1615 13.6 years

Payback means how many years of savings it takes to earn back the cost of the system. These figures treat the $24500 as paid in cash. They do not include loan interest or dealer fees.

When monthly savings can cover a loan payment

A simple test is to compare your expected savings with the loan payment. Take the year-1 savings for your state, divide by 12, and compare it to a quoted monthly payment.

In the example home, Los Angeles has year-1 savings of $3529 and Houston has $1580. A given payment is much easier to cover in the first case than in the second. Higher electricity prices help, because every kilowatt-hour (kWh, the unit your utility bills you for) the panels produce replaces more expensive grid power. If you want to see how your local price compares, check the electricity prices page or your state page, such as Texas electricity prices.

Two cautions apply. First, savings from solar can change with your usage, your rate plan and your utility's export credit rules. See the guide on net metering vs. net billing payback. Second, a payment that is covered on paper in year one still has to be covered in the months when production is lower, such as winter. The guide on solar panels in winter by state explains that pattern.

Cash price changes the picture too

Installed price matters a great deal. The fact pack shows how payback moves with price per kW for three states:

State $2500/kW ($17500) $3000/kW ($21000) $3500/kW ($24500)
Texas 10.7 years 12.5 years 14.3 years
California 4.9 years 5.8 years 6.6 years
Florida 10.5 years 12.3 years 14.1 years
Arizona 10.2 years 12 years 13.6 years
New York 6.4 years 7.5 years 8.7 years

A loan-financed price that is higher per watt pushes you toward the right-hand columns. Add interest, and your true cost moves further right again. That is why comparing a cash quote and a loan quote for the same system is worth the effort. Our solar payback period guide explains how payback is calculated.

No federal credit to lean on

The 30% federal Residential Clean Energy Credit (Section 25D) is not available for property placed in service after December 31, 2025, according to the IRS. Many buyers used to count on it to lower the net cost. With no credit, the installed price and financing costs matter even more. For more on this, read is solar worth it without the tax credit. State and utility programs differ, and DSIRE keeps a database of current ones. Check what applies to you before deciding.

Questions to ask before choosing a loan

  • What is the cash price, and what is the loan price for the same system?
  • What is the APR, and are there upfront or dealer fees?
  • How long is the term, and is there a penalty for early payoff?
  • What monthly savings does the installer expect, and what assumptions sit behind that number?
  • How does my utility credit solar exports?

Use the loan option in the solar calculator to test different prices and terms. The methodology page lists the assumptions behind our numbers.

Bottom line

Cash is often cheaper because loan-financed systems had a higher median installed price ($4.7 per watt versus $3.5 per watt for cash) in Berkeley Lab's 2024 data, and interest comes on top. A loan can still work if your monthly savings cover the payment and you compare the cash and loan quotes carefully. Your own utility rates, quote and loan terms decide the answer. This article gives general information, not financial or tax advice.
