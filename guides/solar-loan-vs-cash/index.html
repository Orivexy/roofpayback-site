Solar Loan vs. Cash: How Financing Changes Payback

Loan-financed solar had a higher median installed price than cash in Berkeley Lab data. See how interest changes payback and when savings cover payments.

Published October 23, 2026·Updated November 15, 2026·5 min read·RoofPayback editorial·Figures checked against EIA and PVWatts data

Official data: EIA · DOE · IRS · how we check

Quick verdict

Cash usually wins on total cost for rooftop solar. In Berkeley Lab data, loan-financed systems installed in 2024 had a higher median installed price than cash-purchased ones, and a loan adds interest on top of that. A loan can still be the sensible route when you lack the cash and the monthly savings cover the payment. The higher your electricity rate, the easier that test is to pass; you can try your own loan terms in the solar calculator.

Side by side

Paying cash Taking a solar loan
Median installed price, 2024 installs (Berkeley Lab) $3.50 per watt $4.70 per watt
Interest None Yes, set by lender and borrower
Lender fee built into price No lender involved Possibly, as a dealer fee
Money needed up front Full system cost Set by loan terms
What decides if it pays Payback years Savings vs. monthly payment, plus total cost

For context, Berkeley Lab puts the overall median at $4.00 per watt for host-owned residential systems installed in 2024, in 2024 dollars and before incentives. The 20th to 80th percentile range is $3.00 to $5.20 per watt. That data shows a gap by payment method. It does not reveal the fee on any single quote.

Why the loan price starts higher

A lender often charges the installer a fee for offering its loan, sometimes called a dealer fee. Installers may fold that fee into the system price. That may be part of why the loan median sits above the cash median, but ask your installer whether the loan price differs from the cash price.

Interest then comes on top. Interest is what you pay the lender for borrowing. So financing can cost you twice: once in a higher installed price, and again over the loan term.

This article does not assume any interest rate. Rates, lengths and fees vary by lender and by borrower. Ask for the full terms in writing, including the APR (the yearly cost of the loan with fees included), any origination fees, and what happens if you pay the loan off early.

When cash wins

  • You have the money and don't need it elsewhere. You avoid both the higher loan price and the interest.
  • Your state has a long payback. Every added dollar of cost stretches an already slow return. In our example, Houston needs 14.3 years even when paid in cash.
  • Your quote shows a cash discount. If the installer prices the same system lower for cash, that gap goes straight into your pocket.
  • You want the shortest possible payback. Price is a big lever, as the table below shows.

How much installed price moves payback in our example home (a 7 kW system with no incentives; full setup on the methodology page):

State $2,500/kW ($17,500) $3,000/kW ($21,000) $3,500/kW ($24,500)
Texas 10.6 years 12.5 years 14.3 years
California 5.5 years 6.5 years 7.4 years
Florida 10.5 years 12.3 years 14.1 years
Arizona 10.4 years 12.2 years 13.9 years
New York 6.6 years 7.9 years 9 years

Payback is the number of years of savings it takes to earn back the system cost. A higher per-watt loan price pushes you toward the right-hand column. Interest moves your real cost further right still. Solar panel cost per watt and payback shows the same effect across more states, and our solar payback period guide explains the calculation.

When a loan wins

  • You don't have the cash. Without a loan, there may be no system at all.
  • Monthly savings comfortably cover the payment. Then the loan can pay for itself as you go.
  • Your power is expensive. Every kilowatt-hour the panels make replaces costlier grid power, which makes a payment easier to cover.

A quick test helps. Take your expected year-1 savings, divide by 12, and set that against a quoted monthly payment. In the example home, with full export credit and the system treated as paid in cash:

State (city) Year-1 savings, full credit Payback, full credit
California (Los Angeles) $3,124 7.4 years
New York (New York City) $2,590 9 years
Texas (Houston) $1,583 14.3 years
Florida (Orlando) $1,574 14.1 years
Arizona (Phoenix) $1,580 13.9 years

A given payment is far easier to cover with Los Angeles savings of $3,124 than with Houston savings of $1,583. These figures leave out loan interest and dealer fees. To see how your local price compares, look at electricity prices by state or a state page such as Texas electricity prices.

Where they tie

Some factors hit both options the same way.

  • Export credit rules. How your utility credits power you send to the grid changes savings whether you borrow or not. See net metering vs. net billing payback.
  • Seasons. Savings that cover a payment on paper in year one still have to cover it in low-production months, like winter. Our guide to solar panels in winter by state shows that pattern. With cash, a weak month just slows payback. With a loan, the payment is still due.
  • No federal credit. Cash and loan buyers alike can no longer claim the 30% federal credit (Section 25D) for systems placed in service after December 31, 2025, per the IRS. With nothing to cut the net cost, price and financing carry more weight. Read is solar worth it without the tax credit; state and utility programs are listed in the DSIRE database.
  • Your usage and rate plan. Changes in either shift savings under both options.

Which one fits you

Lean toward cash if:

  • you can pay without draining your emergency savings,
  • the cash quote is lower than the loan quote for the same system,
  • your area's payback is already long.

Lean toward a loan if:

  • you can't or don't want to pay up front,
  • your monthly savings clearly exceed the payment, including in weaker months,
  • you have read the full terms and know the total you will repay.

Before choosing, ask the installer and lender:

  • What is the cash price, and what is the loan price for the same system?
  • What is the APR, and are there upfront or dealer fees?
  • How long is the term, and is there a penalty for early payoff?
  • What monthly savings do you expect, and what assumptions sit behind that number?
  • How does my utility credit solar exports?

Try different prices and terms with the loan option in the solar calculator. Berkeley Lab's medians, $4.70 per watt for loan-financed versus $3.50 per watt for cash, show why comparing both quotes is worth the effort. This is general information, not financial or tax advice.
