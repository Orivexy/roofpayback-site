Solar Loan vs. Cash: How Financing Changes Payback

Loan-financed solar cost $4.50 a watt against $3.00 for cash in 2025. See monthly payments, total interest and 25-year results in three states.

Published October 23, 2026·Updated November 28, 2026·5 min read·RoofPayback editorial·Figures checked against EIA and PVWatts data

Official data: EIA · DOE · IRS · how we check

Quick verdict

Cash costs less, usually by a wide margin. Berkeley Lab found that host-owned home systems installed in 2025 had a median price of $3.00 per watt when bought with cash and $4.50 when financed with a loan, and those prices do not include interest. On a 7 kW system, that is $21,000 against $31,500 before the first loan payment.

A loan can still work where power is expensive. In our Massachusetts example, a loan at the loan-median price still leaves the household between about $15,000 and $46,000 ahead after 25 years, depending on the rate and term. In a low-price state such as Tennessee, none of the loan cases we ran comes out ahead within 25 years.

Berkeley Lab's financing figures come from the roughly 45% of host-owned systems where it could identify the financing type with high confidence. The gap describes the market, not any single quote, so always ask your installer for both prices on the same system.

What the loan costs

We do not know your loan rate, so here are three illustrative annual rates (APR) at two terms on $31,500, the loan-median price for 7 kW. These are not market quotes; replace them with the terms you are offered.

APR Term Monthly payment Total repaid Interest
6% 10 years $350 $41,966 $10,466
6% 20 years $226 $54,162 $22,662
8% 10 years $382 $45,862 $14,362
8% 20 years $263 $63,235 $31,735
10% 10 years $416 $49,953 $18,453
10% 20 years $304 $72,956 $41,456

A longer term lowers the payment but roughly doubles the interest. At 8% over 20 years, the household repays about three times the $21,000 cash price.

Three states, cash against loan

Same 7 kW example home (10,500 kWh a year, full retail credit for exports, electricity prices rising 2.5% a year, September 2026 EIA prices), in a high-, middle- and low-price state. The cash case pays $21,000 up front. The loan case borrows $31,500 with nothing down, and "25-year result" is total savings minus total loan payments.

"Monthly shortfall" is the loan payment minus the first year's bill savings after $100 of upkeep: what you pay out of pocket each month in year one.

Three patterns:

  • The loan price premium is the bigger cost in short loans. Over 10 years at 6%, interest is $10,466, about the same as the $10,500 gap between the cash and loan medians.
  • Long terms only work where savings are large. In Massachusetts, a 20-year loan at 6% roughly matches the payment from the first year. In Illinois the same loan ends slightly behind after 25 years.
  • In low-price states, financing at the loan-median price does not pay in our model. Tennessee's cash case already takes 15.6 years.

If your installer quotes the same price for cash and loan, the premium disappears and only interest remains, which changes these results a lot. That is the first question to ask.

When a loan makes sense

  • You do not have the cash, or you would have to drain your emergency savings to pay it.
  • You live where power is expensive, and the payment is close to or below your expected monthly savings.
  • The loan price matches the cash price, or close to it.
  • You have read the full terms: APR, fees, term, and whether you can repay early without a penalty.

When cash makes sense

  • You have the money and no better use for it.
  • The installer offers a lower price for cash.
  • Your state's payback is already long. Every extra dollar of cost stretches it further; solar panel cost per watt shows the most you can pay in your state for a 10- or 15-year payback.

Where they tie

  • Export credit. A lower credit for exported power cuts savings under both options; a loan just has less room to absorb it. Net metering vs. net billing shows by how much.
  • Seasons. Savings are smaller in winter, but loan payments are not. Check the monthly picture, not just the yearly one.
  • No federal credit. The IRS says the Residential Clean Energy Credit "is not available for any property placed in service after December 31, 2025," for cash and loan buyers alike.

Questions for the installer and lender

  • What is the cash price, and what is the loan price, for the same system?
  • What is the APR, the term and the total amount I will repay?
  • Are any lender fees included in the system price?
  • Can I repay early without a penalty?
  • What monthly savings do you expect in my first year, and which electricity price and export credit sit behind that figure?

Then compare both options with your own numbers in the solar calculator. This is general information, not financial advice.
