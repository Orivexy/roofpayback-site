Solar in Alaska vs. Hawaii: Which Pays Back Faster?

Alaska and Hawaii both have costly power. See how sunshine, prices and export credit change solar payback in each state, with side-by-side numbers.

Published November 13, 2026·Updated November 18, 2026·5 min read·RoofPayback editorial·Figures checked against EIA and PVWatts data

The answer

Hawaii, by a wide margin. In our example, a system in Honolulu pays for itself in 4.8 years with full export credit. The same system in Anchorage takes 12.1 years.

You'd expect the two to be close, since both states pay far more for electricity than the U.S. average. They aren't. Two things split them: how high the local price is, and how much sunshine turns into kilowatt-hours. Hawaii wins on both.

Both results use our example home with no incentives, so a real quote and your utility's export rule will move them.

How expensive is power in each state?

Very, by national standards. The U.S. Energy Information Administration (EIA) put the U.S. average residential price at 18.16¢ per kWh in August 2026. Both states sit far above that.

Alaska Hawaii
Residential price, August 2026 29.41¢/kWh 47.18¢/kWh
A year earlier 27.79¢/kWh 38.53¢/kWh
One-year change 5.8% 22.5%
Example monthly cost, 900 kWh about $265 about $425

Keep in mind that EIA averages are utility revenue divided by kWh sold. That folds in taxes and fees. Your real bill depends on your rate plan, your usage and your utility.

EIA also groups the two states into one "Pacific Noncontiguous" division. Its residential price was 40.79¢ per kWh in August 2026, up 17.5% from 34.72¢ a year earlier. That was the largest jump among the ten divisions in our data. If you want to know why Hawaii in particular costs so much, read why electricity is expensive in Hawaii.

How much power will panels actually make?

Far more in Honolulu. Our estimates come from PVWatts, with south-facing panels at a 20° pitch and one reference city per state (see the methodology).

  • Honolulu, Hawaii: 1,622 kWh per kW of panels per year.
  • Anchorage, Alaska: 922 kWh per kW of panels per year.

The yearly totals also hide very different seasons. Anchorage gets long summer days and very short winter ones, so its panels make about 6.6 times as much from June to August as from December to February. Honolulu's ratio is 1.3: its output barely dips. Under an annual true-up, Alaska's summer surplus can carry part of the winter. Under monthly settlement, those dark months cost more. Solar panels in winter by state compares every state's seasonal gap.

What does payback look like side by side?

Payback is the number of years until your savings equal what you paid. It shifts with how your utility credits power you send back to the grid. We tested three cases: full credit at the retail price, half credit and quarter credit.

Export credit Alaska (Anchorage) Hawaii (Honolulu)
Full credit 12.1 years 4.8 years
Half credit 15.6 years 6.5 years
Quarter credit 18.1 years 8.3 years
Year-1 savings, full credit $1,898 $4,954

Look at Hawaii's worst case. At quarter credit, it pays back in 8.3 years. That still beats Alaska's best case of 12.1 years. A very high price makes up for a lot of lost credit.

Is Alaska a bad place for solar, then?

Not necessarily. Alaska holds up better than its weak sun suggests. Its price of 29.41¢ per kWh does much of the work.

Washington makes a useful contrast. The same example there gives a much longer payback, in a cloudier climate with a lower price; solar on the Pacific coast has the mainland numbers. A mid-range result in Alaska is respectable once you see where other states land. The national picture is in best states for solar payback.

Why does the export credit matter so much?

Because a large share of your solar power leaves the house. Our model assumes only about 40% of the household's electricity use happens while the sun is up, so much of what the panels make at midday goes to the grid. Whatever your utility pays for that power shapes the result.

Rules for crediting exported power vary by state and utility, and they have changed in the past. The Database of State Incentives for Renewables & Efficiency (DSIRE) tracks current programs. Check it, and your utility's tariff, before you lean on any estimate. Net metering vs. net billing explains how the main crediting systems differ.

Thinking of a system bigger than your yearly use? Our model caps credits at a year of grid purchases. The oversized system guide explains that limit.

What will a system cost, and is there still a tax credit?

Our example uses $3,500 per kW. National price ranges, and how a cheaper or dearer quote changes payback, are in solar panel cost by state. Remote and island markets can differ from national figures, so get several written quotes.

No federal credit applies to a new system in either state: the IRS says the 30% Residential Clean Energy Credit (Section 25D) is not available for property placed in service after December 31, 2025, which is why the example assumes no incentives. State or local programs may still exist, and DSIRE is the place to look. Is solar worth it without the tax credit covers the broader picture.

What else could change my result?

A few things, in either direction:

  • Future prices. Our model assumes electricity gets 2.5% more expensive each year. Hawaii's recent jump was much larger, but past changes don't predict future ones. See what if electricity prices rise.
  • Shade and roof direction. A shaded or north-facing roof will produce less than our south-facing assumption.
  • Panel aging and upkeep. We assume 0.5% less output each year and $100 per year for maintenance.
  • An electric vehicle. Charging 3,500 kWh a year at home costs about $1,029 in Alaska and about $1,651 in Hawaii at average prices. More use can make a bigger system worth a look. See solar and electric car charging.

How do I check my own numbers?

Start with your bill and work outward:

  1. Note your price per kWh and your yearly use.
  2. Enter them in the solar calculator.
  3. Compare your rate with the state figures on electricity prices, including Alaska and Hawaii.
  4. Read the state pages for solar panels in Alaska and solar panels in Hawaii.
  5. Ask your utility how it credits exported power, and whether that rule could change.

If you remember one thing

Hawaii has both more sun and a higher price than Alaska. Its very high rates give it some of the shortest solar paybacks in the country, even with a weak export credit. Alaska's sun is weaker, but expensive power still puts its payback ahead of most states.

For a Honolulu homeowner, the export rule mostly changes how fast solar pays, not whether it does. For an Anchorage homeowner, the export rule decides whether payback lands nearer 12 years or 18, and the dark winter makes monthly crediting costlier, so get the utility's crediting rule in writing before comparing quotes.
