Solar in Alaska vs. Hawaii: Which Pays Back Faster?

Hawaii still wins, but new systems no longer get retail credit. Payback with Hawaiian Electric's export rates, real household use and Alaska's range.

Published November 13, 2026·Updated November 28, 2026·5 min read·RoofPayback editorial·Figures checked against EIA and PVWatts data

Official data: EIA · DOE · IRS · how we check

The answer

Hawaii, clearly, but by less than the usual headline suggests. A Honolulu system only pays back in 4.7 years if every exported kWh earns the full retail price, and new Hawaiian Electric customers cannot get that: the Hawaii Public Utilities Commission capped net energy metering with an order issued on October 12, 2015, and Hawaiian Electric lists the program as closed to new applicants.

With the export rate Hawaiian Electric pays new customers on Oahu for daytime power, our 7 kW example home pays back in 7.8 years. A system sized for an average Hawaii household does better, at 5.5 to 6.9 years. Anchorage needs 12.7 years even with full retail credit, and longer if its utility pays less for exports.

Both states pay far above the national price, but neither household uses as much as the 10,500 kWh a year in our standard example home. That matters below. Why Hawaii's price is so high is covered in why electricity is expensive in Hawaii.

How much power will panels make?

PVWatts puts a south-facing system at a 20° pitch at 1,622 kWh per kW a year in Honolulu and 922 kWh in Anchorage, the lowest of any state's reference city. The seasons differ even more. Anchorage panels make about 6.6 times as much in June to August as in December to February; Honolulu's ratio is 1.3. Solar panels in winter by state has every state's seasonal gap.

What does a new Hawaii system earn for exports?

Hawaiian Electric's Smart Renewable Energy Export program credits exported power at time-of-day rates. Its program page, read on November 28, 2026, lists these rates for 2024 to 2026:

Island Daytime, 9am to 5pm Evening peak, 5pm to 9pm Overnight, 9pm to 9am
Oahu 13.5¢/kWh 32.9¢/kWh 18.9¢/kWh
Hawaii Island 10.6¢/kWh 23.1¢/kWh 14.8¢/kWh
Maui 6.6¢/kWh 18.2¢/kWh 13.1¢/kWh

The page says rates are locked for the first seven years of a new interconnection agreement and updated every three years, credits are reconciled annually, and credits left at the end of the year expire.

Panels without a battery export almost entirely during the day, so we priced exports at the Oahu daytime rate. Against the state's average price of 48.31¢, 13.5¢ is about 28% of retail. Two simplifications push the result slightly in solar's favor: our model raises the export credit 2.5% a year along with retail prices, while the real rate is fixed for seven years, and the state average price is not the Oahu rate.

What does payback look like?

The standard example (7 kW, $24,500, 10,500 kWh a year):

Export credit Alaska (Anchorage) Hawaii (Honolulu)
Full retail (upper limit) 12.7 years 4.7 years
Oahu daytime rate, about 28% of retail not applicable 7.8 years
Half retail 16.3 years 6.3 years
Quarter retail 18.9 years 8.1 years
No credit 22.4 years 11.1 years

In Hawaii, a small system that the home uses as it produces pays back fastest. A 2 kW system makes about 3,240 kWh a year and the house uses roughly three quarters of it directly, so the export rate barely matters. Each added kW pushes more power onto the grid at 13.5¢ instead of saving 48.31¢. A battery can shift some of that surplus into the evening; the battery payback calculator tests it with your own rates.

In Alaska, full credit makes size almost irrelevant: 4, 6 and 8 kW all land near 13 years. A weak credit separates them by more than four years, which is why the crediting terms are the first thing to settle there.

What does Alaska pay for exports?

We could not confirm Alaska's current rules in an official source for this update, so the Alaska rows show a range rather than one answer. Ask your utility two questions: what it pays per kWh for power you export, and whether unused credits carry from summer into winter. With Anchorage's 6.6-to-1 summer-to-winter ratio, a credit that expires monthly would cost far more than in Hawaii. DSIRE lists programs by state and utility.

How do I check my own numbers?

  1. Find your price per kWh and your yearly use on your bills.
  2. Hawaii: look up your island's current export rates on Hawaiian Electric's program page. Alaska: get your utility's export rate.
  3. Enter the use, the export credit as a share of your rate, and a system size near your daytime use in the solar calculator.
  4. Compare with the Alaska and Hawaii state pages.

Why Hawaii still comes out ahead

Hawaii's price is so high that even a kWh used at home without any export credit saves 48.31¢. That keeps payback under nine years for a 2 or 3 kW Hawaii system in every case we ran, including no credit at all. Alaska's expensive power makes solar viable despite the weakest sun in the country, but payback there sits in the low teens at best and depends heavily on what exports earn.
