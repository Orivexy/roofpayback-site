23:Tb8e,<div class="short-answer"><h2 id="short-answer">Short answer</h2>
<p>Alaska and Hawaii both pay a lot for electricity, so rooftop solar can look attractive in both. But the two states are very different places for panels. In the example below, Hawaii&#39;s system pays for itself in 4.8 years with full export credit. Alaska&#39;s takes 12.1 years. The gap comes from two things: how much sunshine turns into kilowatt-hours (kWh, the unit your utility bills you for) and how high the local price is.</p>
<p>Both results assume the same example home: a 7 kW system costing $24,500 with 10,500 kWh of yearly use and no incentives. The <a href="/methodology/">methodology</a> page covers the rest. Your own quote and utility rules will change the numbers.</p>
</div><h2 id="two-expensive-states-two-different-stories">Two expensive states, two different stories</h2>
<p>The U.S. average residential price was 18.16¢ per kWh in August 2026, according to the U.S. Energy Information Administration (EIA). Alaska and Hawaii sit far above that line.</p>

<p>EIA averages are utility revenue divided by kWh sold, so they include taxes and fees. A real bill depends on your rate plan, your usage and your utility.</p>
<p>EIA also groups the two states together as the &quot;Pacific Noncontiguous&quot; division. Its residential price was 40.79¢ per kWh in August 2026, up 17.5% from 34.72¢ a year earlier. That is the largest jump among the ten divisions in our data. For more on why Hawaii in particular costs so much, see <a href="/guides/why-is-electricity-expensive-in-hawaii/">why electricity is expensive in Hawaii</a>.</p>
<h2 id="what-the-sun-delivers-in-each-state">What the sun delivers in each state</h2>
<p>Solar savings start with production. Our estimates use PVWatts, a free solar calculator from the U.S. Department of Energy&#39;s National Laboratory of the Rockies, with south-facing panels at a 20° pitch and one reference city per state.</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Honolulu, Hawaii:</strong> 1622 kWh per kW of panels per year.</li>
<li><strong>Anchorage, Alaska:</strong> 922 kWh per kW of panels per year.</li>
</ul>
<p>Anchorage gets long summer days and very short winter ones. A system there can make plenty of power in June and very little in December. Since the year-end total is what matters for an annual credit, the averages hide a lot of seasonal swing. If you want to see how winter changes the picture elsewhere, read <a href="/guides/solar-panels-in-winter-by-state/">solar panels in winter by state</a>.</p>

37:T1433,<h2 id="the-payback-math-side-by-side">The payback math side by side</h2>
<p>Payback is the number of years until your savings equal what you paid. It depends on how your utility credits the power you send back to the grid. We test three cases: full credit at the retail price, half credit and quarter credit.</p>
<table>
<thead>
<tr>
<th>Export credit</th>
<th>Alaska (Anchorage)</th>
<th>Hawaii (Honolulu)</th>
</tr>
</thead>
<tbody><tr>
<td>Full credit</td>
<td>12.1 years</td>
<td>4.8 years</td>
</tr>
<tr>
<td>Half credit</td>
<td>15.6 years</td>
<td>6.5 years</td>
</tr>
<tr>
<td>Quarter credit</td>
<td>18.1 years</td>
<td>8.3 years</td>
</tr>
<tr>
<td>Year-1 savings, full credit</td>
<td>$1898</td>
<td>$4954</td>
</tr>
</tbody></table>
<p>Two points stand out.</p>
<p>First, Hawaii stays quick even when credit drops. Its quarter-credit result, 8.3 years, is still shorter than Alaska&#39;s full-credit result of 12.1 years. A high price makes up for a lot of lost credit.</p>
<p>Second, Alaska holds up better than its low sun suggests. Its price of 29.41¢ per kWh does much of the work. Compare it with Washington, where the same example gives a much longer payback in a cloudier climate with a lower price. You can see the national picture in <a href="/guides/best-states-for-solar-payback/">best states for solar payback</a>.</p>
<h2 id="why-export-credit-matters-so-much-in-hawaii">Why export credit matters so much in Hawaii</h2>
<p>Our model assumes you use about 40% of your solar power while the sun is up. The rest goes to the grid, and the credit you get for it changes the result. Rules for crediting exported power vary by state and utility, and they have changed in the past. The Database of State Incentives for Renewables &amp; Efficiency (DSIRE) tracks current programs, so check it and your utility&#39;s tariff before relying on any estimate.</p>
<p>For a closer look at how different crediting systems change payback, see <a href="/guides/net-metering-vs-net-billing-payback/">net metering vs. net billing</a>. If a system is bigger than your yearly use, our model caps credits at a year of grid purchases. The <a href="/guides/oversized-solar-system-annual-true-up/">oversized system guide</a> explains that limit.</p>
<h2 id="costs-incentives-and-what-could-change-the-answer">Costs, incentives and what could change the answer</h2>
<p>Our example uses $3500 per kW. Berkeley Lab reports a median installed price of $4.0 per watt for host-owned residential systems installed in 2024, with cash purchases at a median of $3.5 per watt and loan-financed systems at $4.7 per watt. Prices in remote or island markets can differ from national medians, so get several written quotes.</p>
<p>The 30% federal Residential Clean Energy Credit under Section 25D is not available for property placed in service after December 31, 2025. Our example therefore assumes no incentives. State or local programs may still exist, and DSIRE is the place to look. See <a href="/guides/solar-incentives-after-federal-tax-credit/">solar incentives after the federal tax credit</a> for the broader picture.</p>
<p>Other factors can shift your result:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Rising prices.</strong> Our model assumes electricity gets 2.5% more expensive each year. Hawaii&#39;s recent jump was much larger, but past changes do not predict future ones. See <a href="/guides/solar-payback-if-electricity-prices-rise/">what if electricity prices rise</a>.</li>
<li><strong>Shading and roof direction.</strong> A shaded or north-facing roof will produce less than our south-facing assumption.</li>
<li><strong>Panel aging.</strong> We assume 0.5% less output each year and $100 per year for maintenance.</li>
<li><strong>Electric vehicles.</strong> Charging 3500 kWh a year at home costs about $1029 in Alaska and about $1651 in Hawaii at average prices. More use can make a bigger system worth considering. See <a href="/guides/solar-and-electric-car-charging/">solar and electric car charging</a>.</li>
</ul>
<h2 id="how-to-check-your-own-numbers">How to check your own numbers</h2>
<ol>
<li>Find your latest bill and note your price per kWh and yearly use.</li>
<li>Run your numbers through the <a href="/solar-calculator/">solar calculator</a>.</li>
<li>Compare your rate with the state figures on <a href="/electricity-prices/">electricity prices</a>, including <a href="/electricity-prices/alaska/">Alaska</a> and <a href="/electricity-prices/hawaii/">Hawaii</a>.</li>
<li>Read the state pages for <a href="/solar-panels/alaska/">solar panels in Alaska</a> and <a href="/solar-panels/hawaii/">solar panels in Hawaii</a>.</li>
<li>Ask your utility how it credits exported power, and whether the rule could change.</li>
</ol>
<h2 id="bottom-line">Bottom line</h2>
<p>Hawaii&#39;s very high prices give it some of the shortest solar paybacks in the country. Alaska has weaker sun but still shows a mid-range result, because its power is expensive too. In both states, the credit your utility pays for exported power matters a great deal, and costs vary by installer. Treat these figures as a starting point, not a quote, and compare real offers before you decide.</p>
