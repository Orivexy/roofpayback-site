22:T9a0,<div class="short-answer"><p class="short-answer-label">The answer</p>
<p>Hawaii, clearly, but by less than the usual headline suggests. A Honolulu system only pays back in 4.7 years if every exported kWh earns the full retail price, and new Hawaiian Electric customers cannot get that: the Hawaii Public Utilities Commission capped net energy metering with an order issued on October 12, 2015, and Hawaiian Electric lists the program as closed to new applicants.</p>
<p>With the export rate Hawaiian Electric pays new customers on Oahu for daytime power, our 7 kW example home pays back in <strong>7.8 years</strong>. A system sized for an average Hawaii household does better, at <strong>5.5 to 6.9 years</strong>. Anchorage needs <strong>12.7 years</strong> even with full retail credit, and longer if its utility pays less for exports.</p>

<p>The page says rates are locked for the first seven years of a new interconnection agreement and updated every three years, credits are reconciled annually, and credits left at the end of the year expire.</p>
<p>Panels without a battery export almost entirely during the day, so we priced exports at the Oahu daytime rate. Against the state&#39;s average price of 48.31¢, 13.5¢ is about 28% of retail. Two simplifications push the result slightly in solar&#39;s favor: our model raises the export credit 2.5% a year along with retail prices, while the real rate is fixed for seven years, and the state average price is not the Oahu rate.</p>

<p>In Hawaii, a small system that the home uses as it produces pays back fastest. A 2 kW system makes about 3,240 kWh a year and the house uses roughly three quarters of it directly, so the export rate barely matters. Each added kW pushes more power onto the grid at 13.5¢ instead of saving 48.31¢. A battery can shift some of that surplus into the evening; the <a href="/battery-payback-calculator/">battery payback calculator</a> tests it with your own rates.</p>
<p>In Alaska, full credit makes size almost irrelevant: 4, 6 and 8 kW all land near 13 years. A weak credit separates them by more than four years, which is why the crediting terms are the first thing to settle there.</p>
<h2 id="what-does-alaska-pay-for-exports">What does Alaska pay for exports?</h2>
<p>We could not confirm Alaska&#39;s current rules in an official source for this update, so the Alaska rows show a range rather than one answer. Ask your utility two questions: what it pays per kWh for power you export, and whether unused credits carry from summer into winter. With Anchorage&#39;s 6.6-to-1 summer-to-winter ratio, a credit that expires monthly would cost far more than in Hawaii. DSIRE lists programs by state and utility.</p>
<h2 id="how-do-i-check-my-own-numbers">How do I check my own numbers?</h2>
<ol>
<li>Find your price per kWh and your yearly use on your bills.</li>
<li>Hawaii: look up your island&#39;s current export rates on Hawaiian Electric&#39;s program page. Alaska: get your utility&#39;s export rate.</li>
<li>Enter the use, the export credit as a share of your rate, and a system size near your daytime use in the <a href="/solar-calculator/">solar calculator</a>.</li>
<li>Compare with the <a href="/solar-panels/alaska/">Alaska</a> and <a href="/solar-panels/hawaii/">Hawaii</a> state pages.</li>
</ol>
<h2 id="why-hawaii-still-comes-out-ahead">Why Hawaii still comes out ahead</h2>
<p>Hawaii&#39;s price is so high that even a kWh used at home without any export credit saves 48.31¢. That keeps payback under nine years for a 2 or 3 kW Hawaii system in every case we ran, including no credit at all. Alaska&#39;s expensive power makes solar viable despite the weakest sun in the country, but payback there sits in the low teens at best and depends heavily on what exports earn.</p>
