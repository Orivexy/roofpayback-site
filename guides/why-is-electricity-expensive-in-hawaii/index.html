Why Is Electricity So Expensive in Hawaii? EIA Data

Hawaii homes paid 48.31¢ per kWh in September 2026, 2.7 times the U.S. average. Ten years of EIA data show when the gap widened and what it costs.

Published November 7, 2026·Updated November 28, 2026·4 min read·RoofPayback editorial·Figures checked against EIA and PVWatts data

Official data: EIA · DOE · IRS · how we check

Plain-English answer

Mostly because of the fuel. The U.S. Energy Information Administration (EIA) puts it in one sentence: "Prices in Hawaii are high relative to other states mainly because most of its electricity is generated with petroleum fuels that must be imported into the state."

In September 2026, Hawaii homes paid an average of 48.31¢ per kilowatt-hour (kWh), 2.66 times the U.S. residential average of 18.17¢ and the highest of any state. A year earlier the figure was 39.53¢, so it rose 22.2% in twelve months.

The general reasons prices vary are in why electricity prices differ by state. This guide uses ten years of Hawaii data to show when the gap opened and what it means for a household.

Why fuel sets the price

The EIA says that "the cost of generating electricity is the largest component of the price of electricity," so whatever makes generation expensive dominates the rate. In Hawaii that is petroleum shipped in from outside the state. Fuel costs also swing: the EIA notes that fuel prices, "especially for natural gas and petroleum fuels (mainly in Hawaii and villages in Alaska), may increase during periods of high electricity demand."

If fuel is the cause, every type of customer should pay more, not just homes. The September 2026 figures show exactly that:

Customer type Hawaii U.S. average Hawaii as a multiple
Residential 48.31¢/kWh 18.17¢/kWh 2.66×
Commercial 45.06¢/kWh 14.27¢/kWh 3.16×
Industrial 40.89¢/kWh 9.52¢/kWh 4.30×
All sectors 44.47¢/kWh 14.62¢/kWh 3.04×

Hawaii's industrial customers, normally the cheapest class, paid 2.25 times what the average U.S. household paid. Businesses pay a larger multiple of their national average than homes do, which points to the cost of producing power rather than anything specific to residential billing. Residential vs. business electricity prices explains why the classes normally split apart.

Ten years of prices

EIA-861M, the agency's monthly survey of utility sales and revenue, lets us follow the average residential price year by year (revenue divided by kWh sold; months from January 2026 are preliminary):

Year Hawaii U.S. Hawaii as a multiple
2016 27.48¢ 12.55¢ 2.19×
2018 32.47¢ 12.87¢ 2.52×
2020 30.28¢ 13.16¢ 2.30×
2021 33.49¢ 13.65¢ 2.45×
2022 43.03¢ 15.04¢ 2.86×
2024 42.87¢ 16.48¢ 2.60×
2025 40.40¢ 17.23¢ 2.34×
2026, January to September 46.64¢ 18.19¢ 2.56×

Three things show up in the series:

  • One step, not a slope. Most of the decade's increase came in a single year. Hawaii's average rose 28.5% from 2021 to 2022, against 10.2% nationally, and has not returned to its earlier level.
  • It moves in both directions. Prices fell in 2019 and 2020, slightly in 2023 and again in 2025, which is what a fuel-linked price does. Over the nine years from 2016 to 2025 Hawaii rose 47%, against 37.3% for the U.S.
  • 2026 has been volatile. The monthly price went from 39.79¢ in January to a peak of 52.72¢ in June, then eased to 48.31¢ in September.

The data show when prices jumped, not why each move happened. The EIA's explanation of Hawaii's level is the fuel mix; we have not attributed individual years to specific fuel prices.

What it means for a household

Hawaii homes use less electricity than almost anyone. Over October 2025 to September 2026, the average Hawaii household used 515 kWh a month, 58% of the U.S. average of 885 kWh. Its bill still averaged $232 a month, the highest in the country and 1.45 times the U.S. average of $160. The most expensive month was August, at $269.

So Hawaii households already conserve: the price is 2.66 times the national one, but the bill is only 1.45 times as large. Each further kWh saved is worth 48.31¢ at the September price, so the same efficiency step pays back about 2.7 times as fast as at the U.S. average price. How to lower your electric bill lists the measures; the electricity cost calculator prices them at your rate.

What it means for solar

A high price raises the value of every kWh your panels make and use at home. It does less for power you send to the grid. New Hawaiian Electric customers are credited under time-of-day export rates; on Oahu the daytime rate for 2024 to 2026 is 13.5¢ per kWh, a little over a quarter of the average residential price. That pushes the best design toward small systems the house uses directly. Solar in Alaska vs. Hawaii runs the payback with those rates and with Hawaii's real household use.

Common misreadings

"A 22.2% rise means prices will keep climbing that fast." The ten-year series argues against it: the yearly average fell in three of the six years from 2020 to 2025. Recent EIA months are also preliminary and can be revised.

"Being an island explains everything." Separate island grids are often mentioned, but the EIA statement quoted above names imported petroleum as the main reason, and the sector figures fit that explanation. We do not put numbers on the other factors.

"Homes are being overcharged compared with businesses." In Hawaii, businesses pay a larger multiple of their national average than homes do.

Glossary

  • Residential, commercial, industrial: homes; businesses such as stores and offices; factories and other large users.
  • All sectors: the average across every customer type.
  • EIA-861M: the EIA's monthly survey of utility sales, revenue and customer counts, used here for the ten-year series and household bills.
  • Preliminary data: recent EIA figures that may still be revised.
