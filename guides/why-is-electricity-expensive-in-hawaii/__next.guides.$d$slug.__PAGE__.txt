24:Tc2d,<div class="short-answer"><h2 id="short-answer">Short answer</h2>
<p>Hawaii&#39;s average residential electricity price was 47.18¢ per kilowatt-hour (kWh) in August 2026, according to the U.S. Energy Information Administration (EIA). The U.S. residential average was 18.16¢. A kWh is the unit your utility bills you for; it measures the energy you use over time. Hawaii is the highest-priced state in the EIA data, and the gap grew over the past year.</p>
<p>This guide sticks to what the numbers show. The EIA tables tell us <em>how much</em> Hawaii pays, not <em>why</em>, so we do not guess at causes here. For the reasons behind state price gaps in general, see <a href="/guides/why-electricity-prices-differ-by-state/">why electricity prices differ by state</a>.</p>
</div><h2 id="the-gap-in-plain-numbers">The gap in plain numbers</h2>

35:T13cc,<h2 id="how-fast-did-it-move">How fast did it move?</h2>
<p>A year earlier, in August 2025, Hawaii&#39;s residential price was 38.53¢. By August 2026 it was 47.18¢, a change of 22.5%. The U.S. residential average rose 3.4% over the same period.</p>
<p>The census division that includes Hawaii and Alaska, which EIA calls Pacific Noncontiguous, went from 34.72¢ to 40.79¢, up 17.5%. Alaska itself rose 5.8%, so most of that movement came from Hawaii.</p>
<p>Two caveats matter. First, EIA marks recent months as preliminary, so figures can be revised. Second, a single month is a snapshot. Fuel costs, seasonal use and billing timing can all shift a monthly average. Treat 22.5% as a sign of direction, not a forecast. Our <a href="/guides/electricity-price-increases-by-state/">electricity price increases by state</a> guide tracks the wider picture.</p>
<h2 id="what-it-means-for-a-household-bill">What it means for a household bill</h2>
<p>A higher price per kWh multiplies through every appliance. Air conditioning, a refrigerator, an electric water heater and an EV charger each cost more to run than they would on the mainland. If you want to see your own number, plug your monthly kWh into the <a href="/electricity-cost-calculator/">electricity cost calculator</a>. Your utility bill shows your actual rate and any fixed charges, and those vary.</p>
<p>Hawaii is also one of the few places where cutting use pays back unusually fast, simply because each saved kWh is worth so much more. The practical steps in <a href="/guides/how-to-lower-your-electric-bill/">how to lower your electric bill</a> apply anywhere, but the same effort is worth more at 47.18¢ than at 18.16¢.</p>
<h2 id="what-it-means-for-rooftop-solar">What it means for rooftop solar</h2>
<p>Solar is the other lever. A rooftop system makes electricity you would otherwise buy. When the grid price is high, each kWh your panels produce displaces a more expensive one.</p>
<p>In our example home (a 7 kW system costing $24,500, 10,500 kWh of use a year, no incentives), the price you pay per kWh is the main driver of payback. The <a href="/methodology/">methodology</a> page lists the remaining assumptions. Hawaii&#39;s price level is why the state tends to look strong in payback comparisons; you can see how it stacks up on the <a href="/solar-panels/hawaii/">Hawaii solar page</a> and in <a href="/guides/best-states-for-solar-payback/">best states for solar payback</a>.</p>
<p>Some points to keep straight:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Prices can change.</strong> Payback calculations assume a price path. If rates fall, payback lengthens. See <a href="/guides/solar-payback-if-electricity-prices-rise/">solar payback if electricity prices rise</a>.</li>
<li><strong>Incentives have changed.</strong> The 30% federal Residential Clean Energy Credit (Section 25D) is not available for property placed in service after December 31, 2025, according to the IRS. Our example home assumes no incentives at all.</li>
<li><strong>Rules vary by utility.</strong> Net metering and solar incentive programs differ by state and utility, and DSIRE keeps a database of current programs. Hawaii&#39;s rules for exporting power may differ from what you expect, so read your utility&#39;s current terms before you rely on any credit.</li>
<li><strong>Quotes differ.</strong> Berkeley Lab reports a median installed price of $4.0 per watt for host-owned residential systems installed in 2024, with a 20th to 80th percentile range of $3.0 to $5.2 per watt, before incentives. Your own quote may land anywhere in that range.</li>
</ul>
<p>To model your own roof, use the <a href="/solar-calculator/">solar calculator</a>. Production estimates there come from PVWatts, the free estimator from the U.S. Department of Energy&#39;s National Laboratory of the Rockies.</p>
<h2 id="what-we-cannot-say-from-this-data">What we cannot say from this data</h2>
<p>It is tempting to list reasons for Hawaii&#39;s prices: isolation, imported fuel, the cost of running separate island grids. Those may well matter, but the EIA tables above do not measure them, so we have not attached numbers or weight to any of them. If you want the causes, look for explanations published by Hawaii&#39;s utility regulators and the state energy office.</p>
<p>What the data does show is simple. Hawaii&#39;s residential price is the highest in the country, its commercial and industrial prices are also far above the national levels, and the residential price rose 22.5% in a year.</p>
<h2 id="bottom-line">Bottom line</h2>
<p>Electricity in Hawaii costs 47.18¢ per kWh for homes, against 18.16¢ for the U.S. as a whole. Businesses and factories there pay far more than their mainland peers too, and the household price jumped 22.5% in a year. That makes every kWh you save or generate worth more, but it also means your bill is sensitive to future price moves. Check your own utility rate, get more than one quote if you consider solar, and run your numbers in the <a href="/solar-calculator/">solar calculator</a> before deciding.</p>
