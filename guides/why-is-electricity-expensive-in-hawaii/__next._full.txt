38:T14fd,<div class="short-answer"><h2 id="plain-english-answer">Plain-English answer</h2>
<p>Hawaii&#39;s power is expensive mainly because of the fuel it is made from. The U.S. Energy Information Administration (EIA) says it directly: &quot;Prices in Hawaii are high relative to other states mainly because most of its electricity is generated with petroleum fuels that must be imported into the state.&quot;</p>
<p>That shows up on every bill. Homes in Hawaii paid an average of 47.18¢ per <a href="/guides/what-is-a-kilowatt-hour/">kilowatt-hour</a> (kWh) in August 2026, against a U.S. residential average of 18.16¢. No state in the EIA data charges more, and the gap widened over the past year. The general reasons prices differ from place to place are in <a href="/guides/why-electricity-prices-differ-by-state/">why electricity prices differ by state</a>; this guide follows the Hawaii case.</p>
</div><h2 id="how-it-works">How it works</h2>
<p>A price per kWh bundles several costs. The EIA says prices &quot;generally reflect the cost to build, finance, maintain, and operate power plants and the electricity grid,&quot; and that &quot;the cost of generating electricity is the largest component of the price of electricity.&quot; So whatever makes generation costly in a state tends to dominate its rate.</p>
<p>In Hawaii, that is fuel. Most of the state&#39;s electricity comes from burning petroleum, and that petroleum arrives from outside the state. Many mainland states draw on a wider mix of sources, and the EIA lists hydropower and wind among those that can produce low-cost electricity when conditions allow.</p>
<p>Petroleum also brings price swings. The EIA notes that fuel prices, &quot;especially for natural gas and petroleum fuels (mainly in Hawaii and villages in Alaska), may increase during periods of high electricity demand.&quot; A state that depends on one imported fuel feels those moves more directly than one with a broad mix.</p>

<p>Look at the industrial line. Hawaii&#39;s factory price, 39.33¢, is far above the national household price of 18.16¢. The whole system is costly, not only the part that serves homes, which is what a fuel-driven price looks like. For contrast, Texas businesses pay 8.96¢ commercial while Texas homes pay 15.91¢. Our <a href="/guides/residential-vs-business-electricity-prices/">residential vs. business prices guide</a> explains why those two numbers usually split apart.</p>
<h3>How fast it moved</h3>
<p>In August 2025, Hawaii&#39;s residential price was 38.53¢. A year later it was 47.18¢, a rise of 22.5%. Over the same stretch, the U.S. residential average rose 3.4%.</p>
<p>The EIA groups Hawaii and Alaska into a census division called Pacific Noncontiguous. That division went from 34.72¢ to 40.79¢, up 17.5%. Alaska alone rose 5.8%, so most of the division&#39;s jump came from Hawaii. Our <a href="/guides/electricity-prices-2026-08/">August 2026 electricity price report</a> tracks the rest of the country.</p>
<h2 id="an-everyday-analogy">An everyday analogy</h2>
<p>Think of the price per kWh as the price per gallon at a gas pump. Every appliance in your home is a car you have to fill. A refrigerator, an air conditioner, an electric water heater and an EV charger all drive the same routes in Hawaii as on the mainland. The trip is identical. The pump price is not.</p>
<p>Now picture a gas station whose fuel all comes in by ship. Its price follows the cost of the fuel plus the trip. Hawaii&#39;s power plants are in that position, and the price per kWh carries it.</p>
<p>The business numbers fit the same picture. At a warehouse store, bulk buyers usually get a discount. In Hawaii, even the bulk price (the industrial rate) is higher than what an ordinary shopper pays elsewhere (the national residential rate).</p>

46:T12ab,<h2 id="what-it-means-for-your-bill-and-for-solar">What it means for your bill and for solar</h2>
<p>A higher price per kWh multiplies through everything you plug in. To see your own figure, enter your monthly kWh in the <a href="/electricity-cost-calculator/">electricity cost calculator</a>. Your bill shows your actual rate and any fixed charges, and those vary.</p>
<p>Saving power pays off quickly here, because each saved kWh is worth so much. The steps in <a href="/guides/how-to-lower-your-electric-bill/">how to lower your electric bill</a> work anywhere, but the same effort is worth more at 47.18¢ than at 18.16¢.</p>
<p>Rooftop solar works on the same principle. Panels make electricity you would otherwise buy, so a high grid price makes each panel-made kWh more valuable. In our example home (a 7 kW system; see the <a href="/methodology/">methodology</a>), the price per kWh is the first thing that decides payback, and Hawaii&#39;s price is why it tends to look strong on the <a href="/solar-panels/hawaii/">Hawaii solar page</a>. How Hawaii compares with Alaska, the other high-price state outside the mainland, is in <a href="/guides/solar-in-alaska-and-hawaii-compared/">solar in Alaska vs. Hawaii</a>.</p>
<p>You can model your own roof in the <a href="/solar-calculator/">solar calculator</a>, which uses PVWatts production estimates.</p>
<h2 id="where-people-get-confused">Where people get confused</h2>
<p><strong>&quot;Homes must be overcharged compared with businesses.&quot;</strong> The data does not show that. Every customer class in Hawaii pays well above its national counterpart. If only homes paid more, the answer would lie in how homes are billed. Here, the cost runs through the whole system.</p>
<p><strong>&quot;A 22.5% jump means prices will keep climbing that fast.&quot;</strong> Not necessarily. EIA marks recent months as preliminary, so figures can be revised. One month is also a snapshot: fuel costs, seasonal use and billing timing all shift a monthly average. Because Hawaii&#39;s power depends on petroleum, its price can fall as well as rise when fuel moves. Read 22.5% as a direction, not a forecast.</p>
<p><strong>&quot;Being an island explains everything.&quot;</strong> The EIA&#39;s explanation is specific: imported petroleum fuels. Other factors, such as running separate grids on separate islands, are often mentioned, but the EIA statement quoted above names fuel as the main reason, and we attach no figures to the rest. Hawaii&#39;s utility regulators and state energy office publish more detail.</p>
<p><strong>&quot;High prices make solar a sure thing.&quot;</strong> High prices help, but two other points matter:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Prices can fall.</strong> Payback math assumes a price path, and lower rates stretch it. See <a href="/guides/solar-payback-if-electricity-prices-rise/">solar payback if electricity prices rise</a>.</li>
<li><strong>Export rules come second only to price.</strong> How your utility credits power you send to the grid can cut savings sharply when credits are low. Programs differ by utility, and DSIRE keeps a database of current ones. Read your utility&#39;s current terms before trusting any estimate.</li>
</ul>
<h2 id="glossary">Glossary</h2>
<ul>
<li><strong>Kilowatt-hour (kWh):</strong> the unit on your bill.</li>
<li><strong>Petroleum fuels:</strong> oil-based fuels burned in power plants; most of Hawaii&#39;s electricity comes from them, according to the EIA.</li>
<li><strong>Generation:</strong> making electricity at a power plant, the largest part of the price in EIA&#39;s description.</li>
<li><strong>Residential price:</strong> the average price homes pay per kWh, as reported by the EIA.</li>
<li><strong>Commercial and industrial prices:</strong> average prices paid by businesses and by factories or other large users.</li>
<li><strong>All sectors:</strong> the average across every customer type combined.</li>
<li><strong>Pacific Noncontiguous:</strong> the EIA census division made up of Hawaii and Alaska.</li>
<li><strong>Preliminary data:</strong> recent EIA figures that may still be revised.</li>
<li><strong>Net metering:</strong> a utility rule that credits you for power your panels send to the grid.</li>
</ul>
<h2 id="the-short-version">The short version</h2>
<p>Hawaii pays the most for electricity because most of it is generated from petroleum that must be shipped in, and generation is the biggest piece of any power price. Homes pay 47.18¢ per kWh against 18.16¢ nationally, factories there pay more than mainland households, and the residential price rose 22.5% in a year. For anyone in Hawaii, each kWh saved or produced on the roof is worth more than almost anywhere else, and the bill stays tied to the price of imported fuel.</p>
