21:T1d8b,<div class="short-answer"><p class="short-answer-label">What we found</p>
<p>Over the twelve months from October 2025 to September 2026, the cheapest state for home electricity was <strong>North Dakota, at 12.38¢ per <a href="/guides/what-is-a-kilowatt-hour/">kilowatt-hour</a></strong>, followed by Nebraska (12.78¢), Idaho (12.89¢), Louisiana (13.12¢) and Utah (13.21¢). The most expensive was <strong>Hawaii, at 45.05¢</strong>, then California (32.22¢), Massachusetts (30.44¢), Maine (29.83¢) and Rhode Island (29.6¢).</p>
<p>A Hawaii household paid 3.6 times as much per kWh as a North Dakota household over the year. On the mainland the gap was smaller but still large: California paid 2.6 times North Dakota&#39;s price.</p>
<p>We rank on twelve months rather than one because a single month can reorder the list. In September 2026 alone, Nevada was the cheapest state at 13.25¢; over the full year it ranks sixth. North Dakota, cheapest over the year, ranked seventh in September, because September was its most expensive month.</p>
</div><h2 id="the-full-ranking">The full ranking</h2>
<p>Prices are the average residential price over October 2025 to September 2026, computed from EIA-861M as total revenue divided by total kWh sold to homes, so heavy-use months count more. The September 2026 column is the latest single month. The last column shows the lowest and highest monthly price in the twelve months, which tells you how much a state&#39;s price moves within a year.</p>

2f:Tc83,<h2 id="finding-2-the-expensive-end-is-hawaii-california-and-the-northeast">Finding 2: the expensive end is Hawaii, California and the Northeast</h2>
<p>Nine of the ten most expensive places are Hawaii, Alaska, California and Northeastern states; the tenth is the District of Columbia. Hawaii stands apart: its 45.05¢ is 12.83¢ above California, the next state, which is more than North Dakota&#39;s entire price.</p>
<h2 id="finding-3-some-states-change-price-with-the-season-far-more-than-others">Finding 3: some states change price with the season far more than others</h2>
<p>Utah&#39;s monthly price stayed within 0.73¢ all year. Hawaii&#39;s swung by almost 13¢, from 39.74¢ to 52.72¢. Connecticut moved 8.08¢ and Illinois 7.49¢. In six low-price states (North Dakota, South Dakota, Nebraska, Idaho, Montana and Oklahoma) the highest price of the twelve months was September itself, and the lowest came in December or January. That is why they look more expensive in a September snapshot than over a full year.</p>
<p>For your own budget, a large swing means a single month&#39;s price is a poor guide. Use a full year of bills, or the twelve-month average above.</p>
<h2 id="finding-4-a-cheap-rate-does-not-mean-a-cheap-bill">Finding 4: a cheap rate does not mean a cheap bill</h2>
<p>Louisiana is the fourth cheapest state per kWh, but its average household bill over the same twelve months was $161, because Louisiana homes use the most power of any state. New Mexico, 23rd on price, had the lowest bill in the country at $102. California, second most expensive per kWh, had a $163 average bill because its homes use the least. The full bill and usage list is in <a href="/guides/average-electric-bill-by-state/">average electric bill by state</a>.</p>
<h2 id="how-we-measured-it">How we measured it</h2>
<p>The twelve-month averages and monthly ranges come from EIA-861M, the monthly survey of sales and revenue that utilities file with the U.S. Energy Information Administration (EIA). Price equals revenue divided by kWh sold, so taxes and fees are already included. Months from January 2026 are preliminary and can be revised. The September 2026 column matches the EIA Electric Power Monthly, Table 5.6.A. Your own rate depends on your utility and plan; compare your bill with your state on the <a href="/electricity-prices/">electricity prices</a> page.</p>
<h2 id="what-the-ranking-means-for-rooftop-solar">What the ranking means for rooftop solar</h2>
<p>Solar saves money by replacing kWh you would otherwise buy, so a state near the bottom of this table needs a cheaper system or more self-use to pay back, and a state near the top starts with an advantage. How your utility credits exported power is the other half of the answer, and in California and Hawaii new customers no longer get full retail credit; <a href="/guides/net-metering-vs-net-billing-payback/">net metering vs. net billing</a> explains the difference. For payback by state, see <a href="/guides/best-states-for-solar-payback/">best states for solar payback</a>, and for the low-price case, <a href="/guides/is-solar-worth-it-where-electricity-is-cheap/">is solar worth it where electricity is cheap</a>.</p>
