24:T9b5,<div class="short-answer"><h2 id="short-answer">Short answer</h2>
<p>Right now, Louisiana and Nevada have the cheapest residential electricity in the U.S. Louisiana averages 12.72¢ per kWh and Nevada 12.77¢ (EIA, July 2026). Hawaii is the most expensive at 48¢, followed by California at 33.61¢ and Maine at 32.41¢.</p>
<p>A kWh (kilowatt-hour) is the unit your utility bills you for. See <a href="/guides/what-is-a-kilowatt-hour/">what a kilowatt-hour is</a> if the term is new to you. The gap between the cheapest and priciest states is large. For the same 900 kWh in a month, the example bill is about $114 in Louisiana and about $432 in Hawaii.</p>
</div><h2 id="where-these-prices-come-from">Where these prices come from</h2>
<p>The U.S. Energy Information Administration (EIA) publishes average residential prices by state each month in the <a href="https://www.eia.gov/electricity/monthly/epm_table_grapher.php?t=epmt_5_6_a">Electric Power Monthly, Table 5.6.A</a>. Recent months are preliminary, so numbers can change a little.</p>
<p>Each state price is total utility revenue divided by kWh sold. That means it includes taxes and fees, and it is an average. Your own bill depends on your utility, your rate plan and how much power you use. Use your own bill as the final check.</p>

<p>One month of data is a snapshot. A year-over-year change from a single July can be affected by weather, fuel costs and rate cases. Do not treat it as a forecast.</p>
<h2 id="what-this-means-for-your-monthly-bill">What this means for your monthly bill</h2>
<p>Price per kWh is only half of the story. Your bill also depends on how many kWh you use. A hot-climate home with heavy air conditioning can use far more power than a mild-climate home. A cheap price does not always mean a cheap bill.</p>
<p>Electric vehicle owners feel the difference too. Charging at home with 3500 kWh a year costs about $445 in Louisiana and about $1680 in Hawaii in our example. See <a href="/guides/home-ev-charging-cost-by-state/">home EV charging cost by state</a> for the full list, and <a href="/guides/average-electric-bill-by-state/">average electric bill by state</a> for typical bills.</p>
<p>To estimate your own bill, try the <a href="/electricity-cost-calculator/">electricity cost calculator</a>. To look up any state, start at <a href="/electricity-prices/">electricity prices by state</a>.</p>
<h2 id="what-it-means-for-rooftop-solar">What it means for rooftop solar</h2>
<p>Solar saves money by replacing kWh you would have bought. So the higher your price, the more each solar kWh is worth. That is why high-price states often show faster payback in our model, even when they get less sun.</p>
<p>Our example home uses these assumptions: a 7 kW system, 10500 kWh per year of use, 40% of use while the sun is up, $3000 per kW installed ($21000 total), no incentives, $100 per year maintenance, electricity price rising 2.5% per year, panel degradation of 0.5% per year, and a 25-year horizon. Export credits are capped at a year of grid purchases with an annual true-up. Production comes from <a href="https://pvwatts.nlr.gov/">PVWatts</a>, the free estimator from the U.S. Department of Energy&#39;s National Laboratory of the Rockies.</p>
<p>Two cautions apply:</p>
<ul>
<li>The 30% federal Residential Clean Energy Credit (Section 25D) is not available for property placed in service after December 31, 2025, per the <a href="https://www.irs.gov/credits-deductions/residential-clean-energy-credit">IRS</a>. Read <a href="/guides/is-solar-worth-it-without-tax-credit/">is solar worth it without the tax credit</a>.</li>
<li>Net metering and similar programs vary by state and utility. <a href="https://programs.dsireusa.org/">DSIRE</a> keeps a database of current programs, and <a href="/guides/net-metering-vs-net-billing-payback/">net metering vs. net billing</a> explains why the rules change the math.</li>
</ul>
<p>For our ranking of states by payback, see <a href="/guides/best-states-for-solar-payback/">best states for solar payback</a>. To test your own numbers, use the <a href="/solar-calculator/">solar calculator</a>. Our <a href="/methodology/">methodology</a> page shows how we calculate.</p>
<h2 id="bottom-line">Bottom line</h2>
<p>Louisiana, Nevada and Utah sit near the bottom of the price table, while Hawaii, California and Maine sit at the top. The same 900 kWh costs very different amounts depending on where you live.</p>
<p>Use these averages as a starting point, not a quote. Check your own utility&#39;s rate, your usage and the rules in your state. If your price is high, solar may be worth a closer look. If it is low, the case is weaker and depends more on your own numbers.</p>
