23:T9d9,<div class="short-answer"><h2 id="short-answer">Short answer</h2>
<p>Solar in the Mid-Atlantic pays back faster than its modest sunshine would suggest, because electricity there is expensive. In our example home model, the full-credit payback runs from 9 years in New York City to 12.8 years in Wilmington, Delaware. The gap comes almost entirely from the price per kilowatt-hour (kWh), not from the sun.</p>
<p>The catch: those numbers assume you get full credit for the power you send back to the grid. If your utility pays less for exports, the timeline stretches, sometimes by years.</p>
</div><h2 id="what-we-mean-by-mid-atlantic">What we mean by Mid-Atlantic</h2>
<p>This guide covers six places: New York, New Jersey, Pennsylvania, Delaware, Maryland and Washington, D.C. Virginia sits in a different federal region, so it is left out here. For a wider look at the East Coast, see our guides to <a href="/guides/solar-panels-in-new-england/">New England</a> and <a href="/guides/solar-panels-in-the-southeast/">the Southeast</a>.</p>
<h2 id="the-bill-pressure-is-real">The bill pressure is real</h2>
<p>The U.S. Energy Information Administration (EIA) tracks average residential prices. In August 2026 the U.S. average was 18.16¢ per kWh. The Middle Atlantic census division (New York, New Jersey and Pennsylvania) averaged 25.17¢, up 6.1% from 23.73¢ a year earlier.</p>

37:T145d,<h2 id="the-sun-is-average-the-prices-are-not">The sun is average, the prices are not</h2>
<p>Yearly output per kilowatt of panels, from PVWatts v8 for one reference city each, is similar across the region: 1291 kWh/kW in New York City and Newark&#39;s 1306 at the low end, and 1411 in Washington at the high end. By comparison, Phoenix gets 1755.</p>
<p>So a Mid-Atlantic roof makes less power than a desert roof. Yet the payback can still be competitive, because each kWh it makes replaces an expensive one.</p>

<p>Two things stand out. First, even the slowest case here, Delaware at quarter credit, pays back within the 25-year horizon. In several cheaper-power states it does not. Second, the spread between full and quarter credit is 5.3 years in New York but 7.6 years in Delaware. Lower prices make the credit rules matter more.</p>
<p>Washington and New York look almost identical at full credit, even though Washington&#39;s panels produce more. The higher New York price offsets it.</p>
<h2 id="credit-rules-are-the-biggest-unknown">Credit rules are the biggest unknown</h2>
<p>Your payback depends on how your utility values exported power. Net metering gives you retail-rate credit; net billing usually pays less. Rules differ by state and sometimes by utility, so check the <a href="https://programs.dsireusa.org/">DSIRE database</a> before relying on any estimate. Our explainer on <a href="/guides/net-metering-vs-net-billing-payback/">net metering vs. net billing</a> shows how much the choice moves the numbers.</p>
<p>Also remember that about 40% of use in our model happens while the sun is up. The rest of your solar power is exported, so sizing matters. The <a href="/guides/oversized-solar-system-annual-true-up/">oversized system guide</a> explains why a bigger array does not always help.</p>
<h2 id="no-federal-credit-to-lean-on">No federal credit to lean on</h2>
<p>The IRS says the 30% federal Residential Clean Energy Credit (Section 25D) is not available for property placed in service after December 31, 2025. Every payback figure above assumes no incentives, so any state or local program you qualify for would shorten them. Our guide to <a href="/guides/solar-incentives-after-federal-tax-credit/">incentives after the federal credit</a> lists where to look.</p>
<p>Price also matters. Berkeley Lab reports a median installed price of $4.0 per watt for host-owned systems installed in 2024, higher than our $3.50 per watt example. A higher quote lengthens every number in the table.</p>
<h2 id="how-to-check-your-own-roof">How to check your own roof</h2>
<ol>
<li>Pull a year of bills and add up your kWh. Use that, not our 10,500, if yours differs.</li>
<li>Find your utility&#39;s export credit rule.</li>
<li>Run your numbers through the <a href="/solar-calculator/">solar calculator</a>.</li>
<li>Compare at least a few quotes, and ask each to show assumed degradation and price increases.</li>
<li>Use <a href="https://pvwatts.nlr.gov/">PVWatts</a> to sanity-check any production claim.</li>
</ol>
<p>For state specifics, see <a href="/solar-panels/new-york/">New York</a>, <a href="/solar-panels/new-jersey/">New Jersey</a> or <a href="/solar-panels/pennsylvania/">Pennsylvania</a>, and compare local rates on our <a href="/electricity-prices/">electricity prices</a> page.</p>
<h2 id="bottom-line">Bottom line</h2>
<p>In the Mid-Atlantic, the price of power does the heavy lifting. New York, Washington and New Jersey show the quickest paybacks in our model, while Delaware is slowest at full credit. Export rules are the swing factor, and the missing federal credit means your quote and local incentives matter more than ever. These are model results, not guarantees. Your utility, roof and contract decide the real outcome.</p>
