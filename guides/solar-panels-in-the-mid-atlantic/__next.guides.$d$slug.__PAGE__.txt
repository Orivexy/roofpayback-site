22:Tc2d,<div class="short-answer"><h2 id="the-answer">The answer</h2>
<p>Yes, rooftop solar can pay back reasonably fast in the Mid-Atlantic, and the reason isn&#39;t the sunshine. Electricity here is expensive, so every <a href="/guides/what-is-a-kilowatt-hour/">kilowatt-hour</a> (kWh) your panels make replaces a costly one. In our example home model, full-credit payback runs from about 9 years in New York City and Washington, D.C., to 12.8 years in Wilmington, Delaware.</p>
<p>Those figures assume your utility gives you full credit for the power you send back to the grid. Price is what puts the region ahead; the export rule comes second, and where it pays less, the timeline stretches by years.</p>
</div><h2 id="which-places-count-as-the-mid-atlantic-here">Which places count as the Mid-Atlantic here?</h2>
<p>Six: New York, New Jersey, Pennsylvania, Delaware, Maryland and Washington, D.C.</p>
<p>New York appears in the tables for comparison, but it has its own detailed guide, <a href="/guides/is-solar-worth-it-in-new-york/">is solar worth it in New York</a>, so this one spends its time on the other five. Virginia sits in a different federal region, so it&#39;s left out. If you live elsewhere on the East Coast, our guides to <a href="/guides/solar-panels-in-new-england/">New England</a> and <a href="/guides/solar-panels-in-the-southeast/">the Southeast</a> cover those areas.</p>
<h2 id="how-expensive-is-power-in-the-region-right-now">How expensive is power in the region right now?</h2>
<p>Expensive, and climbing. The U.S. Energy Information Administration (EIA) put the national residential average at 18.16¢ per kWh in August 2026. The Middle Atlantic census division (New York, New Jersey and Pennsylvania) averaged 25.17¢, up 6.1% from 23.73¢ a year earlier.</p>

35:T175c,<h2 id="isn-39-t-the-mid-atlantic-too-cloudy-for-solar">Isn&#39;t the Mid-Atlantic too cloudy for solar?</h2>
<p>Not too cloudy, just average. PVWatts v8 puts yearly output per kilowatt of panels, for one reference city in each place, at 1,291 kWh/kW in New York City and 1,306 in Newark at the low end. Washington, D.C., tops the region at 1,411. Phoenix, for comparison, gets 1,755.</p>
<p>So your roof will make less than a desert roof. What closes the gap is price: a kWh in a high-price state is worth far more on your bill than one in a cheaper-power state. Baltimore and Wilmington show the other side of that. They produce more per kW than Newark (1,392 and 1,363 kWh), yet pay back more slowly, because power in Maryland and Delaware costs less than in New Jersey.</p>

<p>Even the slowest case, Delaware at quarter credit, pays back inside the 25-year horizon. That isn&#39;t true in several cheaper-power states. These are model results, not quotes.</p>
<h2 id="how-much-do-export-rules-really-matter">How much do export rules really matter?</h2>
<p>A lot. After price, the export rule is the second thing that sets payback and the least certain input in these estimates. Net metering credits exports at the retail rate. Net billing usually pays less. Rules differ by state and sometimes by utility, so check the <a href="https://programs.dsireusa.org/">DSIRE database</a> before trusting any estimate, including ours.</p>
<p>The size of the swing varies. Going from full to quarter credit adds 5.3 years in New York but 7.6 years in Delaware. Where power is cheaper, credit rules weigh more heavily. Our explainer on <a href="/guides/net-metering-vs-net-billing-payback/">net metering vs. net billing</a> shows how much the choice moves the numbers.</p>
<p>Why do exports matter so much? The example assumes about 40% of the home&#39;s use happens while the sun is up. Whatever the panels produce beyond what the house is using at that moment goes to the grid, so a large share of output is exported and its value depends on the credit. That&#39;s also why a bigger array doesn&#39;t always help, as the <a href="/guides/oversized-solar-system-annual-true-up/">oversized system guide</a> explains.</p>
<h2 id="can-i-still-get-the-federal-tax-credit">Can I still get the federal tax credit?</h2>
<p>No, not for new systems: according to the IRS, the 30% federal credit ended for systems placed in service after 2025.</p>
<p>Every payback figure in this guide already leaves it out. Any state, local or utility program you qualify for would shorten them. <a href="/guides/is-solar-worth-it-without-tax-credit/">Solar without the tax credit</a> explains where to look for what remains.</p>
<h2 id="what-if-my-quote-costs-more-than-your-example">What if my quote costs more than your example?</h2>
<p>Then every number in the payback table gets longer. The example uses $3.50 per watt ($3,500 per kW), so divide your quote by its size in watts and compare. <a href="/guides/solar-panel-cost-by-state/">Solar panel cost by state</a> shows how payback shifts as the price per watt moves.</p>
<p>Ask for several quotes. Have each one show its assumed panel degradation (the slow loss of output as panels age) and the price increases it builds in. Then compare like with like.</p>
<h2 id="how-do-i-check-my-own-roof">How do I check my own roof?</h2>
<p>Work through these five steps:</p>
<ol>
<li>Pull a year of bills and add up your kWh. Use that total rather than our 10,500 if yours differs.</li>
<li>Find your utility&#39;s export credit rule.</li>
<li>Run your numbers through the <a href="/solar-calculator/">solar calculator</a>.</li>
<li>Compare at least a few quotes, and ask each to show assumed degradation and price increases.</li>
<li>Use <a href="https://pvwatts.nlr.gov/">PVWatts</a> to sanity-check any production claim.</li>
</ol>
<p>For state detail, see <a href="/solar-panels/new-york/">New York</a>, <a href="/solar-panels/new-jersey/">New Jersey</a> or <a href="/solar-panels/pennsylvania/">Pennsylvania</a>. Local rates are on our <a href="/electricity-prices/">electricity prices</a> page.</p>
<h2 id="if-you-remember-one-thing">If you remember one thing</h2>
<p>In the Mid-Atlantic, the price of power does the heavy lifting, not the sun. New York, Washington, D.C., and New Jersey show the quickest paybacks in our model; Delaware is slowest at full credit. After price, the export rule is what separates a 10-year payback from a 16-year one, so get your utility&#39;s rule in writing before you compare quotes.</p>
