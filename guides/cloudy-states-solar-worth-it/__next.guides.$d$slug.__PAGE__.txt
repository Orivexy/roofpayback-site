22:T1662,<div class="short-answer"><p class="short-answer-label">The verdict</p>
<p>In a cloudy state, solar is worth it when the grid price is well above what the panels&#39; electricity costs you. Clouds raise that cost, because the same $24,500 system makes fewer kWh. In Seattle, our example system&#39;s power works out to about 15¢ per kWh over 25 years, the same as the grid price there in September 2026, so it only pays off if rates rise. In Hartford it costs about 13.1¢ against a grid price of 27.09¢.</p>
<p>That comparison is the quickest test for a low-sun state, and it explains why some cloudy states pay back faster than sunny Arizona while others take close to 20 years.</p>

<p>For comparison, the same calculation gives about 9.3¢ in Phoenix and Las Vegas and 10.6¢ in Orlando. Sun lowers the cost of each solar kWh; the grid price decides whether that cost is a bargain. In Phoenix, 9.3¢ against a 14.92¢ grid price is a smaller margin than 13¢ against 29.17¢ in Manchester, which is why New Hampshire&#39;s payback (9.1 years) beats Arizona&#39;s (14 years).</p>
<p>Three groups appear in the table:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Wide margin:</strong> New Hampshire, Connecticut, Vermont, Michigan and Alaska, where grid power costs 1.6 to 2.2 times the solar kWh.</li>
<li><strong>Thin margin:</strong> West Virginia and Oregon, where the gap is a cent or three.</li>
<li><strong>No margin today:</strong> Washington, where the two are equal and the system relies on future price increases. At flat prices, the example there does not pay back within 25 years.</li>
</ul>
<h2 id="export-credit-counts-twice-in-a-cloudy-state">Export credit counts twice in a cloudy state</h2>
<p>The cost-per-kWh test assumes every kWh saves the retail price. Exports change that. In a low-sun state, a large share of the year&#39;s value is made in summer and banked for the darker months, so the credit for that summer surplus matters more than in a state with an even year. Portland and Seattle make about 12% of their yearly output in December to February, and Anchorage 6%; <a href="/guides/solar-panels-in-winter-by-state/">solar panels in winter by state</a> has the full seasonal table.</p>

<p>What applies to you depends on your state and utility. Two examples from official sources:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Connecticut:</strong> the state&#39;s Public Utilities Regulatory Authority says its Residential Renewable Energy Solutions program, &quot;launched in January 2022,&quot; replaces the legacy net metering program. The program has Buy-All and Netting tariffs, with rates updated for projects that enroll in 2026. Ask the installer which tariff the estimate assumes.</li>
<li><strong>Vermont:</strong> the Public Utility Commission reviews utility net-metering tariffs and updates net-metering rate adjustors every two years, with a 2026 biennial update listed on its site.</li>
</ul>

33:T6b9,<h2 id="what-clouds-change-beyond-the-numbers">What clouds change beyond the numbers</h2>
<ul>
<li><strong>Bigger arrays for the same use.</strong> Covering 10,500 kWh a year takes about 9.7 kW in Seattle against 6 kW in Phoenix, so roof space runs out sooner. <a href="/guides/how-many-solar-panels-do-i-need/">How many solar panels do I need</a> covers sizing.</li>
<li><strong>The cost per kWh is sensitive to the quote.</strong> Because the system makes fewer kWh, each extra dollar per watt raises the cost of a solar kWh more than it would in a sunny state. <a href="/guides/solar-panel-cost-by-state/">Solar panel cost by state</a> shows how price per watt moves payback.</li>
<li><strong>Production estimates deserve a second look.</strong> Ask which weather data the installer used and compare the yearly kWh with PVWatts for your address.</li>
</ul>
<h2 id="check-your-own-case">Check your own case</h2>
<ol>
<li>Divide your quote plus expected upkeep by the installer&#39;s 25-year production estimate. That is your cost per solar kWh.</li>
<li>Compare it with the price per kWh on your bill (or your state on the <a href="/electricity-prices/">electricity prices page</a>).</li>
<li>Ask how exports are credited and when credits are settled.</li>
<li>Run the result through the <a href="/solar-calculator/">solar calculator</a>, at flat prices first.</li>
</ol>
<p>If your grid price is well above your cost per solar kWh and summer credits carry into winter, a cloudy sky is not a reason to skip solar. If the two are close, as in Seattle today, the system depends on prices rising; <a href="/guides/solar-payback-if-electricity-prices-rise/">solar payback if electricity prices rise</a> shows how much.</p>
