Is Solar Worth It in Cloudy States? What the Numbers Show

What a solar kWh costs over 25 years in the eight lowest-sunshine states, compared with the grid price there, and how export credit changes the payback.

Published October 31, 2026·Updated November 28, 2026·5 min read·RoofPayback editorial·Figures checked against EIA and PVWatts data

Official data: EIA · DOE · IRS · how we check

The verdict

In a cloudy state, solar is worth it when the grid price is well above what the panels' electricity costs you. Clouds raise that cost, because the same $24,500 system makes fewer kWh. In Seattle, our example system's power works out to about 15¢ per kWh over 25 years, the same as the grid price there in September 2026, so it only pays off if rates rise. In Hartford it costs about 13.1¢ against a grid price of 27.09¢.

That comparison is the quickest test for a low-sun state, and it explains why some cloudy states pay back faster than sunny Arizona while others take close to 20 years.

What a solar kWh costs where the sun is weakest

The eight states below have the lowest yearly output per kW in our data. "Cost per solar kWh" is the example system's price ($24,500) plus 25 years of $100 upkeep, divided by everything it makes in 25 years with 0.5% yearly degradation. It is a simple, undiscounted average with no financing, and it assumes every kWh is worth the retail price to you.

State (reference city) Output per kW a year Cost per solar kWh Grid price, Sept. 2026 Payback, full credit
Alaska (Anchorage) 922 kWh 17.8¢ 28¢ 12.7 years
Washington (Seattle) 1,088 kWh 15¢ 15¢ 19.2 years
Oregon (Portland) 1,128 kWh 14.5¢ 15.73¢ 17.8 years
Vermont (Burlington) 1,222 kWh 13.4¢ 24.83¢ 10.9 years
West Virginia (Charleston) 1,232 kWh 13.3¢ 16.23¢ 16 years
Connecticut (Hartford) 1,252 kWh 13.1¢ 27.09¢ 9.8 years
New Hampshire (Manchester) 1,259 kWh 13¢ 29.17¢ 9.1 years
Michigan (Detroit) 1,291 kWh 12.7¢ 23.17¢ 11.1 years

For comparison, the same calculation gives about 9.3¢ in Phoenix and Las Vegas and 10.6¢ in Orlando. Sun lowers the cost of each solar kWh; the grid price decides whether that cost is a bargain. In Phoenix, 9.3¢ against a 14.92¢ grid price is a smaller margin than 13¢ against 29.17¢ in Manchester, which is why New Hampshire's payback (9.1 years) beats Arizona's (14 years).

Three groups appear in the table:

  • Wide margin: New Hampshire, Connecticut, Vermont, Michigan and Alaska, where grid power costs 1.6 to 2.2 times the solar kWh.
  • Thin margin: West Virginia and Oregon, where the gap is a cent or three.
  • No margin today: Washington, where the two are equal and the system relies on future price increases. At flat prices, the example there does not pay back within 25 years.

Export credit counts twice in a cloudy state

The cost-per-kWh test assumes every kWh saves the retail price. Exports change that. In a low-sun state, a large share of the year's value is made in summer and banked for the darker months, so the credit for that summer surplus matters more than in a state with an even year. Portland and Seattle make about 12% of their yearly output in December to February, and Anchorage 6%; solar panels in winter by state has the full seasonal table.

State Full credit Half credit Quarter credit
New Hampshire 9.1 years 12 years 14.2 years
Connecticut 9.8 years 12.9 years 15.2 years
Vermont 10.9 years 14.2 years 16.6 years
Michigan 11.1 years 14.6 years 17.3 years
Alaska 12.7 years 16.3 years 18.9 years
West Virginia 16 years 20.5 years 23.8 years
Oregon 17.8 years 22.7 years not within 25 years
Washington 19.2 years 24.1 years not within 25 years

High-price states absorb a weak credit; the thin-margin states do not. At quarter credit, Oregon and Washington drop out of the 25-year horizon entirely.

What applies to you depends on your state and utility. Two examples from official sources:

  • Connecticut: the state's Public Utilities Regulatory Authority says its Residential Renewable Energy Solutions program, "launched in January 2022," replaces the legacy net metering program. The program has Buy-All and Netting tariffs, with rates updated for projects that enroll in 2026. Ask the installer which tariff the estimate assumes.
  • Vermont: the Public Utility Commission reviews utility net-metering tariffs and updates net-metering rate adjustors every two years, with a 2026 biennial update listed on its site.

For other states, check your utility's tariff and your state utility commission. Net metering vs. net billing explains the arrangements.

What clouds change beyond the numbers

  • Bigger arrays for the same use. Covering 10,500 kWh a year takes about 9.7 kW in Seattle against 6 kW in Phoenix, so roof space runs out sooner. How many solar panels do I need covers sizing.
  • The cost per kWh is sensitive to the quote. Because the system makes fewer kWh, each extra dollar per watt raises the cost of a solar kWh more than it would in a sunny state. Solar panel cost by state shows how price per watt moves payback.
  • Production estimates deserve a second look. Ask which weather data the installer used and compare the yearly kWh with PVWatts for your address.

Check your own case

  1. Divide your quote plus expected upkeep by the installer's 25-year production estimate. That is your cost per solar kWh.
  2. Compare it with the price per kWh on your bill (or your state on the electricity prices page).
  3. Ask how exports are credited and when credits are settled.
  4. Run the result through the solar calculator, at flat prices first.

If your grid price is well above your cost per solar kWh and summer credits carry into winter, a cloudy sky is not a reason to skip solar. If the two are close, as in Seattle today, the system depends on prices rising; solar payback if electricity prices rise shows how much.
