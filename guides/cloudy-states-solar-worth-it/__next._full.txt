37:Te25,<div class="short-answer"><h2 id="the-verdict">The verdict</h2>
<p>Yes, solar can pay off in cloudy states. Sunshine counts, but the price of your electricity often counts for more. Vermont makes far less power per kW of panels than Arizona, yet in our example its payback is shorter: 11.3 years in Vermont versus 13.9 years in Arizona, with full export credit.</p>
<p>That is not a blanket yes. Low sun plus a middling power price, as in Washington and Oregon, makes for slow paybacks. Compare your own utility rate and your own quote, not a sunshine map, and test them in the <a href="/solar-calculator/">solar calculator</a>.</p>
<p>Every figure below uses the same 7 kW example home with no incentives (<a href="/methodology/">methodology</a>), with output from PVWatts for one reference city per state. Prices are EIA August 2026 residential averages; the U.S. average was 18.16¢ per <a href="/guides/what-is-a-kilowatt-hour/">kilowatt-hour</a>.</p>

<h2 id="myth-more-sunshine-always-means-bigger-savings">Myth: More sunshine always means bigger savings</h2>
<p><strong>Fact:</strong> Panels save you the price of each kWh they replace. Fewer kWh at a high price can be worth more than many kWh at a low one.</p>
<p>Arizona (Phoenix) and Maine (Portland) make the point clearly:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Arizona:</strong> 1,755 kWh per kW, power at 15.05¢ per kWh, year-1 savings of $1,580 with full credit, payback of 13.9 years.</li>
<li><strong>Maine:</strong> 1,319 kWh per kW, power at 32.11¢ per kWh, year-1 savings of $2,964, payback of 8 years.</li>
</ul>
<p>Maine&#39;s panels produce less, but each kWh they make displaces much pricier grid power.</p>
<p>Massachusetts and Nevada repeat the pattern. Massachusetts makes 1,302 kWh per kW and pays 30.48¢ per kWh, for an 8.5-year payback. Nevada makes 1,753 kWh per kW but pays 12.72¢, and its payback stretches to 16.1 years.</p>

46:T144a,<h2 id="myth-so-any-cloudy-state-is-now-a-safe-bet">Myth: So any cloudy state is now a safe bet</h2>
<p><strong>Fact:</strong> Cloudy is not the same as expensive. Where low output meets an average price, payback runs long.</p>
<p>Look back at the table. Washington makes 1,088 kWh per kW and pays 14.63¢. Oregon makes 1,128 kWh and pays 15.92¢. Their paybacks, 19.6 and 17.6 years, are among the longer ones in our data. West Virginia, at 16 years, is not far behind.</p>
<p>The difference from Vermont or Connecticut is the price, not the clouds. Our state pages for <a href="/solar-panels/washington/">Washington</a>, <a href="/solar-panels/oregon/">Oregon</a> and <a href="/solar-panels/vermont/">Vermont</a> show the details side by side, and the guide to <a href="/guides/is-solar-worth-it-where-electricity-is-cheap/">solar where electricity is cheap</a> covers what still works when the rate is low.</p>
<h2 id="myth-dark-winters-wipe-out-the-year">Myth: Dark winters wipe out the year</h2>
<p><strong>Fact:</strong> Winters are weak in cloudy states, but strong summers can balance them, if your utility credits the surplus.</p>
<p>The summer-to-winter ratio compares June-to-August output with December-to-February output. A higher number means a weaker winter compared with summer:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Washington:</strong> 128 kWh in winter, 417 kWh in summer per kW, a ratio of 3.3.</li>
<li><strong>Oregon:</strong> 134 kWh winter, 423 kWh summer, a ratio of 3.2.</li>
<li><strong>Alaska:</strong> 57 kWh winter, 372 kWh summer, a ratio of 6.6.</li>
<li><strong>Florida</strong>, for contrast: 332 kWh winter, 395 kWh summer, a ratio of 1.2.</li>
</ul>
<p>In a cloudy-state winter, panels may cover little of your bill. In summer they may overproduce. Over a full year, those can even out when your utility gives credit for extra summer power. Our example home caps export credits at a year of grid purchases, which works like an annual true-up (one yearly settlement of credits and charges).</p>
<p>Net metering, where exported power earns bill credit, and similar programs vary by state and utility. DSIRE keeps a database of current programs. Our guides to <a href="/guides/solar-panels-in-winter-by-state/">winter solar by state</a> and <a href="/guides/net-metering-vs-net-billing-payback/">net metering versus net billing</a> go deeper.</p>

<p>High prices cushion the drop. Vermont still pays back in 17.2 years at quarter credit. Oregon and Washington do not pay back within 25 years at that level. In a cloudy state the export rule matters twice over, because so much of the year&#39;s value is earned in summer and banked for winter. Your utility&#39;s real rules could fall anywhere in this range, so ask for them in writing.</p>
<h2 id="what-actually-decides-it">What actually decides it</h2>
<p>Four things to weigh alongside the clouds overhead.</p>
<p><strong>Your price per kWh.</strong> It comes first. It is the main reason Vermont, Connecticut and Alaska beat sunnier states.</p>
<p><strong>Your export credit.</strong> It comes second, and in a low-output state a weak credit can push payback past 25 years, as Oregon and Washington show at quarter credit.</p>
<p><strong>Your installed cost.</strong> The example uses $3,500 per kW. A higher quote means a longer payback; the <a href="/guides/solar-panel-cost-by-state/">solar panel cost by state guide</a> shows how much.</p>
<p><strong>Incentives.</strong> The example includes none. The federal 30% residential credit ended for systems placed in service after December 31, 2025, so any help now comes from state or utility programs listed in DSIRE. The <a href="/guides/is-solar-worth-it-without-tax-credit/">no-tax-credit guide</a> covers what is left.</p>
<p>To check your own case:</p>
<ol>
<li>Find your current rate on your bill and compare it with the figures above.</li>
<li>Ask your utility how it credits exported power and whether credits reset each year.</li>
<li>Get quotes and compare the price per watt.</li>
<li>Run your numbers in the <a href="/solar-calculator/">solar calculator</a>.</li>
</ol>
<p>Clouds lower the output of every panel, but they do not set the price of the power those panels replace. If your rate is closer to Vermont&#39;s 23.84¢ than to Washington&#39;s 14.63¢ and your utility banks summer surplus for winter, a gray sky is no reason to rule solar out.</p>
