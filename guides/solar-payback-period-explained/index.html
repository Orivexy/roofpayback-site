Solar Payback Period Explained: The Math, Step by Step

How solar payback is calculated, worked year by year for one home, why the simple formula overstates it, and what payback leaves out: return and gain.

The answer up front

A solar payback period is the number of years until the money a system saves you adds up to what it cost. The quick version divides the cost by one year's savings. The version we use adds the savings up year by year, because electricity prices and panel output both change over time.

For a Chicago home with the system described below, the quick formula gives 14.8 years and the year-by-year count gives 13 years. The rest of this guide shows both calculations, explains the gap, and covers what payback does not tell you.

The two formulas

Simple payback = net system cost ÷ (first-year bill savings − yearly upkeep)

Cumulative payback = the point at which the running total of each year's savings, minus upkeep, reaches the net system cost

"Net cost" is the installed price minus any rebate or credit you actually receive. Since the IRS says the Residential Clean Energy Credit "is not available for any property placed in service after December 31, 2025," a 2026 system's net cost is usually its full price unless a state or utility program applies.

A worked example: one Chicago home

Our example home is a 7 kW system costing $24,500 ($3,500 per kW) in a house that uses 10,500 kWh a year, 40% of it while the sun is up. In Chicago, PVWatts puts output at 1,308 kWh per kW a year, so the system makes about 9,160 kWh in its first year. Illinois homes paid an average of 19.14¢ per kilowatt-hour in September 2026 (EIA). We assume exported power earns the retail price, up to a year of grid purchases.

Step 1: first-year savings. The model puts year-1 bill savings at $1,753. Subtract $100 of upkeep and the system nets $1,653.

Step 2: simple payback. $24,500 ÷ $1,653 = 14.8 years.

Step 3: cumulative payback. Now let electricity prices rise 2.5% a year and panel output fall 0.5% a year, and add up the result:

Year Net savings that year Running total, after the $24,500 cost
1 $1,653 −$22,847
2 $1,688 −$21,159
5 $1,797 −$15,880
10 $1,993 −$6,316
12 $2,077 −$2,205
13 $2,120 −$85
14 $2,164 +$2,079
20 $2,448 +$16,040
25 $2,711 +$29,059

The running total crosses zero just after the end of year 13, so payback is 13 years. Yearly savings grow from $1,653 to $2,711 because each kWh the panels save is worth more as prices rise, which outweighs the slow loss of output.

Why the two answers differ

The simple formula freezes the first year forever. We switched each assumption off in turn for the same Chicago home:

Assumption Payback
Prices +2.5% a year, output −0.5% a year (our model) 13 years
Prices +2.5% a year, no loss of output 12.7 years
Flat prices, output −0.5% a year 15.4 years
Flat prices, no loss of output 14.8 years, the same as simple payback

So simple payback is the special case where prices never move and panels never age. The price assumption moves the answer by more than two years; panel wear by about a third of a year. Solar payback if electricity prices rise tests other price paths, and panel degradation and payback tests faster wear.

When an installer quotes a payback much shorter than simple payback, ask which price growth rate they assumed.

What payback leaves out

Payback says when you break even. It says nothing about what happens afterward, or how the money compares with other uses of it. Two more numbers from the same model fill the gap:

  • 25-year gain: total net savings minus the cost. For the Chicago home, $29,059.
  • Return: the yearly rate of return that the stream of savings represents on the upfront cost (an internal rate of return). For the Chicago home, about 6.5% a year.

Across states, the spread is wide:

Case Payback 25-year gain Return
Maine, full credit 8.2 years $65,367 12.4%
Hawaii, Oahu daytime export rate 7.8 years $71,945 13.2%
Illinois, full credit 13 years $29,059 6.5%
Florida, full credit 14.3 years $23,797 5.5%
Washington, full credit 19.2 years $9,533 2.5%

For Hawaii, exports are priced at Hawaiian Electric's 13.5¢ daytime export rate on Oahu, about 28% of the state's average retail price, because new customers no longer get retail credit.

A long payback is not a loss: even Washington's 19-year case ends up ahead after 25 years. But a 2.5% return leaves little room for a higher quote, a shaded roof or a cut in export credit, and the return is before any interest you pay if you borrow. Solar loan vs. cash works through financing.

Three payback claims, checked

"Solar pays for itself in five years." In our run of all 50 states and D.C., the only result under five years is Hawaii with full retail credit, and new Hawaiian Electric customers cannot get that. A five-year figure today needs an unusually low price per watt, a large rebate, or both, in a high-price state.

"Panels wear out before they pay for themselves." In the Chicago example, removing panel wear entirely changes payback from 13 to 12.7 years. Most of the output loss falls after break-even.

"A bigger bill means a faster payback." Not if the system is sized to the bill: cost and savings grow together. What changes payback is how much of the output the house uses directly and what exports earn. How many solar panels do I need covers sizing.

What moves the number, and where to look

  • Electricity price: the strongest driver across states. Best states for solar payback.
  • Export credit: decisive where power is cheap. Net metering vs. net billing.
  • Installed price per watt: Solar panel cost per watt.
  • System size: Can a solar system be too big?
  • Daytime use: Solar and daytime electricity use.

Rebuild any payback figure in four steps

  1. Take the net cost from the quote, after any incentive you are sure to receive.
  2. Ask for the first-year savings estimate and the electricity price and export credit behind it.
  3. Do the simple division. If the quoted payback is much shorter, find out which growth rate or incentive explains the difference.
  4. Run the same inputs, with your own bill, in the solar calculator, which adds up the years for you.
