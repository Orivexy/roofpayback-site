Solar Payback Period Explained: What Sets the Number

Solar payback depends on cost, output, price, export credit and self-use. See real examples by state and why a bigger system is not always better.

The answer up front

A solar payback period is the number of years before your bill savings add up to what you paid for the system.

Five things set that number: the system's cost, how much power it makes, what your electricity costs, what your utility pays for power you send back, and how much of your solar power you use yourself.

Of those, the price of a kilowatt-hour usually matters most, and the export credit comes second. To show how, we followed one household through a series of "what ifs." The same home pays back in 4.8 years in Hawaii and 19.6 years in Washington. Your own quote and your own utility's rules decide where you land.

Meet the household

The story uses our standard example home, a family using 10,500 kWh a year with no incentives, with output from PVWatts for one reference city per state (methodology).

Picture the family getting their first quote. The installer hands over a single payback figure. Six what-ifs sit behind it.

Scenario 1: The price tag

The family's first lesson is that payback is simple division. Cost goes on top, yearly savings go on the bottom. Raise the price while savings stay put, and the wait gets longer.

When they compare quotes, the number to line up is cost per kW. A total price alone hides the system size.

They also learn there is no federal discount to subtract from the cost. The 30% residential credit ended for systems placed in service after December 31, 2025, so any help has to come from a state or utility program, which DSIRE (programs.dsireusa.org) tracks. More on this in solar without the tax credit.

Scenario 2: If the family lived somewhere sunnier

Now imagine the same family packing up and moving the house to different cities. The panels don't change. Their output does.

State (city) Output per kW per year
Arizona (Phoenix) 1,755 kWh
California (Los Angeles) 1,677 kWh
Texas (Houston) 1,421 kWh
Illinois (Chicago) 1,308 kWh
Washington (Seattle) 1,088 kWh

More output means fewer panels can cover the same use. To produce 10,500 kWh a year, the family would need about 6 kW in Arizona, 7.4 kW in Texas and 9.7 kW in Washington. How many solar panels you need walks through that sizing.

Scenario 3: If power cost twice as much

The story turns at this point. Sun is not the main event. Price is.

Every kWh the panels make saves whatever the family would have paid for it. A higher price shortens payback, even under cloudier skies. For reference, the EIA's U.S. average residential price was 18.16¢/kWh in August 2026, against 17.57¢ a year earlier.

State (city) Output per kW Price Payback (full credit)
Hawaii (Honolulu) 1,622 kWh 47.18¢/kWh 4.8 years
California (Los Angeles) 1,677 kWh 29.75¢/kWh 7.4 years
Massachusetts (Boston) 1,302 kWh 30.48¢/kWh 8.5 years
Arizona (Phoenix) 1,755 kWh 15.05¢/kWh 13.9 years
Nevada (Las Vegas) 1,753 kWh 12.72¢/kWh 16.1 years
Washington (Seattle) 1,088 kWh 14.63¢/kWh 19.6 years

In Boston, the family's roof gets less sun than it would in Phoenix. Yet payback comes much sooner, because power in Boston costs about twice as much. Prices by state are on electricity prices by state, the best states for solar payback ranking digs further, and solar where electricity is cheap covers the low-price end. The EIA publishes these prices monthly in the Electric Power Monthly, Table 5.6.A. Recent months are preliminary.

Scenario 4: If the utility paid less for exports

Around midday, the family's panels often make more than the house uses. The extra flows to the grid. What they get back depends on their utility.

Under net metering, the credit is often close to the retail price. Under net billing, it can be lower. Rules vary by state and utility. We tested three versions: "full credit" at the retail price, then "half credit" and "quarter credit."

State (city) Full credit Half credit Quarter credit
California (Los Angeles) 7.4 years 9.8 years 12.5 years
Texas (Houston) 14.3 years 19.2 years 23.1 years
New York (New York City) 9 years 12 years 14.3 years
Illinois (Chicago) 12.9 years 17 years 20.1 years
Washington (Seattle) 19.6 years 24.6 years Not within 25 years

Say the family lives in Houston. Nothing about the roof changes, yet payback moves from 14.3 years to 23.1 years purely on the credit rule. That is why the export rule ranks second only to price: where credits are low, it can undo much of what a decent price earns. Net metering vs net billing explains the rules, and the family's next call should be to their utility.

Scenario 5: If they ran the dishwasher at noon

Self-consumption means using solar power in the home as it's made. In our example, 40% of the family's use happens while the sun is up. That share offsets power directly, at the full retail price. Everything else rides on the export credit.

The family can push that share up. Running the dishwasher, laundry or EV charging during the day all help. A house that sits empty all day with a large system does the opposite: it exports more and leans harder on the credit rule.

Scenario 6: If they bought a bigger system

The installer suggests filling the roof. Tempting, but our model caps export credits at a year of grid purchases. Once the system covers the family's yearly use, extra panels earn nothing more. They still cost money.

In Texas, the numbers look like this:

System size Cost Year-1 savings Payback
3 kW $10,500 $678 15.3 years
5 kW $17,500 $1,131 14.6 years
7 kW $24,500 $1,583 14.3 years
9 kW $31,500 $1,671 16.3 years
12 kW $42,000 $1,671 20.5 years

Beyond 7 kW, savings barely move while cost keeps climbing. California follows the same curve: 6.9 years at 5 kW, 7.4 years at 7 kW and 12 years at 12 kW.

Payback isn't the only lens. Over 25 years, the total gain in Texas peaks at 7 kW at $23,624, with 9 kW close behind at $23,062. Either way, the lesson for the family is to match the system to their yearly use, not to the size of the roof.

What the family does next

Armed with all this, the household runs its own estimate:

  1. Pull yearly kWh use from the bills.
  2. Find the price per kWh, including fees where possible.
  3. Ask the utility how it credits exported power.
  4. Get a written quote, then divide cost by estimated yearly savings.
  5. Enter the numbers in the solar calculator and compare with our state pages.

What would change the result

Every scenario above changed one thing at a time. Real homes change several at once.

  • Electricity price first, export credit second, and both usually move payback more than sunshine does. In our example it runs from 4.8 years in Hawaii to 19.6 years in Washington, and from 14.3 to 23.1 years in Texas depending on the credit.
  • System size past your yearly use adds cost without adding much savings.
  • Daytime use shifts how much of your output escapes the export rule.
  • Roof shade, direction, rate plans and future price changes can all push the figure up or down.

A single payback figure folds all six scenarios into one number. Before accepting an installer's, find out which price per kWh, which export credit and which system size it assumes, then rebuild it in the solar calculator with your own bill.
