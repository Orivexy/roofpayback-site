22:T785,<div class="short-answer"><h2 id="the-answer-up-front">The answer up front</h2>
<p>A solar payback period is the number of years before your bill savings add up to what you paid for the system.</p>
<p>Five things set that number: the system&#39;s cost, how much power it makes, what your electricity costs, what your utility pays for power you send back, and how much of your solar power you use yourself.</p>
<p>Of those, the price of a <a href="/guides/what-is-a-kilowatt-hour/">kilowatt-hour</a> usually matters most, and the export credit comes second. To show how, we followed one household through a series of &quot;what ifs.&quot; The same home pays back in 4.8 years in Hawaii and 19.6 years in Washington. Your own quote and your own utility&#39;s rules decide where you land.</p>
</div><h2 id="meet-the-household">Meet the household</h2>
<p>The story uses our standard example home, a family using 10,500 kWh a year with no incentives, with output from PVWatts for one reference city per state (<a href="/methodology/">methodology</a>).</p>
<p>Picture the family getting their first quote. The installer hands over a single payback figure. Six what-ifs sit behind it.</p>
<h2 id="scenario-1-the-price-tag">Scenario 1: The price tag</h2>
<p>The family&#39;s first lesson is that payback is simple division. Cost goes on top, yearly savings go on the bottom. Raise the price while savings stay put, and the wait gets longer.</p>
<p>When they compare quotes, the number to line up is cost per kW. A total price alone hides the system size.</p>
<p>They also learn there is no federal discount to subtract from the cost. The 30% residential credit ended for systems placed in service after December 31, 2025, so any help has to come from a state or utility program, which DSIRE (programs.dsireusa.org) tracks. More on this in <a href="/guides/is-solar-worth-it-without-tax-credit/">solar without the tax credit</a>.</p>

<p>Say the family lives in Houston. Nothing about the roof changes, yet payback moves from 14.3 years to 23.1 years purely on the credit rule. That is why the export rule ranks second only to price: where credits are low, it can undo much of what a decent price earns. <a href="/guides/net-metering-vs-net-billing-payback/">Net metering vs net billing</a> explains the rules, and the family&#39;s next call should be to their utility.</p>
<h2 id="scenario-5-if-they-ran-the-dishwasher-at-noon">Scenario 5: If they ran the dishwasher at noon</h2>
<p>Self-consumption means using solar power in the home as it&#39;s made. In our example, 40% of the family&#39;s use happens while the sun is up. That share offsets power directly, at the full retail price. Everything else rides on the export credit.</p>
<p>The family can push that share up. Running the dishwasher, laundry or EV charging during the day all help. A house that sits empty all day with a large system does the opposite: it exports more and leans harder on the credit rule.</p>

<p>Payback isn&#39;t the only lens. Over 25 years, the total gain in Texas peaks at 7 kW at $23,624, with 9 kW close behind at $23,062. Either way, the lesson for the family is to match the system to their yearly use, not to the size of the roof.</p>
<h2 id="what-the-family-does-next">What the family does next</h2>
<p>Armed with all this, the household runs its own estimate:</p>
<ol>
<li>Pull yearly kWh use from the bills.</li>
<li>Find the price per kWh, including fees where possible.</li>
<li>Ask the utility how it credits exported power.</li>
<li>Get a written quote, then divide cost by estimated yearly savings.</li>
<li>Enter the numbers in the <a href="/solar-calculator/">solar calculator</a> and compare with <a href="/solar-panels/">our state pages</a>.</li>
</ol>
<h2 id="what-would-change-the-result">What would change the result</h2>
<p>Every scenario above changed one thing at a time. Real homes change several at once.</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Electricity price first, export credit second,</strong> and both usually move payback more than sunshine does. In our example it runs from 4.8 years in Hawaii to 19.6 years in Washington, and from 14.3 to 23.1 years in Texas depending on the credit.</li>
<li><strong>System size</strong> past your yearly use adds cost without adding much savings.</li>
<li><strong>Daytime use</strong> shifts how much of your output escapes the export rule.</li>
<li><strong>Roof shade, direction, rate plans and future price changes</strong> can all push the figure up or down.</li>
</ul>
<p>A single payback figure folds all six scenarios into one number. Before accepting an installer&#39;s, find out which price per kWh, which export credit and which system size it assumes, then rebuild it in the <a href="/solar-calculator/">solar calculator</a> with your own bill.</p>
