23:T84d,<div class="short-answer"><h2 id="short-answer">Short answer</h2>
<p>Solar payback period is the number of years it takes for your bill savings to equal what you paid for the system. Five things set that number: what the system costs, how much it produces, what your electricity costs, what your utility pays for power you send back, and how much of your solar power you use yourself.</p>
<p>Price per kWh (kilowatt-hour, the unit your bill uses) matters most. In our example home, payback runs from 4.1 years in Hawaii to 17.2 years in Washington. Your own quote and your own utility rules decide where you land.</p>
</div><h2 id="the-example-home-we-use">The example home we use</h2>
<p>All numbers below come from one example home, so the comparisons are fair:</p>
<ul>
<li>7 kW system, installed cost $3000 per kW ($21000 total)</li>
<li>10500 kWh of use per year, with 40% of that use while the sun is up</li>
<li>No incentives, $100 per year maintenance</li>
<li>Electricity price rising 2.5% per year, panels losing 0.5% of output per year</li>
<li>25-year horizon</li>
<li>Export credits capped at a year of grid purchases (annual true-up)</li>
</ul>
<p>Output comes from PVWatts, a free solar estimator from the U.S. Department of Energy&#39;s National Laboratory of the Rockies (formerly NREL). We use a south-facing roof, 20° pitch and 14% system losses, with one reference city per state. See our <a href="/methodology/">methodology</a> for details.</p>
<h2 id="factor-1-cost">Factor 1: Cost</h2>
<p>Payback is simple division: cost divided by yearly savings. If the price goes up and savings stay the same, payback gets longer. Cost per kW is the number to compare when you read quotes.</p>
<p>Incentives change this a lot. The 30% federal Residential Clean Energy Credit (Section 25D) is not available for property placed in service after December 31, 2025. Our example uses no incentives. State and utility programs vary, and DSIRE (programs.dsireusa.org) keeps a database of current ones. Our guide on <a href="/guides/is-solar-worth-it-without-tax-credit/">solar without the tax credit</a> goes deeper.</p>

<h2 id="factor-4-export-credit">Factor 4: Export credit</h2>
<p>When your panels make more than your home uses, extra power goes to the grid. What you get for it depends on your utility. Under net metering, you often get a credit close to the retail price. Under net billing, the credit can be lower. Rules vary by state and utility.</p>

<h2 id="factor-5-self-consumption">Factor 5: Self-consumption</h2>
<p>Self-consumption means using your solar power in your home as it is made. In our example, 40% of use happens while the sun is up. That share is power you offset directly, at full retail price. The rest depends on export credits.</p>
<p>You can raise your share by running the dishwasher, laundry or EV charging during the day. A home that is empty all day, with a large system, exports more and depends more on the credit rule.</p>

<h2 id="how-to-estimate-your-own-payback">How to estimate your own payback</h2>
<ol>
<li>Get your yearly kWh use from your bill.</li>
<li>Find your price per kWh, including fees if you can.</li>
<li>Ask your utility how it credits exported power.</li>
<li>Get a written quote, then divide cost by estimated yearly savings.</li>
<li>Try the <a href="/solar-calculator/">solar calculator</a> with your numbers, and compare with <a href="/solar-panels/">our state pages</a>.</li>
</ol>
<p>Payback is only an estimate. Roof shade, direction, rate plans and future price changes can all move it. Treat any single number, ours included, as a starting point.</p>
<h2 id="bottom-line">Bottom line</h2>
<p>Payback is not set by sunshine alone. Electricity price and export credit often matter more than location. In our example, it runs from 4.1 years in Hawaii to 17.2 years in Washington, and from 12.5 to 20.5 years in Texas depending on the credit. Size the system to your use, ask your utility about export rules, and compare quotes per kW. Then run your own numbers in the <a href="/solar-calculator/">solar calculator</a>.</p>
