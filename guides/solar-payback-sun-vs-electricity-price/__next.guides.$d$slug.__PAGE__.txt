24:Ta80,<div class="short-answer"><h2 id="what-we-found">What we found</h2>
<p>Sunshine is not what sets solar payback in the U.S.; the price of electricity is. In the August 2026 numbers, Alaska produces the least solar power per kilowatt of any state (<strong>922 kWh per kW</strong> a year) yet pays back in <strong>12.1 years</strong>, faster than Arizona at 13.9 years. Maine, with much less sun than Nevada, pays back in 8 years against Nevada&#39;s 16.1.</p>
</div><h2 id="the-sunniest-states-are-not-the-fastest-to-pay-back">The sunniest states are not the fastest to pay back</h2>
<p>Start with output, meaning the kilowatt-hours (kWh, the unit your utility bills) one kilowatt of panels makes in a year. New Mexico leads at 1,779 kWh per kW, followed by Arizona at 1,755 and Nevada at 1,753. Their paybacks run from 12.8 years in New Mexico to 16.1 years in Nevada.</p>
<p>Nevada is the clearest case. Its panels out-produce nearly every other state&#39;s, but its residential price is <strong>12.72¢ per kWh</strong>, among the lowest in the table. Each kWh the roof makes replaces a cheap kWh from the utility, so the savings stay small: $1,336 in the first year.</p>
<h2 id="the-priciest-states-win-with-far-less-sun">The priciest states win with far less sun</h2>
<p>Flip the view and sort by what the utility charges. Hawaii (47.18¢), Maine (32.11¢) and Massachusetts (30.48¢) all pay back in under nine years, and California (29.75¢) takes 7.4 years. None of the three mainland states makes as much power per kW as Arizona.</p>
<p>Maine makes 1,319 kWh per kW and still returns <strong>$2,964</strong> in year-one savings, against $1,580 in Arizona. Price multiplies every kWh the roof produces; sun only changes how many there are.</p>

37:Tc50,<h2 id="at-a-similar-price-sun-still-matters">At a similar price, sun still matters</h2>
<p>Price does not make sunshine irrelevant. Compare states that charge about the same. Arizona (15.05¢) and Washington (14.63¢) sit close together on price, yet Arizona pays back in 13.9 years and Washington in <strong>19.6 years</strong>, because Seattle&#39;s reference output is 1,088 kWh per kW against Phoenix&#39;s 1,755.</p>
<p>Georgia and Texas tell a quieter version of the same story. At 15.90¢ and 15.91¢, they land at 14.5 and 14.3 years. When prices are nearly equal, the extra output decides the order, but the gap is a few tenths of a year, not a few years.</p>
<h2 id="alaska-shows-how-far-price-can-carry-a-weak-solar-resource">Alaska shows how far price can carry a weak solar resource</h2>
<p>Alaska is the stress test. Anchorage&#39;s 922 kWh per kW is the lowest figure for any state, and a rooftop there would produce far less than one in Phoenix. Still, at 29.41¢ per kWh the example home saves $1,898 in year one, more than New Mexico or Arizona.</p>
<p>That does not mean every Alaska roof is a bargain. Snow cover, orientation and the utility&#39;s export rules all change real results, and the <a href="/solar-panels/alaska/">Alaska solar page</a> shows the full assumptions. It does show that a low-sun label alone should not rule a state out.</p>
<h2 id="how-we-measured-it">How we measured it</h2>
<p>Production comes from PVWatts v8, the free solar estimator from the U.S. Department of Energy&#39;s National Laboratory of the Rockies, for a south-facing roof at 20° pitch with 14% losses and one reference city per state. Prices are the EIA&#39;s August 2026 average residential price, which includes taxes and fees.</p>
<p>The example home is a 7 kW system costing $24,500 with 10,500 kWh of yearly use and no incentives; the <a href="/methodology/">methodology</a> page lists the rest. &quot;Full credit&quot; means the utility credits every exported kWh at the retail price. Payback is the number of years until bill savings cover the cost.</p>
<h2 id="what-this-means-for-your-bill">What this means for your bill</h2>
<p>Your own utility rate is the better predictor than your state&#39;s sunshine rating. Look at a recent bill, find the price per kWh, and compare it with the state average in the <a href="/electricity-prices/">electricity price tables</a>. If the figure is well above 20¢, solar economics are strong even in a gray climate; if it is near 13¢, even a desert roof takes well over a decade.</p>
<p>Then run your own numbers in the <a href="/solar-calculator/">solar calculator</a>. Check <a href="/solar-panels/nevada/">Nevada</a> and <a href="/solar-panels/maine/">Maine</a> side by side to see the effect first-hand. For the wider ranking, see the <a href="/guides/best-states-for-solar-payback/">best states for solar payback</a>, and for low-sun regions, the guide on <a href="/guides/cloudy-states-solar-worth-it/">cloudy states</a>.</p>
<p>Quotes should state how exported power is credited, because the payback figures above assume full credit and the answer drops if your utility pays less.</p>
