33:T128d,<div class="short-answer"><p class="short-answer-label">Plain-English answer</p>
<p>Power costs more in some states because making it and delivering it costs more there. The U.S. Energy Information Administration (EIA) puts it this way: prices vary by place &quot;based on the availability of power plants and fuels, local fuel costs, and pricing regulations.&quot; Weather and the cost of the grid add to the mix.</p>
<p>The difference is not small. In September 2026, the average home in Hawaii paid 48.31¢ per <a href="/guides/what-is-a-kilowatt-hour/">kilowatt-hour</a> (kWh), and the average home in Nevada paid 13.25¢. And the gap is not a passing quirk: the states that were expensive ten years ago are, with few exceptions, the states that are expensive now.</p>
</div><h2 id="how-it-works">How it works</h2>
<p>Think of your electricity rate as a bundle of three costs. EIA says prices &quot;generally reflect the cost to build, finance, maintain, and operate power plants and the electricity grid.&quot;</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Making the power.</strong> &quot;The cost of generating electricity is the largest component of the price of electricity,&quot; according to EIA. Fuel and the plants themselves sit here.</li>
<li><strong>Moving the power.</strong> Transmission and distribution lines carry power from plants to homes, and they cost money to build, run and repair, including after storms.</li>
<li><strong>Running the utility.</strong> Billing and customer service, plus, for some for-profit utilities, a return for owners and shareholders, as EIA notes.</li>
</ul>
<p>Five forces push those costs up or down from state to state.</p>
<h3>Fuel</h3>
<p>EIA explains that fuel prices, &quot;especially for natural gas and petroleum fuels (mainly in Hawaii and villages in Alaska), may increase during periods of high electricity demand.&quot; Hawaii shows the effect most clearly: EIA says prices there are high &quot;mainly because most of its electricity is generated with petroleum fuels that must be imported into the state.&quot;</p>
<h3>Water and wind</h3>
<p>&quot;Rain and snow provide water for low-cost hydropower generation, and wind can provide low-cost electricity generation when wind speeds are favorable,&quot; EIA writes. Droughts or calm winds work the other way, because other sources must fill the gap.</p>
<h3>Poles, wires and substations</h3>
<p>Every state pays for its grid. EIA lists repairing damage from accidents or extreme weather and improving cybersecurity among those costs.</p>
<h3>Weather and demand</h3>
<p>&quot;Extreme temperatures can increase demand for heating and cooling,&quot; EIA says, and higher demand can raise fuel and electricity prices. Prices usually peak in summer, when more expensive power plants come online.</p>
<h3>State rules</h3>
<p>Per EIA: &quot;In some states, public service (utility) commissions fully regulate prices, and other states have a combination of unregulated prices (for generators) and regulated prices (for transmission and distribution).&quot; In some of the second group, households can pick who supplies their power; <a href="/guides/electricity-choice-states-explained/">electricity choice states explained</a> lists where.</p>
<h2 id="the-ranking-is-persistent-and-the-gap-has-widened">The ranking is persistent, and the gap has widened</h2>
<p>Most of those forces change slowly: a state&#39;s fuel supply, its rivers, its distance from other grids. So the ranking should be stable, and EIA-861M data confirm it. We compared each state&#39;s average residential price in calendar 2016 with its average over the twelve months to September 2026, both weighted by the kWh homes bought each month.</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>The top stays the top.</strong> Nine of the ten most expensive places in 2016 are still in the top ten: Hawaii, Alaska, Connecticut, Massachusetts, Rhode Island, New Hampshire, New York, California and Maine. Vermont dropped out and the District of Columbia moved in.</li>
<li><strong>The bottom mostly stays the bottom.</strong> Seven of the ten cheapest states in 2016 are still in the bottom ten: Kentucky, Tennessee, Oklahoma, North Dakota, Idaho, Arkansas and Louisiana.</li>
<li><strong>The gap grew in cents and in ratio.</strong> In 2016, Hawaii (27.48¢) paid 2.94 times the cheapest state, Louisiana (9.34¢), a gap of 18.14¢. Over the latest twelve months, Hawaii (45.05¢) paid 3.64 times the cheapest, North Dakota (12.38¢), a gap of 32.66¢. On the mainland alone, the ratio rose from 2.17 (Alaska against Louisiana) to 2.6 (California against North Dakota).</li>
</ul>
<p>Ranking the 51 states from cheapest to most expensive in both periods gives a rank correlation of 0.9, where 1 would mean an identical order.</p>

<h2 id="where-people-get-confused">Where people get confused</h2>
<p><strong>&quot;A cheap state means a cheap bill.&quot;</strong> Not always. Louisiana has one of the lowest prices but an average bill near the national one, because its homes use the most power; see <a href="/guides/average-electric-bill-by-state/">average electric bill by state</a>.</p>
<p><strong>&quot;One month&#39;s price is the yearly price.&quot;</strong> Prices move with the seasons, and by different amounts in different states. Hawaii&#39;s monthly price ranged from 39.74¢ to 52.72¢ in the twelve months to September 2026. <a href="/guides/cheapest-and-most-expensive-states-for-electricity/">Cheapest and most expensive states</a> ranks states on a full year for that reason.</p>
<p><strong>&quot;Businesses always pay more than homes.&quot;</strong> It is the other way around. In every EIA region in September 2026, households paid the most per kWh and industry the least; <a href="/guides/residential-vs-business-electricity-prices/">residential vs. business electricity prices</a> ranks the states.</p>
<p><strong>&quot;Rankings change every month.&quot;</strong> The September 2026 report shows big one-year moves (Hawaii up 22.2%, Connecticut down 10.9%), but over ten years the order has barely changed. Our <a href="/guides/electricity-prices-2026-09/">September 2026 electricity price report</a> has the latest month in full.</p>
<p><strong>&quot;High prices automatically make solar a winner.&quot;</strong> Every kWh you avoid buying is worth more where power is expensive, so price weighs heavily on solar payback. The other half is how your utility credits power sent to the grid, which in California and Hawaii is now well below the retail price for new customers. See <a href="/guides/net-metering-vs-net-billing-payback/">net metering vs. net billing payback</a> and test your case in the <a href="/solar-calculator/">solar calculator</a>; the <a href="/methodology/">methodology</a> page lists its assumptions.</p>
<h2 id="glossary">Glossary</h2>
<ul>
<li><strong>Kilowatt-hour (kWh):</strong> the unit your bill counts.</li>
<li><strong>Grid:</strong> the transmission and distribution lines that carry power from plants to buildings.</li>
<li><strong>Generation:</strong> making electricity at a power plant; EIA calls it the largest part of the price.</li>
<li><strong>Public service (utility) commission:</strong> the state body that, in some states, fully regulates utility prices.</li>
<li><strong>Preliminary data:</strong> recent EIA figures that may still be revised.</li>
<li><strong>Rank correlation:</strong> a measure from −1 to 1 of how similar two orderings are.</li>
</ul>
<h2 id="why-the-gap-persists">Why the gap persists</h2>
<p>The forces behind a state&#39;s price, fuel, geography, the grid and the rules, change slowly, so the expensive states of 2016 are still the expensive states today, and the distance between top and bottom has grown. If your rate looks high, the cause is usually upstream of your house. Find your state on the <a href="/electricity-prices/">electricity prices</a> page and compare it with your own bill.</p>
