38:T1240,<div class="short-answer"><h2 id="plain-english-answer">Plain-English answer</h2>
<p>Power costs more in some states because making it and delivering it costs more there. The U.S. Energy Information Administration (EIA) puts it this way: prices vary by place &quot;based on the availability of power plants and fuels, local fuel costs, and pricing regulations.&quot; Weather and the cost of the grid add to the mix.</p>
<p>The difference is not small. In August 2026, the average home in Hawaii paid 47.18¢ per <a href="/guides/what-is-a-kilowatt-hour/">kilowatt-hour</a> (kWh). In Nevada, the average home paid 12.72¢ in the same month.</p>
<p>Both figures are EIA state averages. What you pay depends on your own utility and plan.</p>
</div><h2 id="how-it-works">How it works</h2>
<p>Think of your electricity rate as a bundle of three costs. EIA says prices &quot;generally reflect the cost to build, finance, maintain, and operate power plants and the electricity grid.&quot;</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Making the power.</strong> &quot;The cost of generating electricity is the largest component of the price of electricity,&quot; according to EIA. Fuel and the plants themselves sit here.</li>
<li><strong>Moving the power.</strong> The grid is the network of transmission and distribution lines that carries power from plants to homes. Those lines cost money to build, run and fix, including after storms and accidents.</li>
<li><strong>Running the utility.</strong> Billing and customer service, plus, for some for-profit utilities, a return for owners and shareholders, as EIA notes.</li>
</ul>
<p>When any part of that bundle costs more in one state, home prices there tend to be higher. Five forces push those parts up or down.</p>
<h3>Fuel</h3>
<p>What the plants burn matters a lot. EIA explains that fuel prices, &quot;especially for natural gas and petroleum fuels (mainly in Hawaii and villages in Alaska), may increase during periods of high electricity demand.&quot; Higher fuel costs mean higher generating costs.</p>
<p>Hawaii shows this most clearly. EIA says prices there are high &quot;mainly because most of its electricity is generated with petroleum fuels that must be imported into the state.&quot; Its price reached 47.18¢ in August 2026, up from 38.53¢ a year earlier. Alaska, at 29.41¢, also has villages that rely on petroleum fuels.</p>
<h3>Water and wind</h3>
<p>Some states are lucky with natural resources. &quot;Rain and snow provide water for low-cost hydropower generation, and wind can provide low-cost electricity generation when wind speeds are favorable,&quot; EIA writes. The reverse also holds: droughts or calm winds can raise prices because other sources must fill the gap.</p>
<h3>Poles, wires and substations</h3>
<p>Every state pays for its grid. EIA lists repairing damage from accidents or extreme weather and improving cybersecurity among those costs. Where the grid is pricier to build and maintain, the cost lands on home bills.</p>
<h3>Weather and demand</h3>
<p>&quot;Extreme temperatures can increase demand for heating and cooling,&quot; EIA says, and higher demand can raise fuel and electricity prices. Prices usually peak in summer, when more expensive power plants come online to meet demand.</p>
<h3>State rules</h3>
<p>Each state chooses how prices get set. Per EIA: &quot;In some states, public service (utility) commissions fully regulate prices, and other states have a combination of unregulated prices (for generators) and regulated prices (for transmission and distribution).&quot; In some of the second group, households can pick who supplies their power; <a href="/guides/electricity-choice-states-explained/">electricity choice states explained</a> covers how that works.</p>
<h2 id="the-grocery-store-comparison">The grocery store comparison</h2>
<p>Picture the same loaf of bread sold in two towns. In one town the bakery sits next to a wheat farm. In the other, flour arrives by ship, and the roads are long and costly to maintain. Same bread, different price.</p>
<p>Electricity works the same way. Hawaii is the town whose flour comes by ship: most of its power is made with imported petroleum. A state with plenty of hydropower or steady wind is the town next to the farm.</p>
<p>The roads in this picture are the grid. A house is like a small corner shop at the end of a long route. A factory is like a big warehouse that takes deliveries by the truckload. Serving many small stops costs more per item. EIA says the same about power: &quot;Retail electricity prices are usually highest for residential and commercial consumers because it costs more to distribute electricity to them.&quot;</p>

<p>Neighbors tend to look alike. EIA groups states into census divisions, and in August 2026 the highest residential averages were in Pacific Noncontiguous (Alaska and Hawaii) at 40.79¢, New England at 28.31¢, Pacific Contiguous at 25.59¢ and Middle Atlantic at 25.17¢. <h2 id="where-people-get-confused">Where people get confused</h2>
<p><strong>&quot;A cheap state means a cheap bill.&quot;</strong> Not always. The price per kWh is an average across a whole state. Your utility and rate plan set what you actually pay.</p>
<p><strong>&quot;August prices are the yearly price.&quot;</strong> August is a summer month, when prices are usually highest because more expensive plants are added to meet demand. August figures can run higher than a full-year average.</p>
<p><strong>&quot;Businesses always pay more than homes.&quot;</strong> Homes paid the most in every region in August 2026. Industry usually pays the least, but there was one exception: in Alaska and Hawaii combined, the industrial price (34.69¢) beat the commercial price (34.46¢). Everywhere else, businesses paid more than industry.</p>
<p><strong>&quot;Rankings stay put.&quot;</strong> They move. Between August 2025 and August 2026, Hawaii rose 22.5%. Maine rose 14.1%, and New Hampshire rose 13.1%, to 27.64¢. Connecticut fell 18.8%, to 24.51¢, and California fell 3.8%. Our <a href="/guides/electricity-prices-2026-08/">August 2026 electricity price guide</a> has the latest month in full.</p>
<p><strong>&quot;High prices automatically make solar a winner.&quot;</strong> Every kWh you avoid buying is worth more when power is pricey, so the price level is the first thing that decides rooftop solar payback. The second is your utility&#39;s credit rule for power you send back to the grid, and where that credit is low it can override a high price. Net metering rules vary by state and utility; the <a href="https://programs.dsireusa.org/">DSIRE database</a> lists current programs.</p>
<p>To run your own case, the <a href="/solar-calculator/">solar calculator</a> combines your state&#39;s EIA price with a PVWatts production estimate; the <a href="/methodology/">methodology</a> page lists its assumptions.</p>
<h2 id="glossary">Glossary</h2>
<ul>
<li><strong>Kilowatt-hour (kWh):</strong> The unit your bill counts (<a href="/guides/what-is-a-kilowatt-hour/">explained here</a>).</li>
<li><strong>Grid:</strong> The transmission and distribution lines that carry power from plants to buildings.</li>
<li><strong>Generation:</strong> Making electricity at a power plant. EIA calls it the largest part of the price.</li>
<li><strong>Public service (utility) commission:</strong> The state body that, in some states, fully regulates utility prices.</li>
<li><strong>Census division:</strong> A group of neighboring states EIA uses to report regional averages.</li>
<li><strong>Preliminary data:</strong> Recent EIA figures that may still be revised.</li>
<li><strong>Net metering:</strong> A program where your utility credits the power your solar panels send back to the grid.</li>
</ul>
<h2 id="the-short-version">The short version</h2>
<p>Prices differ because the costs behind them differ: fuel, power plants, the grid, weather and state rules. Hawaii pays the most, largely because it burns imported petroleum fuels. Nevada, Louisiana and Utah were among the cheapest in August 2026.</p>
<p>If your rate looks high, the cause is usually upstream of your house: the fuel your region burns, the grid it maintains and the rules your state sets. Find your state on the <a href="/electricity-prices/">electricity prices</a> page, then compare it with your own bill to see how far your utility sits from the average.</p>
