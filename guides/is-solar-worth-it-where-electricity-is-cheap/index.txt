33:T1394,<div class="short-answer"><p class="short-answer-label">The verdict</p>
<p>It can be, but only with a good price per watt. Six states had an average residential price under 14¢ per kWh in September 2026: Nevada, Tennessee, Louisiana, Utah, Kentucky and Nebraska. At $3.50 per watt and full export credit, our example system pays back in 15.5 to 17.8 years there. At half credit it takes 19.8 to 23.2 years.</p>
<p>Cheap power is the one thing a homeowner cannot change. The two levers that can move the answer are the installed price and how much of the output you use yourself. In these six states, getting to a 12-year payback takes a price of roughly $2.20 to $2.60 per watt with full credit, and about $1.55 to $1.95 with half credit.</p>

42:T8e0,<h2 id="lever-2-use-more-of-the-power-yourself">Lever 2: use more of the power yourself</h2>
<p>A kWh used as the panels make it saves the full retail price; an exported kWh earns the credit. Where exports earn half of retail, raising the daytime share from our 40% assumption to 60% shortens payback by about two to three years: Nevada from 19.8 to 17.7 years, Louisiana from 21.9 to 19.4, Kentucky from 22.7 to 20.1. Under full credit, timing makes no difference. <a href="/guides/solar-daytime-electricity-use/">Solar and daytime electricity use</a> works through how to shift loads.</p>
<p>The same logic argues for a smaller system. With weak export credit, a system sized to your daytime use exports less of its output; <a href="/guides/how-many-solar-panels-do-i-need/">how many solar panels do I need</a> shows that at half credit the smallest systems pay back first.</p>
<h2 id="what-the-state-average-hides">What the state average hides</h2>
<p>EIA prices are utility revenue divided by kWh sold, so they include fixed charges, taxes and fees spread across every kWh. Your volumetric rate, the part solar can offset, may be lower than the state figure, and a fixed monthly customer charge cannot be reduced by panels at all. Read the per-kWh charges on a recent bill and use that number. <a href="/guides/fixed-charges-on-your-electric-bill/">Fixed charges on your electric bill</a> explains the difference.</p>
<h2 id="four-numbers-that-settle-a-slow-payback">Four numbers that settle a slow payback</h2>
<ol>
<li><strong>Your per-kWh rate</strong> from a recent bill, not the state average.</li>
<li><strong>Your export credit</strong>, from the utility tariff.</li>
<li><strong>Your quote in dollars per watt</strong>, compared with the targets in the table above.</li>
<li><strong>Your daytime share</strong>, from smart-meter data.</li>
</ol>
<p>Put them in the <a href="/solar-calculator/">solar calculator</a>. If the result clears 15 years only at full credit and a price well below $3 per watt, cheaper ways to cut the bill may pay back faster; <a href="/guides/how-to-lower-your-electric-bill/">how to lower your electric bill</a> lists them. If it clears 15 years at half credit with a quote you actually have, cheap power is no reason to say no.</p>
