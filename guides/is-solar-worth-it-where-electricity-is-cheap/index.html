Is Solar Worth It Where Electricity Is Cheap?

Low electricity prices mean slower solar payback. See eight cheap-power states, how sun and export credits change the math, and how to check your own numbers.

Published November 14, 2026·Updated November 18, 2026·5 min read·RoofPayback editorial·Figures checked against EIA and PVWatts data

The verdict

Solar is not off the table in cheap-power states. It is a slow bet.

In the low-price states we modeled, the example system pays back in roughly 16 to 18 years with full export credit. Net billing rules that pay half or a quarter of retail for exported power push that out further. Your own rate, your roof and the credit your utility pays for extra power decide whether the wait is worth it.

Four assumptions tend to make the picture look rosier, or bleaker, than the numbers support.

Myth: Panels save the same amount no matter where you live

Fact: Each kilowatt-hour your panels make saves whatever your utility would have charged for it. When that price is low, every kWh saves less, and the system takes longer to earn back its cost.

The spread across states is wide. According to the EIA, the U.S. average residential price was 18.16¢ per kWh in August 2026. Louisiana sat at 12.87¢ and Nevada at 12.72¢, while California was at 29.75¢. Identical panels on identical roofs would save very different amounts in those places. The full state list is on our electricity prices page, and the cheapest and most expensive states guide ranks them.

Eight states had an August 2026 residential price under 14 cents. "Output per kW" is the yearly production from each kilowatt of panels in the state's reference city. Paybacks use our 7 kW example home with no incentives (methodology).

Louisiana (New Orleans) 12.87¢/kWh 1,479 kWh 16.7 years 22.5 years $1,332

Utah (Salt Lake City) 13.31¢/kWh 1,484 kWh 16.2 years 21.9 years $1,382
Idaho (Boise) 13.37¢/kWh 1,456 kWh 16.4 years 22 years $1,363

Full-credit payback in this group runs from 16.1 to 18.3 years. None of them is quick.

Myth: Plenty of sun makes up for cheap power

Fact: Sun helps, but it can't fully rescue a low price.

Nevada is the obvious test. Las Vegas produces 1,753 kWh per kW, the most in the table. It has the fastest payback in the group, but only by a little. Its full-credit payback is 16.1 years, just ahead of Utah at 16.2 years, and its year-1 savings of $1,336 trail Utah's $1,382. Louisiana gets less sun at a similar price and lands at $1,332 in year-1 savings.

The reverse case is just as telling. Washington (Seattle) has a price of 14.63¢, higher than every state in the table. Its output, though, is only 1,088 kWh per kW. Full-credit payback there is 19.6 years, with year-1 savings of $1,114. The higher price didn't make up for weaker production.

Payback rests on two things at once: what each kWh is worth, and how many kWh the roof makes. In a cheap-power state, both need to be decent.

Myth: The export credit is a detail you can sort out later

Fact: Price decides first, and here it has already set a long baseline. The export rule comes second, and in cheap-power states it can make or break the case.

Many utilities pay less for power you send to the grid than the retail price you pay for power you buy. Our model tests full, half and quarter credit, and the drop in low-price states is steep.

  • Louisiana: 16.7 years at full credit, 22.5 years at half.
  • Tennessee: 17.6 years at full credit, 23.1 years at half.
  • Louisiana, Idaho, Utah and others: at quarter credit, the system does not pay back within 25 years in our model.

Our guide on net metering versus net billing explains how these rules work. DSIRE lists current programs by state.

One lever is in your hands. Power you use while the sun is up avoids the retail price instead of earning a reduced export credit. Shifting use into daylight hours helps, as covered in solar and daytime electricity use.

Myth: The state average tells you your payback

Fact: Averages hide a lot. EIA prices are utility revenue divided by kWh sold, so they fold in taxes and fees. Your actual rate may be higher or lower than your state's figure.

Some utilities also charge a fixed monthly fee. Solar can't reduce that part of the bill, no matter how many panels you install.

Incentives cut the other way. Our example assumes none, and the federal 30% residential credit is no longer available for systems placed in service after December 31, 2025. A state, city or utility rebate, where one exists, would shorten every payback in the table; solar without the tax credit explains where to look.

The quote matters too. Our figures assume $3,500 per kW. In a low-price state, where savings arrive slowly, each dollar per watt shaved off the price buys back more years than it would in California. The guide to solar panel cost by state shows how cost feeds into payback.

If solar looks slow, other levers still work

  • Review your rate plan. Some utilities offer cheaper off-peak rates. See time-of-use rates explained.
  • Go after the biggest loads. How to lower your electric bill lists the usual suspects.
  • Collect several quotes. Price per watt varies widely, and it carries extra weight where power is cheap.

What actually decides it

Cheap electricity is good for your monthly bill. It also turns solar into a long-term bet, where small differences carry big weight.

Four things settle it:

  1. Your real rate, taken from a recent bill, not the state average.
  2. Your utility's export credit. Ask how exported power is credited, and get the answer in writing.
  3. Your quote, measured in price per watt.
  4. Your roof's output, which PVWatts can estimate for your address (our settings are on the methodology page).

Put your numbers into the solar calculator and test the full, half and quarter credit cases. If the result only clears 25 years at full credit, your utility's export rule is the deciding question; if it clears comfortably at half credit too, a cheap-power state is no reason to say no.
