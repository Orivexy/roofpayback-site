Is Solar Worth It Where Electricity Is Cheap?

In the six states under 14¢ per kWh, solar takes 15 to 18 years at $3.50 a watt. See the price per watt that gets it to 12 years, and what daytime use adds.

Published November 14, 2026·Updated November 28, 2026·5 min read·RoofPayback editorial·Figures checked against EIA and PVWatts data

Official data: EIA · DOE · IRS · how we check

The verdict

It can be, but only with a good price per watt. Six states had an average residential price under 14¢ per kWh in September 2026: Nevada, Tennessee, Louisiana, Utah, Kentucky and Nebraska. At $3.50 per watt and full export credit, our example system pays back in 15.5 to 17.8 years there. At half credit it takes 19.8 to 23.2 years.

Cheap power is the one thing a homeowner cannot change. The two levers that can move the answer are the installed price and how much of the output you use yourself. In these six states, getting to a 12-year payback takes a price of roughly $2.20 to $2.60 per watt with full credit, and about $1.55 to $1.95 with half credit.

The six states at today's prices

Paybacks use our example home: 7 kW, 10,500 kWh a year, no incentives, prices rising 2.5% a year, with PVWatts output for one reference city per state (methodology).

With prices this close, output sorts the list: Las Vegas makes about 30% more per kW than Nashville and pays back 2.3 years sooner.

Which column applies is a state and utility question. Two of the six have official rules that put new systems below full credit:

  • Louisiana: for systems installed after December 31, 2019, the Public Service Commission credits exported energy "at Avoided Cost," while all power bought from the utility is paid at the full retail rate. If those rules cover your utility, read the quarter-credit column, or longer.
  • Kentucky: under the net metering law in effect since January 1, 2020, customers "will receive dollar credits at a compensation rate set by the PSC in rate proceedings for each utility," according to the Public Service Commission. The rate depends on your utility's latest case.

In the other four, ask your utility or check its tariff with the state utility commission. Net metering vs. net billing explains the arrangements.

Lever 1: the price per watt

The example uses $3.50 per watt. Berkeley Lab's 2026 Data Update put the median for host-owned home systems installed in 2025 at $3.6 per watt, and the median for cash purchases at $3.0, with about a quarter of the 100 largest installers below $3 (2025 dollars, before incentives).

Each 50 cents per watt is worth about two years of payback at full credit here, more than in a high-price state, because savings arrive slowly. A price at the Berkeley Lab cash median brings these states to 13.7 to 15.6 years with full credit. With half credit, even $2.50 leaves them at 15 to 18 years.

Lever 2: use more of the power yourself

A kWh used as the panels make it saves the full retail price; an exported kWh earns the credit. Where exports earn half of retail, raising the daytime share from our 40% assumption to 60% shortens payback by about two to three years: Nevada from 19.8 to 17.7 years, Louisiana from 21.9 to 19.4, Kentucky from 22.7 to 20.1. Under full credit, timing makes no difference. Solar and daytime electricity use works through how to shift loads.

The same logic argues for a smaller system. With weak export credit, a system sized to your daytime use exports less of its output; how many solar panels do I need shows that at half credit the smallest systems pay back first.

What the state average hides

EIA prices are utility revenue divided by kWh sold, so they include fixed charges, taxes and fees spread across every kWh. Your volumetric rate, the part solar can offset, may be lower than the state figure, and a fixed monthly customer charge cannot be reduced by panels at all. Read the per-kWh charges on a recent bill and use that number. Fixed charges on your electric bill explains the difference.

Four numbers that settle a slow payback

  1. Your per-kWh rate from a recent bill, not the state average.
  2. Your export credit, from the utility tariff.
  3. Your quote in dollars per watt, compared with the targets in the table above.
  4. Your daytime share, from smart-meter data.

Put them in the solar calculator. If the result clears 15 years only at full credit and a price well below $3 per watt, cheaper ways to cut the bill may pay back faster; how to lower your electric bill lists them. If it clears 15 years at half credit with a quote you actually have, cheap power is no reason to say no.
