23:Tb56,<div class="short-answer"><h2 id="short-answer">Short answer</h2>
<p>Solar can still work where electricity is cheap, but the payback is slow. In the low-price states in our model, the example system with full export credit pays back in roughly 16 to 18 years, and that is before any quarter-credit or half-credit net billing rules make it longer. Whether it makes sense depends on your own rate, your roof, and the credit your utility pays for extra power.</p>
</div><h2 id="why-a-low-price-slows-everything-down">Why a low price slows everything down</h2>
<p>A solar panel saves you the price of each kilowatt-hour (kWh) it produces. A kWh is the unit your utility bills you for. When that price is low, each kWh saves less, so the system takes longer to earn back its cost.</p>
<p>The U.S. average residential price was 18.16¢ per kWh in August 2026, according to the EIA. Louisiana was at 12.87¢ and Nevada at 12.72¢. California was at 29.75¢. Same panels, very different savings. See the full state list on our <a href="/electricity-prices/">electricity prices page</a>.</p>

37:T10f4,<h2 id="sun-does-not-rescue-a-low-price">Sun does not rescue a low price</h2>
<p>You might expect Nevada to look great, since Las Vegas produces 1753 kWh per kW. It does lead this group, but only by a small margin. Its payback with full credit is 16.1 years, and the year-1 savings are $1336. That is close to Louisiana, which gets less sun but has a similar price.</p>
<p>Now look the other way. Washington (Seattle) has a price of 14.63¢, higher than every state in the table. Yet its output is only 1088 kWh per kW, and the full-credit payback is 19.6 years. Year-1 savings are $1114. A higher price did not make up for the weaker production.</p>
<p>So two things set the payback: what each kWh is worth, and how many kWh the roof makes. In a cheap-power state, you need both to be decent.</p>
<h2 id="the-export-credit-matters-more-here">The export credit matters more here</h2>
<p>Many utilities pay less for the power you send to the grid than the retail price you pay. Our model tests full, half and quarter credit. In cheap-power states, the drop is steep.</p>
<p>In Louisiana, the payback goes from 16.7 years with full credit to 22.5 years with half credit. In Tennessee it goes from 17.6 to 23.1 years. For several states, including Louisiana, Idaho and Utah, the quarter-credit case does not pay back within 25 years in our model. Our guide on <a href="/guides/net-metering-vs-net-billing-payback/">net metering versus net billing</a> explains how these rules work, and <a href="https://programs.dsireusa.org/">DSIRE</a> lists current programs by state.</p>
<p>Using more of your solar power while the sun is up helps, because that power avoids the retail price instead of earning an export credit. Read more in <a href="/guides/solar-daytime-electricity-use/">solar and daytime electricity use</a>.</p>
<h2 id="what-changed-with-the-tax-credit">What changed with the tax credit</h2>
<p>The 30% federal Residential Clean Energy Credit (Section 25D) is not available for property placed in service after December 31, 2025, according to the IRS. Our example home assumes no incentives at all. If your state, city or utility offers a rebate or a special rate, the picture could improve. Our guide on <a href="/guides/is-solar-worth-it-without-tax-credit/">solar without the tax credit</a> goes through the details.</p>
<h2 id="ways-to-cut-a-bill-when-solar-is-a-slow-fit">Ways to cut a bill when solar is a slow fit</h2>
<p>If your payback looks long, you have other levers.</p>
<ul>
<li>Check your rate plan. Some utilities offer cheaper off-peak rates. See <a href="/guides/time-of-use-electricity-rates-explained/">time-of-use rates explained</a>.</li>
<li>Go after the biggest loads first. Our guide on <a href="/guides/how-to-lower-your-electric-bill/">how to lower your electric bill</a> lists the usual suspects.</li>
<li>Get quotes. Berkeley Lab reports a median installed price of $4.0 per watt for host-owned residential systems installed in 2024, with a 20th to 80th percentile range of $3.0 to $5.2 per watt. A lower quote in a cheap-power state can shorten the payback a lot. Our guide on <a href="/guides/solar-cost-per-watt-payback/">solar cost per watt and payback</a> shows how.</li>
</ul>
<h2 id="how-to-check-your-own-numbers">How to check your own numbers</h2>
<p>State averages hide a lot. EIA prices are utility revenue divided by kWh sold, so they include taxes and fees. Your actual rate may be higher or lower, and some utilities charge a fixed monthly fee that solar cannot reduce.</p>
<p>Pull a recent bill and find your price per kWh. Then run it through the <a href="/solar-calculator/">solar calculator</a>. Ask any installer how your utility credits exported power, and get that answer in writing. If you want a free production estimate, <a href="https://pvwatts.nlr.gov/">PVWatts</a> from the U.S. Department of Energy&#39;s National Laboratory of the Rockies is a good place to start.</p>
<h2 id="bottom-line">Bottom line</h2>
<p>Cheap electricity is good news for your monthly bill, but it makes solar a long-term bet. In the states above, the full-credit payback runs from 16.1 to 18.3 years, and the half-credit case is longer. Solar is not ruled out, but a low quote, a good roof and a fair export credit matter more here than anywhere else. Run your own numbers before you sign anything.</p>
