23:Tb07,<div class="short-answer"><h2 id="short-answer">Short answer</h2>
<p>The U.S. average residential electricity price was 18.16¢ per kilowatt-hour (kWh) in August 2026, according to the U.S. Energy Information Administration (EIA). A year earlier it was 17.57¢, a change of 3.4%. A kWh is the unit your utility bills you for; see our guide on <a href="/guides/what-is-a-kilowatt-hour/">what a kilowatt-hour is</a> if the term is new.</p>
<p>EIA&#39;s newest month is preliminary, so these figures can be revised. Your own rate depends on your utility and plan. Use the numbers below as a map, not as a quote for your bill.</p>

<p>The Great Lakes area (East North Central) rose the most among the mainland divisions. New England was the only division to fall. Why does the map look like this? Fuel mix, power-line costs and local rules all play a part, as explained in <a href="/guides/why-electricity-prices-differ-by-state/">why electricity prices differ by state</a> and <a href="/guides/electricity-prices-by-region/">electricity prices by region</a>.</p>
<h2 id="where-prices-are-highest-and-lowest">Where prices are highest and lowest</h2>
<p>At the top, Hawaii (47.18¢), Maine (32.11¢), California (29.75¢), Alaska (29.41¢) and New York (28.65¢) stand out. At the bottom are Nevada (12.72¢), Louisiana (12.87¢), North Dakota (13.31¢), Utah (13.31¢) and Idaho (13.37¢). Our guide to the <a href="/guides/cheapest-and-most-expensive-states-for-electricity/">cheapest and most expensive states for electricity</a> covers the full ranking, and each state has its own page, such as <a href="/electricity-prices/texas/">Texas electricity prices</a> and <a href="/electricity-prices/new-york/">New York electricity prices</a>.</p>
<h2 id="what-this-means-for-your-bill-and-for-solar">What this means for your bill and for solar</h2>
<p>Your bill is roughly your kWh use times your rate, plus fixed fees. A higher rate means each kWh you avoid buying is worth more. That is the core idea behind solar payback: rooftop panels offset purchased power, so a state with higher prices can repay a system faster, all else equal. Our guide on <a href="/guides/electricity-prices-and-solar-payback/">electricity prices and solar payback</a> explains the link, and <a href="/guides/solar-payback-period-explained/">solar payback period explained</a> shows how the math works.</p>
<p>Price is only one input. Sun hours, system cost, how your utility credits exported power, and whether you have incentives all matter. The federal 30% Residential Clean Energy Credit (Section 25D) is not available for property placed in service after December 31, 2025, according to the IRS. Net metering and incentives vary by state and utility; DSIRE keeps a database of current programs. Berkeley Lab reports a median installed price of $4.0 per watt for host-owned residential systems installed in 2024, with cash purchases at a median of $3.5 per watt.</p>
<h3>Our example home</h3>
<p>When we model a home, we use these assumptions: a 7 kW system, 10,500 kWh of yearly use, 40% of use while the sun is up, $3,500 per kW ($24,500 total), no incentives, $100 a year for maintenance, electricity prices rising 2.5% a year, panel output falling 0.5% a year, a 25-year horizon, and export credits capped at a year of grid purchases. Production comes from PVWatts, the Department of Energy&#39;s free solar estimator, for a south-facing roof at a 20° pitch with 14% losses. Full details are in our <a href="/methodology/">methodology</a>.</p>

<h2 id="bottom-line">Bottom line</h2>
<p>Prices rose in most states in August 2026, and the U.S. residential average of 18.16¢/kWh was up 3.4% from a year earlier. The size of the change varies a lot: Hawaii and Maine jumped, while Connecticut fell. Because EIA&#39;s latest month is preliminary, treat these numbers as a snapshot. For decisions about your home, rely on your own bill and written quotes, and use <a href="/electricity-prices/">our price tables</a> to see how your state compares.</p>
