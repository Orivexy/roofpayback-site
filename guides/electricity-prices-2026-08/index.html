Electricity Prices in August 2026: Where They Rose and Fell

U.S. home electricity averaged 18.16¢/kWh in August 2026, up 3.4%. See which states rose or fell most, how July compared, and what it means for bills.

Published October 24, 2026·Updated November 18, 2026·6 min read·RoofPayback editorial·Figures checked against EIA and PVWatts data

What we found

Connecticut's average home electricity price fell from 30.17¢ to 24.51¢ per kilowatt-hour (kWh) between August of last year and August 2026. That 18.8% drop was the steepest in the country, in a month when most states moved the other way. Hawaii went furthest in that direction, up 22.5%.

The U.S. residential average landed at 18.16¢/kWh, compared with 17.57¢ a year earlier, a rise of 3.4%. All figures come from the U.S. Energy Information Administration (EIA), and the newest month is preliminary. This report is our latest monthly reading; it also notes how the picture shifted since the July data.

1. Hawaii's 22.5% jump led a long list of increases

Hawaii's rise stands apart twice over. It is the largest percentage gain in the August data, and it landed on the state that already charged the most. Moving from 38.53¢ to 47.18¢ adds several cents to every unit a household buys.

Most of the next tier sits in the Northeast and the mid-Atlantic. These are the biggest year-over-year increases, down to 9%:

State August 2026 A year earlier Change
Hawaii 47.18¢ 38.53¢ 22.5%
Maine 32.11¢ 28.14¢ 14.1%
New Hampshire 27.64¢ 24.44¢ 13.1%
District of Columbia 25.98¢ 23.18¢ 12.1%
Virginia 17.64¢ 15.85¢ 11.3%
Delaware 18.7¢ 16.85¢ 11%
Michigan 22.99¢ 20.73¢ 10.9%
Idaho 13.37¢ 12.08¢ 10.7%
Ohio 19.38¢ 17.55¢ 10.4%
Iowa 17.06¢ 15.48¢ 10.2%
Pennsylvania 21.77¢ 19.93¢ 9.2%
South Dakota 15.15¢ 13.9¢ 9%
Montana 15.57¢ 14.29¢ 9%

Percentages can mislead in cheap states. Idaho climbed 10.7% and South Dakota 9%, yet both remain well below the national average of 18.16¢. A small starting price makes any increase look large. Maine's 14.1% rise, on top of a much higher base, adds far more cents to each kWh than Idaho's. Reading the cents columns next to the change column gives the truer picture.

2. Connecticut, California and a few others got cheaper

Price cuts were rare. Only seven states had lower residential prices than a year before:

State August 2026 A year earlier Change
Connecticut 24.51¢ 30.17¢ -18.8%
California 29.75¢ 30.92¢ -3.8%
Utah 13.31¢ 13.62¢ -2.3%
Florida 14.99¢ 15.33¢ -2.2%
New Jersey 24.61¢ 24.7¢ -0.4%
Arizona 15.05¢ 15.09¢ -0.3%
Massachusetts 30.48¢ 30.55¢ -0.2%

Alabama (0.4%), Oregon (0.4%) and Wyoming (0.7%) barely moved.

A falling price can still be a high one. Connecticut, California and Massachusetts all remain among the pricier states after their declines. At the top of the August ranking sit Hawaii (47.18¢), Maine (32.11¢), Massachusetts (30.48¢), California (29.75¢), Alaska (29.41¢) and New York (28.65¢). The low end includes Nevada (12.72¢), Louisiana (12.87¢), North Dakota (13.31¢), Utah (13.31¢) and Idaho (13.37¢). The full ranking is in our guide to the cheapest and most expensive states for electricity.

3. Year-over-year increases cooled between July and August

The July data told a hotter story. In July 2026, the national residential average was 18.31¢/kWh, up 4.9% on a year earlier. By August the yearly gain had narrowed to 3.4%.

Several states followed the same direction:

  • New York: up 14% year over year in July, 7.5% in August.
  • New Hampshire: 16.7% in July, 13.1% in August.
  • Maine: 15.8% in July, 14.1% in August.
  • Maryland: 13.7% in July, 7.6% in August.
  • North Carolina: 13.4% in July, just 2.5% in August.

Hawaii held its pace, at 22% in July and 22.5% in August. Connecticut's decline deepened, from -12.5% in July to -18.8% in August.

These swings show why one month is a snapshot. Utilities change rates on their own schedules, so a single month can reflect the timing of a rate case, a shift in fuel costs or a one-time adjustment. Comparing each month with the same month a year earlier is the fair test; setting July's cents directly against August's mixes in seasonal change.

4. Factories saw the sharpest increase, not households

The national split by customer type tells a different story from the state headlines:

  • Industrial: 9.64¢/kWh, from 8.98¢ (up 7.3%)
  • Commercial: 14.47¢/kWh, from 13.87¢ (up 4.3%)
  • Residential: 18.16¢/kWh, from 17.57¢ (up 3.4%)

All sectors together averaged 14.87¢/kWh, up from 14.15¢. The order of the increases flipped from July, when the residential price was rising faster (4.9%) than the industrial one (4.7%).

Homes still pay the most per kWh by a wide margin. Serving many small customers costs more per unit than serving one large plant, so the residential rate stays on top even when it rises more slowly. Our page on residential vs. business electricity prices digs into why.

5. New England was the only region to fall; the Great Lakes rose most

EIA also groups states into census divisions. August 2026 residential prices:

  • Pacific Noncontiguous: 40.79¢, from 34.72¢ (17.5%)
  • New England: 28.31¢, from 29.01¢ (-2.4%)
  • Pacific Contiguous: 25.59¢, from 25.42¢ (0.7%)
  • Middle Atlantic: 25.17¢, from 23.73¢ (6.1%)
  • East North Central: 19.6¢, from 18.11¢ (8.2%)
  • South Atlantic: 16.04¢, from 15.64¢ (2.6%)
  • West North Central: 15.92¢, from 15.17¢ (4.9%)
  • West South Central: 15.3¢, from 14.79¢ (3.4%)
  • Mountain: 14.86¢, from 14.57¢ (2%)
  • East South Central: 14.47¢, from 13.99¢ (3.4%)

East North Central, the Great Lakes area, had the biggest rise among mainland divisions. New England, which was up 0.4% in the July data, slipped into decline in August, though it is still the priciest mainland division. Fuel mix, power-line costs and local rules shape this map, as covered in why electricity prices differ by state and electricity prices by region.

How we measured it

Every price here comes from EIA's Electric Power Monthly, Table 5.6.A, which lists average prices by state, region and customer type each month. Each change compares August 2026 with August of the prior year, keeping summer against summer. The July comparisons use the same table's July release.

Three limits apply:

  • Preliminary data. EIA revises recent months as more utility reports arrive. Small moves are the most likely to shift; big ones, like Hawaii's or Connecticut's, are more likely to hold.
  • One month only. An outlier month can exaggerate or hide a trend.
  • State averages, not your rate. Fixed monthly charges and tiered rates, where the price per kWh changes as use goes up, can push a household's effective price away from the state figure.

Single-state pages, such as Texas electricity prices and New York electricity prices, show the local detail.

What this means for your bill

A higher rate hits a heavy user harder than a light one, because the bill is the rate multiplied by the kWh you use, plus fixed fees. A home running air conditioning hard or heating with electricity can face a large bill even in a cheap state; average electric bill by state shows how usage changes the picture. The electricity cost calculator lets you enter your own usage and rate.

A one-year rise is not a forecast either. North Carolina's swing from July to August is a reminder that a state's pace can change sharply from one month's data to the next.

Rising prices do raise the value of every kWh you avoid buying. For rooftop solar, the local price level is the first thing that decides payback, which is why a higher-price state can repay a system faster. The second is how your utility credits power sent back to the grid, and where those credits are low it can outweigh price; net metering vs. net billing explains the difference. Our best states for solar payback ranking applies both to every state.

Practical next moves:

  1. Find the rate on your latest bill, in cents per kWh, and note any fixed charges.
  2. Compare it with your state in our price tables.
  3. Ask your utility whether a time-of-use plan, where power costs less at certain hours, would suit your habits.
  4. Read how to lower your electric bill for steps that target heating, cooling and water heating first.
  5. If solar interests you, test it in the solar calculator.

August's clearest message: in most states power cost more than a year ago, but the pace of increase slowed from July, and the places paying the most are still the Northeast, California, Alaska and Hawaii.
