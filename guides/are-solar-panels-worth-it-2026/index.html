Are Solar Panels Worth It in 2026? A State-by-State Answer

No federal credit, lower export credits in some states: where solar still pays back in under 10 years, where it takes 16 or more, and what help is left.

Published November 26, 2026·Updated November 28, 2026·5 min read·RoofPayback editorial·Figures checked against EIA and PVWatts data

Official data: EIA · DOE · IRS · how we check

The answer up front

For most U.S. homeowners in 2026, solar still earns back its cost within the 25 years we model, but how fast depends on three things you can look up before you talk to anyone: your electricity price, what your utility pays for power you send to the grid, and the price per watt you are quoted.

Two changes make 2026 different from the advice you may have read before. The 30% federal credit is gone for new systems. And in several states, new customers no longer get the full retail price for exported power. We confirmed it in official sources for California, Hawaii and Louisiana; Kentucky's regulator sets the credit for each utility, and Texas leaves it to each electricity plan. Below, each state is placed in a payback band, with those five states on their working cases.

What ended, and when

The IRS says the Residential Clean Energy Credit (Section 25D) "is not available for any property placed in service after December 31, 2025," and that you claim it "for the tax year when the property is installed, not merely purchased." A system installed in 2026 gets nothing from it.

How much did that cost buyers? In our model, the credit would have cut every state's payback by roughly a quarter. In Alabama, for example, the example home pays back in 14.2 years today and would have taken 10.4 with the credit. That ground is not coming back, so an installer quote that still subtracts it is out of date.

Which band is your state in?

Every state uses our example home (7 kW at $3,500 per kW, no incentives, 10,500 kWh a year) with September 2026 EIA prices and full retail credit for exports, except where noted. The year-by-year figures for each state are in the full ranking.

Under 10 years (8): Hawaii, Maine, Massachusetts, Rhode Island, New Hampshire, New York, District of Columbia, Connecticut. Hawaii stays here even at Hawaiian Electric's current daytime export rate on Oahu: 7.8 years instead of 4.7.

10 to 13 years (12): New Jersey, Vermont, Maryland, Pennsylvania, Michigan, Colorado, New Mexico, Wisconsin, Alaska, Delaware, Virginia, and California. California would be in the top band at full credit (7.2 years), but new customers of its large utilities are on the Net Billing Tariff; with exports worth a quarter of retail, it lands at 12.1 years.

13 to 16 years (21): Illinois, Ohio, Kansas, South Carolina, Arizona, Minnesota, Oklahoma, Missouri, South Dakota, Alabama, Georgia, Indiana, Wyoming, Florida, North Carolina, Montana, Idaho, Nevada, Arkansas, Iowa, Mississippi.

16 years or more (10): West Virginia, Utah, Nebraska, North Dakota, Oregon, Tennessee, Washington, Texas, Kentucky, Louisiana. All charge under 16.3¢ per kWh, and Oregon and Washington have the weakest sun in the lower 48. The last three are here on their working cases, exports worth a quarter of retail:

  • Texas: 23.1 years. The PUCT says buyback rates are usually lower than the retail price; a plan that paid close to retail would put Texas at 14.3 years. The Texas guide shows every rate in between.
  • Louisiana: does not pay back within 25 years. The Public Service Commission credits exports from systems installed after 2019 at avoided cost, 3.58¢ to 3.99¢ per kWh by utility in 2026.
  • Kentucky: does not pay back within 25 years. The Public Service Commission sets the export credit for each utility in rate cases, and we did not find one statewide figure, so the quarter is an assumption.

California, Hawaii, Louisiana, Kentucky and Texas are the states where we checked the export rule in an official source. For every other state the band assumes full credit, which is the best case: confirm the rule with your utility. Net metering vs. net billing shows how far each state moves at half, quarter and zero credit.

What help is left

The federal credit was the one incentive that applied everywhere. What remains is state, local and utility help, and it varies by address.

State tax credits. New York is an example we confirmed: a credit of 25% of qualified solar equipment costs, limited to $5,000, for a system at your principal residence, with unused credit carried over for up to five years. On the example home it cuts payback from 9.1 to 7.4 years. Is solar worth it in New York has the details.

Sales and property tax exemptions. New York also exempts residential solar equipment from its 4% state sales tax. Other states have their own rules; DSIRE lists them.

Rebates. A rebate lowers what you pay up front. Here is what a flat rebate does to the example home in five states, with full export credit except in Louisiana, which uses its quarter-credit working case:

State No rebate $1,000 rebate $2,500 rebate
Massachusetts 8.3 years 8 years 7.5 years
Illinois 13 years 12.6 years 11.9 years
Georgia 14.2 years 13.7 years 12.9 years
Utah 16.1 years 15.5 years 14.7 years
Louisiana (quarter credit) over 25 years over 25 years 24.3 years

The same $2,500 is worth 0.8 years in Massachusetts and 1.4 years in Utah, because it replaces more years of small savings. Where power is cheap, a modest rebate is worth chasing; where it is expensive, it is a bonus. Louisiana shows the limit: when exports earn little, even $2,500 only just brings the example inside 25 years.

To find programs, search your state and utility on DSIRE, check the end date and funding limit, and confirm with the program administrator that it is open before a quote counts it.

The quote matters as much as the state line

Berkeley Lab's 2026 Data Update put the median installed price for host-owned home systems installed in 2025 at $3.60 per watt, $3.00 for cash purchases and $4.50 for loan-financed systems, and found that roughly a quarter of the 100 largest installers had medians below $3 per watt. A cheap quote in a slow state can beat an expensive quote in a fast one. Solar panel cost per watt gives the most you can pay in each state for a 10- or 15-year payback.

So, is it worth it for you?

Probably yes if you are in the first two bands, your utility credits exports at or near retail (or you can use most of your output during the day), and your quote is near or below $3.50 per watt.

Shop harder first if you are in the 13-to-16-year band. A lower price per watt, a local rebate or a system sized to your daytime use can bring you under 13 years. The solar quote checklist helps you compare offers.

Waiting or skipping is reasonable if you are in the slowest band and exports earn well below retail, if you expect to move within a decade, or if the roof needs replacing soon. Replace the roof before panels go on, not after.

Whatever the band, run your own bill, export rate and best quote through the solar calculator before you decide.
