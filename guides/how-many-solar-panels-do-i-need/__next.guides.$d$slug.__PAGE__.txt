22:Tad6,<div class="short-answer"><h2 id="in-one-minute">In one minute</h2>
<p>Divide your yearly electricity use by what one kW of panels makes where you live. That gives the system size that covers 100% of your use on paper. Then divide the size by the rating of one panel to get the count.</p>
<p>For a home using 10,500 kWh a year, the size works out to about 7.4 kW in Texas, 6.3 kW in California, 8.1 kW in New York and 9.7 kW in Washington. With panels rated at 0.4 kW each, that is 19 panels in Texas, 16 in California, 21 in New York and 25 in Washington.</p>
<p>Don&#39;t stop at that number. Test a slightly smaller system too: it often pays back faster, because credits for surplus power are capped.</p>
<p>You need three things: a year of electric bills, your local solar output per kW, and the panel rating from your quote.</p>
</div><h2 id="step-1-pull-a-full-year-of-kwh">Step 1: Pull a full year of kWh</h2>
<p><strong>Do this:</strong> Add up the <a href="/guides/what-is-a-kilowatt-hour/">kilowatt-hours</a> from your last 12 electric bills. Most utilities also let you download usage history from your online account, which is faster.</p>
<p><strong>Why:</strong> Single months mislead. Summer air conditioning and winter heating can push one bill far above or below your real average.</p>
<p><strong>Then adjust:</strong> Planning an electric car, a heat pump or a pool? Add their expected use now, before you size anything. <a href="/guides/solar-for-high-electricity-use/">Solar for high-usage homes</a> and <a href="/guides/solar-and-electric-car-charging/">solar and electric car charging</a> show how a bigger load changes the result. Leave out loads you&#39;re only vaguely considering; more on that under the mistakes below.</p>
<h2 id="step-2-find-out-how-much-one-kw-produces-at-your-address">Step 2: Find out how much one kW produces at your address</h2>
<p><strong>Do this:</strong> Open your <a href="/solar-panels/">state page</a> and note the yearly output per kW of panels for your area. The page also shows how much you lose if your roof faces east, west or southeast instead of south.</p>
<p><strong>Why:</strong> The same panel makes very different amounts of power from place to place. Our PVWatts figures assume south-facing panels at a 20° pitch (the roof angle). Across our reference cities, yearly output ranges from 1,779 kWh per kW in Albuquerque down to 922 in Anchorage. Among the states in the table in Step 3, Phoenix is highest at 1,755 and Seattle lowest at 1,088.</p>
<p>That spread is why a neighbor&#39;s system size from another state tells you almost nothing about yours. <a href="/guides/how-much-electricity-does-a-solar-panel-produce/">How much electricity a solar panel produces</a> breaks the output down by month.</p>

30:T1b45,<h2 id="step-3-divide-use-by-output-then-by-panel-rating">Step 3: Divide use by output, then by panel rating</h2>
<p><strong>Do this:</strong> Annual use ÷ output per kW = the size that covers 100% of your use. Then system size ÷ the rating of one panel = the number of panels, rounded up.</p>
<p><strong>Why:</strong> It gives you a starting point anchored in your own bills, not in a sales pitch.</p>
<p>Panel ratings are printed in watts on the spec sheet; divide by a thousand to get kW. The two ratings below are examples to show the arithmetic, not a recommendation, and the counts are worked from the rounded sizes in the second column. Use the rating in your own quote.</p>

<p><strong>A worked example:</strong> our 7 kW example system needs 7 ÷ 0.4 = 17.5 panels at 0.4 kW, so 18 panels. With 0.45 kW panels, 7 ÷ 0.45 is about 15.6, so 16 panels. The system size, and the power it makes, stays the same; only the count changes. That is why two quotes with different panel counts can describe the same system.</p>
<h2 id="step-4-test-several-sizes-not-just-one">Step 4: Test several sizes, not just one</h2>
<p><strong>Do this:</strong> Run payback for a few sizes below and above your Step 3 number. Payback is the number of years until savings repay what the system cost.</p>
<p><strong>Why:</strong> Covering 100% of your use is not automatically the best financial choice.</p>

<p><strong>Read the table for two things:</strong></p>
<ol>
<li><strong>Where extra panels stop paying.</strong> In Texas, a 9 kW and a 12 kW system both save $1,671 in the first year. Once credits cover a full year of grid purchases, any extra exports earn nothing at true-up (the yearly settlement where the utility squares your credits against your use).</li>
<li><strong>Whether you want speed or total gain.</strong> In Arizona, the fastest payback comes from 5 kW (12.6 years). The largest 25-year gain comes from 7 kW ($26,978 vs. $22,233). Neither answer is wrong. Pick the goal that fits your plans for the house.</li>
</ol>
<h2 id="step-5-correct-for-your-export-credit">Step 5: Correct for your export credit</h2>
<p><strong>Do this:</strong> Find out what your utility pays for power you send back to the grid. Then read <a href="/guides/net-metering-vs-net-billing-payback/">net metering vs. net billing</a>.</p>
<p><strong>Why:</strong> Net metering credits exports at the full retail price. Net billing usually pays less. When exports are worth less than retail, smaller systems look even better, because more of their output is used directly in the house, where it replaces power you would otherwise buy at full price.</p>
<h2 id="step-6-fit-the-system-to-your-roof">Step 6: Fit the system to your roof</h2>
<p><strong>Do this:</strong> Count how many panels of the quoted rating fit on the usable parts of your roof, then check shade and orientation.</p>
<p><strong>Why:</strong> The roof sets the upper limit. Trees or a neighbor&#39;s building can cut output. An east- or west-facing roof needs more kW to make the same amount of power as a south-facing one, so the panel count from Step 3 may grow, or may simply not fit. More efficient panels fit more kW into the same area, which is worth weighing against their price per watt.</p>
<h2 id="mistakes-that-cost-money">Mistakes that cost money</h2>
<ul>
<li><strong>Multiplying one month&#39;s bill by twelve.</strong> Seasonal swings distort the result in either direction.</li>
<li><strong>Sizing for future use you&#39;re not sure about.</strong> Oversizing today costs money now. Adding panels later is usually possible if the car or heat pump actually arrives.</li>
<li><strong>Comparing quotes by panel count.</strong> Two quotes with the same number of panels can differ a lot in output. Compare kW, estimated annual kWh, and price per watt instead; <a href="/guides/solar-panel-cost-by-state/">solar panel cost by state</a> shows how price changes the result.</li>
<li><strong>Sizing to cover your darkest month.</strong> A system big enough for January makes a large summer surplus, and under a yearly credit cap much of it earns nothing. <a href="/guides/solar-panels-in-winter-by-state/">Solar panels in winter by state</a> shows how deep the winter dip runs.</li>
<li><strong>Going big because exports &quot;always pay.&quot;</strong> Under a yearly credit cap, they stop paying once you cover a full year of grid purchases, as the Texas example shows.</li>
</ul>
<h2 id="your-checklist">Your checklist</h2>
<ul>
<li>Total kWh from 12 months of bills, plus any planned loads you&#39;re sure about</li>
<li>Output per kW for your area, from your <a href="/solar-panels/">state page</a></li>
<li>Starting size: annual use ÷ output per kW</li>
<li>Panel count: system kW ÷ the panel rating in kW from the quote, rounded up</li>
<li>Payback for at least two smaller and one larger size</li>
<li>Your goal chosen: fastest payback or largest long-term gain</li>
<li>Export credit checked: net metering or net billing</li>
<li>Roof space, shade and orientation confirmed for that panel count</li>
<li>Quotes compared on kW, annual kWh and price per watt</li>
</ul>
