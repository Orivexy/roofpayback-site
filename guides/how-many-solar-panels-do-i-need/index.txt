67:T5c9,<div class="short-answer"><h2 id="short-answer">Short answer</h2>
<p>Start from your annual electricity use, divide it by what one kW of panels produces where you live, and you get the system size that would cover 100% of your use on paper. For a home using 10500 kWh/yr, that is about 7.4 kW in Texas, 6.3 kW in California, 8.1 kW in New York and 9.7 kW in Washington. Then check whether a slightly smaller system pays back faster — it often does, because credits for surplus power are capped.</p>
</div><h2 id="step-1-find-your-annual-kwh">Step 1: find your annual kWh</h2>
<p>Add up the kWh from your last 12 electric bills, or download the usage history from your utility&#39;s online account. Use a full year: summer air conditioning and winter heating can make single months misleading. If you plan to add an electric car, a heat pump or a pool, add their expected use before sizing.</p>
<h2 id="step-2-look-up-output-per-kw-where-you-live">Step 2: look up output per kW where you live</h2>
<p>One kW of panels produces very different amounts depending on location. In our PVWatts data for south-facing panels at a 20° pitch, the reference cities range from 1779 kWh per kW per year in Albuquerque to 922 in Anchorage; among the states in the table below, Phoenix is highest at 1755 and Seattle lowest at 1088. Your state page lists the figure for your area, plus how much a roof facing east, west or southeast loses: see the <a href="/solar-panels/">state pages</a>.</p>

</ol>
<h2 id="step-5-adjust-for-your-export-credit-and-roof">Step 5: adjust for your export credit and roof</h2>
<p>If your utility pays less than retail for exported power, smaller systems look even better, because a larger share of their output is used directly. Read <a href="/guides/net-metering-vs-net-billing-payback/">net metering vs. net billing</a> for how much this moves payback. Roof space, shading and orientation set the upper limit: an east- or west-facing roof needs more kW for the same output.</p>
<h2 id="common-sizing-mistakes">Common sizing mistakes</h2>
<ul>
<li><strong>Using one month&#39;s bill times twelve.</strong> Seasonal swings distort the result.</li>
<li><strong>Sizing for future use you are not sure about.</strong> Oversizing today costs money now; adding panels later is usually possible.</li>
<li><strong>Comparing quotes by panel count.</strong> Compare kW, estimated annual kWh, and price per watt instead.</li>
<li><strong>Assuming incentives that no longer exist.</strong> The 30% federal credit ended for systems placed in service after 2025; see <a href="/guides/is-solar-worth-it-without-tax-credit/">is solar still worth it without the tax credit</a>.</li>
</ul>
<h2 id="bottom-line">Bottom line</h2>
<p>Size from a full year of kWh and your local output, then test a few sizes around that number. The <a href="/solar-calculator/">solar calculator</a> does all of this with your own bill, roof and quote, and its &quot;Which size pays off best?&quot; table shows where extra panels stop adding value.</p>
