Is Solar Worth It in New York? The Payback Math

New York's 25% state solar credit (up to $5,000) cuts our example payback from 9.1 to 7.4 years with full export credit. The math for typical and upstate homes.

Published November 15, 2026·Updated November 28, 2026·5 min read·RoofPayback editorial·Figures checked against EIA and PVWatts data

The answer

Usually yes. In New York City, our 7 kW example home pays back in 9.1 years with no incentives and full credit for exported power. New York's own solar tax credit, 25% of the equipment cost up to $5,000, brings that to 7.4 years. Both figures assume full credit for exports, which we have not verified for New York, so they are an upper bound: with exports worth a quarter of retail, the same home takes 14.4 years, or 11.9 with the state credit. The federal credit is gone, but the state one is not. Of the two adjustments to a national estimate, the export credit moves the result more, but the state credit is the one whose terms are published.

The reason solar works here is price, not sunshine. New York homes paid an average of 28.42¢ per kilowatt-hour in September 2026, up 4.2% from 27.28¢ a year earlier, against a U.S. average of 18.17¢. New York City panels make a modest 1,291 kWh per kW a year in PVWatts.

What is New York's solar tax credit?

The New York State Department of Taxation and Finance describes the Solar Energy System Equipment Credit this way (page read November 28, 2026):

  • Amount: "equal to 25% of your qualified solar energy system equipment expenditures," limited to $5,000.
  • Where: the system must be installed and used at your principal residence in New York State.
  • If your tax is smaller than the credit: it is not refundable, but the unused part "can be carried over for up to five years."
  • Leases and power purchase agreements: you can also claim it under a written lease of solar equipment, or under a written agreement of at least ten years to buy the power from equipment you don't own.
  • Form: IT-255.

The department's page was last updated in December 2019, so confirm the terms for your tax year with the department or a tax professional before you count on them.

There is a second, smaller break. The sale and installation of residential solar equipment is exempt from the 4% state sales and use tax and the 3/8% tax in the Metropolitan Commuter Transportation District, and may be exempt from local sales tax depending on the jurisdiction. Your contractor or seller needs the right exemption certificate (Form ST-120.1 for contractors, ST-121 for homeowners).

How much does the credit change payback?

On the $24,500 example system, 25% would be $6,125, so the $5,000 limit applies. We entered it as an incentive in the model, treating the whole installed price as qualified equipment expenditure; ask your installer which lines of the quote qualify.

Credit for exported power No state credit With $5,000 state credit
Full retail 9.1 years 7.4 years
Half retail 12.1 years 9.9 years
Quarter retail 14.4 years 11.9 years

The credit is worth more as the export credit gets weaker, because it removes the same $5,000 from a slower stream of savings.

Does it matter that the credit can arrive over several tax years? Not for the payback year. We spread the $5,000 over five years in the model and the result stayed at 7.4 years, because payback counts dollars without discounting and the credit is used up before break-even. It matters for your cash flow, not for the break-even date.

What size suits a New York home?

The example home uses 10,500 kWh a year. EIA-861M data for October 2025 to September 2026 put the average New York household at 591 kWh a month, or 7,088 kWh a year, with an average bill of $169. Covering that in New York City takes about 5.5 kW.

Smaller systems, with the state credit at 25% of each system's cost:

System Cost State credit Payback, full credit Payback, quarter credit
4 kW $14,000 $3,500 7.2 years 10.6 years
5 kW $17,500 $4,375 7.1 years 11.9 years
6 kW $21,000 $5,000 7.7 years 13.1 years

With full credit for exports, 4 to 5 kW gives the shortest payback for an average home, and 6 kW starts to make more power than the house can get credit for. If exports earn much less than retail, the smallest system wins by more than a year. The credit cap matters too: above $20,000 of equipment, each extra dollar gets no state credit.

Does this apply upstate?

Partly. The sunshine is a little weaker upstate, and the rates may differ from the state average. With the state average price, no state credit and the same 7 kW system:

City Output per kW Payback, full credit Payback, quarter credit
New York City 1,291 kWh 9.1 years 14.4 years
Albany 1,225 kWh 9.6 years 14.7 years
Buffalo 1,202 kWh 9.8 years 14.9 years
Rochester 1,199 kWh 9.8 years 15 years

The gap between New York City and Rochester is under a year. A difference between your utility's rate and the 28.42¢ state average would move the result more than the weather does, so use the price per kWh on your own bill.

What should I ask about exports?

We did not verify New York's current crediting rules for this update, so the full and quarter columns above bracket the result rather than predict it. Ask your utility how it credits power you send to the grid, whether any monthly charge applies to solar customers, and how long the terms last for a new system. Net metering vs. net billing shows what each level of credit does across states.

How do I check the numbers for my own home?

  1. Add up a year of use from your bills and size the system to it, not to the roof.
  2. Get a quote with equipment cost itemized, since the state credit is based on qualified equipment expenditures, and confirm no state sales tax is charged on it.
  3. Ask your utility how it credits exports and whether solar customers pay any extra monthly charge.
  4. Enter the quote, the credit and your rate in the solar calculator, which has a field for incentives.

No federal credit applies: the IRS says the Residential Clean Energy Credit "is not available for any property placed in service after December 31, 2025." The solar quote checklist covers the rest of the paperwork, and the New York solar page has the state inputs.
