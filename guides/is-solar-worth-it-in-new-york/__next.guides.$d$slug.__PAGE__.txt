22:Tedc,<div class="short-answer"><p class="short-answer-label">The answer</p>
<p>Usually yes. In New York City, our 7 kW example home pays back in 9.1 years with no incentives and full credit for exported power. New York&#39;s own solar tax credit, 25% of the equipment cost up to $5,000, brings that to <strong>7.4 years</strong>. Both figures assume full credit for exports, which we have not verified for New York, so they are an upper bound: with exports worth a quarter of retail, the same home takes 14.4 years, or 11.9 with the state credit. The federal credit is gone, but the state one is not. Of the two adjustments to a national estimate, the export credit moves the result more, but the state credit is the one whose terms are published.</p>
<p>The reason solar works here is price, not sunshine. New York homes paid an average of 28.42¢ per <a href="/guides/what-is-a-kilowatt-hour/">kilowatt-hour</a> in September 2026, up 4.2% from 27.28¢ a year earlier, against a U.S. average of 18.17¢. New York City panels make a modest 1,291 kWh per kW a year in PVWatts.</p>
</div><h2 id="what-is-new-york-39-s-solar-tax-credit">What is New York&#39;s solar tax credit?</h2>
<p>The New York State Department of Taxation and Finance describes the Solar Energy System Equipment Credit this way (page read November 28, 2026):</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Amount:</strong> &quot;equal to 25% of your qualified solar energy system equipment expenditures,&quot; limited to $5,000.</li>
<li><strong>Where:</strong> the system must be installed and used at your principal residence in New York State.</li>
<li><strong>If your tax is smaller than the credit:</strong> it is not refundable, but the unused part &quot;can be carried over for up to five years.&quot;</li>
<li><strong>Leases and power purchase agreements:</strong> you can also claim it under a written lease of solar equipment, or under a written agreement of at least ten years to buy the power from equipment you don&#39;t own.</li>
<li><strong>Form:</strong> IT-255.</li>
</ul>
<p>The department&#39;s page was last updated in December 2019, so confirm the terms for your tax year with the department or a tax professional before you count on them.</p>
<p>There is a second, smaller break. The sale and installation of residential solar equipment is exempt from the 4% state sales and use tax and the 3/8% tax in the Metropolitan Commuter Transportation District, and may be exempt from local sales tax depending on the jurisdiction. Your contractor or seller needs the right exemption certificate (Form ST-120.1 for contractors, ST-121 for homeowners).</p>
<h2 id="how-much-does-the-credit-change-payback">How much does the credit change payback?</h2>
<p>On the $24,500 example system, 25% would be $6,125, so the $5,000 limit applies. We entered it as an incentive in the model, treating the whole installed price as qualified equipment expenditure; ask your installer which lines of the quote qualify.</p>
<table>
<thead>
<tr>
<th>Credit for exported power</th>
<th>No state credit</th>
<th>With $5,000 state credit</th>
</tr>
</thead>
<tbody><tr>
<td>Full retail</td>
<td>9.1 years</td>
<td>7.4 years</td>
</tr>
<tr>
<td>Half retail</td>
<td>12.1 years</td>
<td>9.9 years</td>
</tr>
<tr>
<td>Quarter retail</td>

</tr>
</tbody></table>
<p>The credit is worth more as the export credit gets weaker, because it removes the same $5,000 from a slower stream of savings.</p>
<p>Does it matter that the credit can arrive over several tax years? Not for the payback year. We spread the $5,000 over five years in the model and the result stayed at 7.4 years, because payback counts dollars without discounting and the credit is used up before break-even. It matters for your cash flow, not for the break-even date.</p>

<h2 id="what-should-i-ask-about-exports">What should I ask about exports?</h2>
<p>We did not verify New York&#39;s current crediting rules for this update, so the full and quarter columns above bracket the result rather than predict it. Ask your utility how it credits power you send to the grid, whether any monthly charge applies to solar customers, and how long the terms last for a new system. <a href="/guides/net-metering-vs-net-billing-payback/">Net metering vs. net billing</a> shows what each level of credit does across states.</p>
<h2 id="how-do-i-check-the-numbers-for-my-own-home">How do I check the numbers for my own home?</h2>
<ol>
<li><strong>Add up a year of use</strong> from your bills and size the system to it, not to the roof.</li>
<li><strong>Get a quote with equipment cost itemized,</strong> since the state credit is based on qualified equipment expenditures, and confirm no state sales tax is charged on it.</li>
<li><strong>Ask your utility how it credits exports</strong> and whether solar customers pay any extra monthly charge.</li>
<li><strong>Enter the quote, the credit and your rate</strong> in the <a href="/solar-calculator/">solar calculator</a>, which has a field for incentives.</li>
</ol>
<p>No federal credit applies: the IRS says the Residential Clean Energy Credit &quot;is not available for any property placed in service after December 31, 2025.&quot; The <a href="/guides/solar-quote-checklist/">solar quote checklist</a> covers the rest of the paperwork, and the <a href="/solar-panels/new-york/">New York solar page</a> has the state inputs.</p>
