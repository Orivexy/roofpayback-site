22:Tab4,<div class="short-answer"><h2 id="the-answer">The answer</h2>
<p>Often yes, and the reason is the price of power, not the weather.</p>
<p>New York has some of the highest residential electricity prices in the country, and that is what drives solar payback here. Payback is the number of years it takes for bill savings to add up to what the system cost. In the RoofPayback example for New York City, a 7 kW system pays itself back in about 9 years if every exported kilowatt-hour earns full credit.</p>
<p>New York City gets 1,291 kWh per kW of panels each year, which is modest. The example assumes no incentives; the rest of its setup is on the <a href="/methodology/">methodology</a> page.</p>
</div><h2 id="how-much-do-new-yorkers-pay-for-electricity">How much do New Yorkers pay for electricity?</h2>
<p>A lot more than most of the country. EIA data for August 2026 puts the average New York residential price at 28.65¢ per <a href="/guides/what-is-a-kilowatt-hour/">kilowatt-hour</a> (kWh). The U.S. average that month was 18.16¢.</p>
<p>Prices also rose over the year. A year earlier, New York was at 26.64¢, so the price rose 7.5%.</p>
<p>Keep in mind what an average is. EIA calculates it as utility revenue divided by kWh sold, so it includes taxes and fees. Your bill will differ with your rate plan, your usage and your utility.</p>

<h2 id="doesn-39-t-new-york-get-too-little-sun-for-solar">Doesn&#39;t New York get too little sun for solar?</h2>
<p>Less sun, yes. Too little, no.</p>
<p>Production in our model comes from PVWatts (<a href="/methodology/">methodology</a>). For New York City it assumes south-facing panels at a 20° pitch and 14% system losses. That gives 1,291 kWh per kW per year.</p>
<p>Phoenix gets 1,755 kWh per kW, and Albuquerque gets 1,779. So New York City clearly has less sun. Yet its example payback is much shorter than either of those cities, because each kWh it produces replaces a more expensive kWh from the grid.</p>
<p>In the example, year-one savings with full credit come to $2,590, a model result rather than a quote.</p>

33:T1369,<h2 id="what-happens-if-my-utility-pays-less-for-the-power-i-send-back">What happens if my utility pays less for the power I send back?</h2>
<p>Your payback gets longer, but in this example it stays reasonable.</p>
<p>When your panels make more than your home is using, the extra goes to the grid. With full retail net metering, each exported kWh offsets a kWh you would otherwise buy. With a lower export credit, the panels still cut what you buy while the sun is up, but the exports earn less.</p>
<p>The New York City example shifts like this:</p>
<table>
<thead>
<tr>
<th>Credit for exported power</th>
<th>Example payback</th>
</tr>
</thead>
<tbody><tr>
<td>Full credit</td>
<td>9 years</td>
</tr>
<tr>
<td>Half credit</td>
<td>12 years</td>
</tr>
<tr>
<td>Quarter credit</td>
<td>14.3 years</td>
</tr>
</tbody></table>
<p>Even at a quarter credit, 14.3 years beats the full-credit example in Alabama, which is 14.5 years. Our guide on <a href="/guides/net-metering-vs-net-billing-payback/">net metering vs net billing</a> explains how the rules differ.</p>
<p>Export rules vary by utility. <a href="https://programs.dsireusa.org/">DSIRE</a>, the Database of State Incentives for Renewables &amp; Efficiency, lists current programs. Check it, and your utility&#39;s tariff (its official rate rules), before you count on any credit rate.</p>
<h2 id="how-does-new-york-compare-with-other-states">How does New York compare with other states?</h2>
<p>It does better than most. The New York City example pays back faster than most states in our data.</p>
<p>Massachusetts (Boston) shows 8.5 years at full credit. Connecticut (Hartford) shows 10.8 years. Texas (Houston) shows 14.3 years and Florida (Orlando) shows 14.1 years, even though both get more sun than New York City.</p>
<p>The pattern in our model is simple: the price you avoid paying matters more than extra sun. A sunnier roof helps; a high rate helps more. The <a href="/guides/best-states-for-solar-payback/">best states for solar payback</a> guide has the full ranking.</p>
<h2 id="does-this-apply-if-i-live-upstate-or-on-long-island">Does this apply if I live upstate or on Long Island?</h2>
<p>Only as a rough guide. These figures come from a single reference city.</p>
<p>Upstate New York, Long Island and the Hudson Valley have different utilities, different rates and different sun. Use the New York City numbers to understand the shape of the answer, not to predict your address.</p>
<p>City homes have their own wrinkle. A dense city roof may have less usable space than the example assumes. Shade, roof direction and local rules also change the result.</p>
<h2 id="can-i-still-get-the-federal-solar-tax-credit">Can I still get the federal solar tax credit?</h2>
<p>No, not for new systems. The 30% federal credit, known as Section 25D, ended for systems placed in service after 2025, according to the IRS. Every figure on this page already leaves it out.</p>
<p>State or utility programs may still exist, but they open and close often. Look them up on DSIRE and with the New York State energy office, and confirm any amount with the program itself. <a href="/guides/is-solar-worth-it-without-tax-credit/">Solar without the tax credit</a> explains how to check what is left.</p>
<h2 id="will-rising-electricity-prices-make-solar-pay-off-faster">Will rising electricity prices make solar pay off faster?</h2>
<p>They can, but nobody can promise they will keep rising.</p>
<p>New York&#39;s price rose 7.5% in a year. If prices keep climbing, solar savings grow with them. If prices fall, savings shrink. Our example assumes prices rise 2.5% a year. That is a modeling choice, not a forecast.</p>
<p>To see how sensitive the result is, read <a href="/guides/solar-payback-if-electricity-prices-rise/">solar payback if electricity prices rise</a>.</p>
<h2 id="how-do-i-check-the-numbers-for-my-own-home">How do I check the numbers for my own home?</h2>
<p>Work through four steps:</p>
<ol>
<li><strong>Find your real rate.</strong> Your bill shows the price per kWh and any fixed fees.</li>
<li><strong>Ask installers for an itemized quote.</strong> Compare the cost per watt across at least two offers; the <a href="/guides/solar-quote-checklist/">solar quote checklist</a> lists what each one should show.</li>
<li><strong>Ask your utility how it credits exports.</strong> This one answer can move payback by years.</li>
<li><strong>Run the numbers</strong> in the <a href="/solar-calculator/">solar calculator</a>, or read the <a href="/solar-panels/new-york/">New York solar page</a> for local context.</li>
</ol>
<h2 id="if-you-remember-one-thing">If you remember one thing</h2>
<p>In New York, the high price of electricity does most of the work. Even with modest sun, the New York City example pays back in about 9 years at full credit and 14.3 years at a quarter credit. Price puts New York near the top of the list; the export rule decides whether you land closer to 9 years or to 14.</p>
