22:Te20,<div class="short-answer"><h2 id="quick-verdict">Quick verdict</h2>
<p>South-facing panels make the most power. East- or west-facing panels still make most of it. In a state-by-state model, panels facing east or west at a 20° pitch produced about 14.5% to 14.8% less electricity per year than south-facing panels at 20°, on average across states.</p>
<p>That is a real loss, but it does not rule solar out. Lower output stretches the payback period. Whether the system still pays off comes down to your electricity price, your utility&#39;s rules for extra power, and the price you are quoted.</p>
<p>The U.S. Department of Energy (DOE) puts it this way: &quot;Typically, solar panels perform best on south-facing roofs with a slope between 15 and 40 degrees, though other roofs may be suitable too.&quot;</p>
</div><h2 id="side-by-side">Side by side</h2>
<p>A few terms first. <strong>Orientation</strong>, also called azimuth, is the compass direction the panels face. <strong>Pitch</strong>, or tilt, is the roof angle in degrees from flat. Every <a href="/guides/what-is-a-kilowatt-hour/">kilowatt-hour</a> the panels make is one you do not buy from the grid.</p>

Shade and local weather on your own roof can move the result either way.</p>
<h2 id="when-a-south-facing-roof-wins">When a south-facing roof wins</h2>
<p>South is the benchmark for a reason. DOE explains: &quot;To obtain the highest annual energy output, modules in the northern hemisphere are pointed due south and inclined at an angle equal to the local latitude.&quot;</p>
<p>South pulls furthest ahead in these cases:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Northern states.</strong> Alaska and Montana showed the widest gaps. An east roof in Alaska lost 20.7% at 20° and 23.3% at 30°. A west roof in Montana lost 18.7% at 20° and 21.4% at 30°.</li>
<li><strong>Steeper roofs.</strong> A steeper pitch helped south panels slightly on average (+2.4% at 30°). Alaska gained the most from it (+4.7%).</li>
<li><strong>Thin margins.</strong> If your electricity is cheap, each lost kWh hurts a payback that was already long. Getting the most output per panel counts for more.</li>
</ul>
<p>Hawaii went the other way: a steeper south roof did worse than the baseline there, at -1.7%, the largest drop of any state.</p>

33:T113a,<h2 id="when-an-east-or-west-roof-wins">When an east or west roof wins</h2>
<p>An east or west roof is never the higher producer in this model. It can still be the right choice when it is the roof you have.</p>
<p>It holds up best when:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>You live where the gap is small.</strong> Hawaii had the smallest losses of any state: -7.2% for east, -7.6% for west and -7.4% for an east-west split.</li>
<li><strong>Your power is expensive.</strong> A higher rate makes each kWh worth more, so a smaller harvest can still add up. Check your state on our <a href="/electricity-prices/">electricity prices page</a>. EIA publishes average residential prices by state each month, and the most recent months are preliminary.</li>
<li><strong>Your utility credits exports well.</strong> Net metering is a billing arrangement that credits you for solar power you send back to the grid. DOE notes: &quot;Whether or not your solar system qualifies for net metering payments depends on policies and practices in your state and electric utility.&quot; Our guide on <a href="/guides/net-metering-vs-net-billing-payback/">net metering vs. net billing</a> explains why the rule matters.</li>
<li><strong>One side is too small.</strong> Splitting panels across both faces lost about the same as using one side alone (-14.7% on average). That can help when one side alone is too small.</li>
</ul>
<h2 id="where-they-tie">Where they tie</h2>
<p>Some roofs sit so close to south that the difference barely matters. Southeast and southwest roofs lost only 4.1% and 4.3% on average. In Florida, a southeast roof lost just 1.8%. For most homes, these count as near-south roofs.</p>
<p>East and west are also close to each other. At 20°, east averaged -14.5% and west -14.8%. The split layout fell in between at -14.7%. Pick whichever side has less shade and more usable space, not the one with the slightly better average.</p>
<p>The bigger split is pitch. At 30°, east dropped to -17.2% and west to -17.7%. A steep east or west roof gives up more than a gentle one.</p>
<h2 id="what-the-roof-does-not-decide">What the roof does not decide</h2>
<p>Direction is only one input to savings. DOE lists several: &quot;The amount of money you can save with solar depends upon how much electricity you consume, the size of your solar energy system, if you choose to buy or lease your system, and how much power it is able to generate given the direction your roof faces and how much sunlight hits it.&quot;</p>
<p>Price is the first thing that decides payback, and the export rule is the second. On an east or west roof, both weigh more, because there is less output to spread the cost over. State and utility programs vary, and the DSIRE database lists current ones; <a href="/guides/solar-panel-cost-by-state/">solar panel cost by state</a> shows how much the installed price moves the result.</p>
<p>Our state pages show payback for an example home described on the <a href="/methodology/">methodology page</a>. Those pages model a south-facing roof at 20°, so an east or west roof would produce less than shown there.</p>
<h2 id="which-one-fits-you">Which one fits you</h2>
<p>If you can choose, south or a near-south direction produces the most. If you cannot, judge the roof you have against your rate and your export credit, not against a south-facing ideal.</p>
<p><strong>Lean toward going ahead on an east or west roof if:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>Your electricity price is high.</li>
<li>Your utility gives fair credit for exported power.</li>
<li>The roof has little shade. DOE notes that if nearby trees create excessive shade, rooftop panels may not be the most ideal option.</li>
<li>Your pitch is gentle rather than steep.</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Be more cautious if:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>Power is cheap where you live.</li>
<li>You are in a state like Alaska or Montana, where the gap to south was widest.</li>
<li>Export credits are low or unclear.</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Either way:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>Ask for a production estimate for your actual roof, with its real direction, pitch and shade.</li>
<li>Compare quotes on expected yearly kWh, not just total price.</li>
<li>Size the system to your use with our guide on <a href="/guides/how-many-solar-panels-do-i-need/">how many solar panels you need</a>.</li>
<li>Run your own numbers in the <a href="/solar-calculator/">solar calculator</a>.</li>
</ul>
