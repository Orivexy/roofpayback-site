East- or West-Facing Roof: Is Solar Still Worth It?

East- or west-facing panels make about 14.5% to 14.8% less than south-facing ones. See the payback cost in five states and why weak export credit softens it.

Published October 12, 2026·Updated November 28, 2026·5 min read·RoofPayback editorial·Figures checked against EIA and PVWatts data

Quick verdict

Usually yes, if power is reasonably expensive where you live. Panels facing due east or due west at a 20° pitch made about 14.5% to 14.8% less electricity per year than south-facing panels at 20°, averaged across our 51 reference cities. In the five states we priced, that stretched payback by roughly 1.5 to 2.6 years.

The loss is smaller in dollars than it looks in kWh when your utility pays little for exported power. Most of the output an east or west roof gives up is midday surplus that would have gone to the grid, so a weak export credit makes the orientation gap cheaper.

The U.S. Department of Energy puts the starting point plainly: "Typically, solar panels perform best on south-facing roofs with a slope between 15 and 40 degrees, though other roofs may be suitable too."

Price sets the pace here as it does for south roofs: an east roof in Maine still pays back faster than a south roof in Colorado. The extra years in each row are what the orientation costs.

Louisiana is the case where the full-credit columns do not apply to new systems. Its Public Service Commission credits exports from systems installed after 2019 at avoided cost, a utility-specific rate far below retail, so the quarter-credit columns are the closer guide there.

Why a weak export credit narrows the gap

The example home uses about 40% of its power while the sun is up. On most days the panels make more than that, and the extra goes to the grid. When an east or west roof makes less, the kWh it loses come mostly out of that surplus, not out of the power the house uses directly.

So the dollar cost of a worse roof depends on what a surplus kWh is worth. In Minnesota, turning the array from south to east cuts first-year savings by $271 (17%) if exports earn full retail credit, by $153 (13%) at half credit and by $94 (10%) at quarter credit. The east roof still pays back later in every case, but the penalty shrinks as exports lose value.

That is also why an east or west roof is a weaker case for a big system. Where exports are worth little, size it close to your daytime use; solar and daytime electricity use shows how much that use matters.

How many more panels you need

To make the same 10,500 kWh a year, an east- or west-facing array needs more kW:

  • Minnesota: 7.9 kW facing south, 9.5 kW facing east or west.
  • Maine and New Jersey: 8 kW south, 9.5 to 9.6 kW east or west.
  • Colorado: 6.6 kW south, 7.7 kW east, 8 kW west.
  • Louisiana: 7.1 kW south, 8 to 8.1 kW east or west.

Whether that fits is a roof-space question. Splitting panels across both faces lost about the same as using one face alone (-14.7% on average), so a split layout is a fair option when neither side is big enough by itself. How many solar panels do I need walks through the count.

Where the gap is largest and smallest

The far north loses most. An east roof in Alaska made 20.7% less than south at 20° and 23.3% less at 30°; a west roof in Montana lost 18.7% and 21.4%. Honolulu loses least, about 7.2% to 7.6%, because the sun is high overhead for much of the year. DOE's design guidance explains the benchmark: "To obtain the highest annual energy output, modules in the northern hemisphere are pointed due south and inclined at an angle equal to the local latitude."

East and west average out almost the same, -14.5% and -14.8%, so pick the face with less shade and more usable space. Pitch matters more than the choice between them: at 30°, east and west lose 17.2% and 17.7%.

Judging the roof you have

Lean toward going ahead on an east or west roof if:

  • Your electricity price is high (check your state on the electricity prices page).
  • The roof has little shade and a gentle pitch.
  • Your system is sized near your daytime use rather than for a large surplus.

Be more cautious if:

  • Power is cheap and the south-facing payback in your state is already long.
  • You live in the northern states where the loss is widest.
  • You cannot find out how exports are credited. DOE notes: "Whether or not your solar system qualifies for net metering payments depends on policies and practices in your state and electric utility."

Either way, ask each installer for a production estimate for your actual direction, pitch and shade, compare quotes on expected yearly kWh rather than total price, and run the result through the solar calculator. Our state pages model a south-facing roof, so an east or west roof will make less than they show.
