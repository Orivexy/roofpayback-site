In Washington, each kW of south-facing panels produces about 1,411 kWh a year, #22 of 51 states for sunshine. Homes in District of Columbia paid 25.82¢/kWh on average in September 2026 (#10 highest in the U.S.; national average 18.17¢), up 9.2% from a year earlier. Above-average power prices make every solar kWh worth more here. District of Columbia electricity rates in detail.

What stands out in District of Columbia

  • District of Columbia has both above-median sunshine (1,411 kWh per kW a year) and above-median power prices (25.82¢/kWh), the combination that makes rooftop solar pay fastest; the example system pays back in about 9.2 years.
  • Among the 9 states of the South Atlantic division, District of Columbia has the highest residential price; Florida is the cheapest at 14.74¢.
  • Seasonal swing is typical: July is the best month (146 kWh per kW) and December the weakest (81), a summer-to-winter ratio of 1.6.
  • East- or west-facing panels make about 15% less than south-facing ones, close to the national typical loss.
  • Export credit rules move the result by 6.0 years: 9.2 years with full net metering vs. 15.2 if exports earned a quarter of retail.
  • Residential electricity prices rose 9.2% in a year (5th largest increase of 51), and households pay 1.1× the commercial rate (23.09¢).

How much do solar panels cost in District of Columbia?

Covering a year of use for a District of Columbia home that uses 10,500 kWh takes a 7.5 kW system. At Berkeley Lab's national medians for home systems installed in 2025, $3.00 per watt for cash purchases and $4.50 for loan-financed ones, that is roughly $22,500–$33,800 before incentives ($27,000 at the $3.60 median for all host-owned systems). What to expect from quotes, and why loans cost more, is in our solar cost guide.

Incentives: the federal residential credit ended for systems placed in service after 2025, so only state, local and utility programs remain. DSIRE lists the ones open in District of Columbia.

Source: Berkeley Lab, U.S. Distributed Solar and Storage: 2026 Data Update. Local prices vary with installer, roof and equipment.

Solar in District of Columbia: common questions

Are solar panels worth it in District of Columbia?

For an example 7 kW system costing $24,500 on a home using 10,500 kWh a year, payback in District of Columbia is 9.2 years with full net metering and 15.2 years if exports earn a quarter of the retail rate. Panels typically last decades, so a shorter payback leaves more years of savings.

How much do solar panels cost in District of Columbia?

Berkeley Lab's national medians for home systems installed in 2025 were $3.00 per watt for cash purchases, $3.60 for all host-owned systems and $4.50 for loan-financed ones, before incentives. A system sized to cover the 7,710 kWh the average District of Columbia household used in the past year, about 5.5 kW here, would cost roughly $16,500 to $24,800 at those prices. The federal tax credit does not apply to systems installed after 2025; state and utility programs may still lower the cost.

How long do solar panels take to pay for themselves in District of Columbia?

9.2 years in our example with full net metering, 12.5 years at half-retail export credits. Your quote, roof and utility rules can move this a lot — run your own numbers in the calculator.

How much electricity does a solar panel produce in District of Columbia?

About 1,411 kWh per year for each kW of south-facing panels at a 20° pitch in Washington (NLR PVWatts), from 81 kWh in Dec to 146 kWh in Jul.

What size solar system do I need in District of Columbia?

To cover a full year of use, divide your annual kWh by about 1,411 kWh per kW. A home using 10,500 kWh a year would need roughly 7.5 kW of south-facing panels in District of Columbia. The average District of Columbia residential customer used 7,710 kWh from October 2025 to September 2026 (EIA-861M), which works out to about 5.5 kW. If exports earn less than retail, a smaller system often pays back faster.

Do solar panels work in winter in District of Columbia?

Yes, but output drops: each kW makes about 81 kWh in Dec versus 146 kWh in Jul, or 55% of the best month (NLR PVWatts, Washington). With net metering, summer credits can offset part of winter bills.
