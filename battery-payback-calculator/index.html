Home battery payback calculator

A home battery saves money only when it moves electricity from a cheap moment to an expensive one: solar power you would export for a small credit, or off-peak grid power, used later instead of power bought at a higher price. Enter your plan's prices and a quote, and see whether that gap pays for the battery before it wears out.

How the estimate works

Every day the battery cycles, it delivers the smallest of three amounts: its usable capacity (which shrinks a little each year), the solar surplus available to charge it after round-trip losses, and what your home uses during the peak window.

Each delivered kWh is worth the price of the grid power it replaces minus what it cost to store it. Storing it costs the export credit you gave up (charging from solar) or the off-peak price (charging from the grid), divided by the round-trip efficiency, because about 15% of the energy is lost going in and out. In formula form:

yearly savings = kWh delivered × (price replaced − charging price ÷ efficiency) − maintenance

Payback is the first year in which cumulative savings cover the cost after incentives, plus a replacement if the analysis period outlasts the battery. If the price gap is smaller than the round-trip losses, every cycle loses money and there is no payback.

When a battery is most likely to pay

  • Low export credits. Where exported solar earns far less than retail power, storing it for the evening is worth the difference. California's net billing tariff is the main example (see the California solar page).
  • A wide time-of-use spread. If peak power costs much more than off-peak power, charging off-peak and discharging at peak can pay, after losses.
  • Not under full net metering. When every exported kWh is credited at the retail rate, the grid already acts as a free battery and storage adds little but backup.

Backup power during outages is a real benefit that this calculator does not price, because its value depends on how often your power fails and what an outage costs you.

Federal tax credit

The federal Residential Clean Energy Credit (which covered batteries of 3 kWh or more) is not allowed for expenditures made after December 31, 2025; an installation counts when it is completed. Batteries installed in 2026 get no federal credit when you buy them. Leased or third-party-owned systems follow different rules that benefit the owner, not necessarily you. Source: IRS FS-2025-05: FAQs on the modification of sections 25C, 25D… (Aug 21, 2025). State and utility rebates may still apply; check your state energy office or the DSIRE database, and enter only amounts you are sure to receive.
